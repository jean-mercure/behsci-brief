# The concept of the day, FORGOOD and the policy that has to count its losers

This belongs to Foundations of Behavioural Science, and the working paper at item five is the occasion for it.

Behavioural public policy is normally taught through interventions whose costs are nearly invisible. Change the default on a pension, reorder the items in a canteen, send a letter worded differently. Nobody is obviously worse off, so the evaluation reduces to whether the intended behaviour increased, and the ethical question collapses into the empirical one.

FORGOOD, the framework from Leonhard Lades and Liam Delaney, earns its keep precisely because it refuses that collapse. It asks you to state seven things separately rather than folding them into a single welfare number: Fairness, Openness, Respect, Goals, Opinions, Options and Dignity. The value of the list is not that it resolves anything. It is that it stops you from resolving things silently.

Now take the case at item five, which is an admissions quota reserving half the seats at Brazil's federal institutions. This is choice architecture of the most consequential kind, and notice how different it is from a nudge. It does not try to change what any applicant wants, or how they weigh their options, or what they understand. It changes what the allocation mechanism gives them. The architecture here is the rule itself.

Two sets of arithmetic come out of the paper, and they point different ways. At the margin, the students who benefit gain about twice what the displaced students lose, because the displaced have better outside options to fall back on. That looks like a clear improvement. In equilibrium, once everyone's applications adjust, about thirty per cent more students are displaced than are benefited, and the losses falling on students the policy was not aimed at offset about two-thirds of the gains to those it was.

Read through FORGOOD, that is not one finding but several. Goals: the targeted gains are real and the policy achieved what it set out to do. Fairness: there are identifiable losers, and they are numerous. Options: the rule removes options from one group to supply them to another, which is what makes it a redistribution rather than an improvement in information. And here is the part that matters most. To say that a gain which is modest in aggregate but large for the intended beneficiaries is nonetheless worth having is a distributional judgement. It is not an empirical finding, and the numbers do not contain it. FORGOOD's contribution is to stop that judgement being smuggled in as though it were.

Two habits follow. First, every allocation rule is choice architecture, so nudges are a subset of this course's subject matter rather than the whole of it. Second, whenever an intervention has identifiable losers, ask whether the evaluation counted them, and where in the distribution it stopped looking.

# One. Two oscillatory signatures of task switching, one specific and one general

In Communications Psychology yesterday, Mareike Hülsemann, Christoph Löffler and Anna-Lena Schubert at Mainz recorded electrical activity from the scalp while a hundred and forty-eight adults worked through three different task-switching paradigms. This item carries today's methods watch, so the findings in brief: they isolated two separate signals, a short burst at the front of the head which was specific to the particular task, and a longer-lasting suppression further back which appeared across all three and so looks domain-general. Neither of the general signals was related to intelligence or to working memory capacity. The study was not preregistered, which the authors state, and some of its comparisons had very little power.

# Two. A proposal to treat empathy as something two people build rather than one person has

In Nature Reviews Psychology the day before yesterday, Shir Genzer and Anat Perry argue for a change in the unit of analysis. Almost all empathy research takes the empathiser as the subject: how accurately did this person read that person, how much distress did they feel, what in their brain was active while they did it. The person being empathised with is treated as a stimulus.

Genzer and Perry propose instead that empathy is constructed by a pair. The person in distress decides what to disclose and how, reads the response, and adjusts. Both parties bring cognitive, emotional and motivational components, and the review works through each for both sides. The research agenda follows: vary the relationship, vary the emotional tone, and follow exchanges over time rather than photographing them once.

It is a review, so no new evidence. As a reframing it makes a point worth noticing early in a degree: a field's unit of analysis is a choice, not a fact, and it determines what can be found.

# Three. A serial mediation model, and why the arrows are assumptions rather than findings

In Scientific Reports yesterday, in an early-release version, Zafer Güney Çağış at İnönü University reports that among four hundred and ninety-nine Turkish adults of average age twenty-seven, positive childhood experiences were associated with flourishing in adulthood, with self-compassion and gratitude acting as mediators, and a smaller path running through both in sequence.

This is included as a teaching case rather than as a finding, and it is worth being clear about why.

Everything was measured at one sitting. Childhood experience was measured by asking adults to remember their childhoods. The outcome, the two mediators and the predictor all came from the same person answering the same questionnaire on the same day, which means any tendency to answer positively across the board will generate exactly this pattern.

And then the structure. A serial mediation model says that A causes B, which causes C, which causes D. The arrows are not estimated from the data; they are imposed by the analyst before the estimation begins. Cross-sectional data are symmetric, and will fit a model with the arrows reversed about as well. So the diagram looks like a finding and is in fact an assumption, drawn as a picture.

The sample is a single national convenience sample, and the early-release version carries no limitations section at all.

# Four. Humans follow the tune, zebra finches take either route

In Communications Biology earlier this week, Zhi-Yuan Ning and colleagues, with Carel ten Cate and Henkjan Honing at Leiden and Amsterdam, compared two hundred and seventy-two people with twenty-three zebra finches on the same discrimination problem.

Both species learned to sort sounds into two categories by moving left or right. The sounds were built so that two properties varied independently: pitch, roughly how high the note is, and the pattern of resonances that in speech distinguishes one vowel from another. Then came probes in which one property or the other was removed or altered.

The humans were pitch-dominant. Take the pitch away and their performance fell apart; take the resonance pattern away and they coped. The finches combined the two properties additively, and could work from either one when the other was missing. The only clear difference between the species came on the probes with the resonance pattern stripped out.

That is a nice result because it undercuts an easy assumption, namely that the way we hear a melody is the way a hearing system hears a melody. Birds that learn songs by ear do not weight the same dimension we do.

Be careful with it, and the authors are. The human data were collected online from unpaid volunteers of mixed musical background, the procedures could not be made identical across species, each novel probe was presented only five times, and several of the comparisons sat at trend level rather than reaching significance.

# Five. A quota whose winners and losers are both counted

A working paper from Nano Barahona, Cauê Dobbin and Sebastián Otero examines Brazil's reservation of half the seats at federal higher-education institutions, using admissions cutoffs to get at effects on students at the margin and a structural model to get at what happens once applications adjust across the whole system. This item carries the concept of the day, so one line: the marginal arithmetic is favourable and the equilibrium arithmetic is not, with around thirty per cent more students displaced than benefited. The equilibrium figures inherit the assumptions of the model that produced them, and the landing page gives no data sources or sample sizes, so it should be read as a working paper and not as a settled result.

# The methods watch, why a robust effect makes a poor measure

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the paper at item one is the case, with the commentary at item six as the live dispute.

Task switching is one of the most reliable paradigms in cognitive psychology. Ask someone to alternate between two simple judgements and they will be slower on the trials where the task changes, essentially every time, in every laboratory. The switch cost replicates almost without fail.

Now here is the trap, and it is one of the most useful things to understand early. Robustness of an average effect and usefulness for measuring individual differences are close to opposites.

Think about what an experimenter does when designing a task to produce a reliable effect. They remove everything that varies between people and is not the manipulation: standardise the instructions, train everyone to the same criterion, use trials so simple nobody misunderstands them, average over hundreds of repetitions. The design maximises the contrast within each person and minimises the variance between them. But variance between people is exactly what you need to use the task as a measure of a trait. A perfect experiment is a terrible instrument. This is the reliability paradox, set out by Craig Hedge, Georgina Powell and Petroc Sumner, and once you have seen it you will see it everywhere.

Which is why the right response is the one the authors of today's paper took: report the reliability. They give internal consistency coefficients and split-half correlations with the standard correction, rather than assuming that a well-behaved task must be measuring something stable about the person. If a measure correlates poorly with itself, it cannot correlate meaningfully with anything else, and the ceiling on any correlation you report is set by that number.

Three features of the paper are worth holding up as a model of candour. It was not preregistered, and the authors say so rather than letting the reader assume otherwise. Some of their model comparisons had power of about one in four, which means those comparisons could not have detected much and the authors note it. And they point out that the sustained signal they found has elsewhere been linked to general goal-directed processing, so the step from the signal to a claim about cognitive flexibility is not secured by their own data.

So the habit, whenever a paper reports that individuals differ in the size of some experimental effect: find the reliability estimate before you read the correlation. If it is not there, the correlation cannot be interpreted.
