No new batch from the National Bureau of Economic Research, which does not post at weekends, nothing new in Nature Human Behaviour since Thursday, and nothing in the psychology section of Retraction Watch for nearly a month, so the methods watch again comes from one of the day's own papers.

# The concept of the day is attribution, and whether we explain behaviour by the person or by the situation.

This belongs to the psychological science course, and it reaches the behavioural science course too, because how you explain poverty predicts whether you support doing anything about it.

The distinction is simple to state. A dispositional explanation locates the cause in the person: they are lazy, they are careless with money. A situational explanation locates it outside: wages are low, there is prejudice in hiring, the available jobs are bad. The well-established finding is that observers lean too far towards the person, a tendency called correspondence bias, applied asymmetrically: we reach for situations when explaining ourselves and for character when explaining others.

In the research on poverty this maps onto two families of lay explanation, individualistic and structuralist, and the usual expectation is that religious belief pushes people towards the individualistic end. That comes from just-world reasoning: if the universe is fundamentally fair, outcomes are deserved, and the poor must have done something to arrive there.

The Korean study in today's brief breaks that expectation in an instructive way. Believing in a warm, responsive, personally supportive God did sit against structural explanation, exactly as the just-world account predicts. But believing in demons, in hell and in the devil sat with structural explanation, and most strongly among political conservatives, where it brought structural attribution up to roughly the level found among liberals. The authors' reading is that a cosmology containing organised malevolent agency already supplies the thought that forces larger than any individual shape what happens to people, and that this transfers from the metaphysical to the economic.

Two lessons follow. The first is about measurement: a single religiosity scale would have averaged a positive and a negative association into nothing, and that null would have been an artefact of the instrument rather than a fact about the world. Whenever you suspect a construct has distinguishable components, the test is whether the halves behave differently, and here they behaved in opposite directions. The second is about design. Attribution is not a fixed trait; it moves with framing, with mood, with who is being explained. So a cross-sectional survey cannot tell you whether the belief shapes the attribution, or whether people who already think structurally find the darker elements of their tradition more salient. The arrow is unresolved, and the authors say so.

# One. Incentivised patience and risk aversion predict which degree young Danes apply for.

Thomas Epper, Ernst Fehr and colleagues, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, did something hard to do. They took a random sample of Danish eighteen and nineteen year olds just before the university application deadline, measured patience with sixteen real intertemporal choices and risk aversion with fifteen investment scenarios, with money actually paid out, then linked those measures to the applications the young people officially filed. About three thousand were analysed, choosing among seventy-six programmes, with earnings benchmarks from the tax records of several hundred thousand older Danes.

Both traits predicted. Ten percentiles more patience went with programmes nearly a year and a half longer, falling to a little over two months once you adjust for grades, parental background and sex. Ten percentiles more risk aversion went with programmes whose graduates were six percentage points less likely to land in the bottom earnings quintile, against an average of about ten per cent, and about three points adjusted. Risk aversion predicted avoiding dispersed earnings roughly twice as well as grades did.

The counterintuitive result is the interesting one: the more risk-averse applicants chose longer programmes, not shorter. A longer degree defers earnings, so it looks like the riskier choice, unless what you are avoiding is the spread of outcomes at the end rather than the delay. Caveats: this is programme-level, applications rather than enrolment, only thirty-nine per cent of those invited took part and they were higher-achieving, and Denmark has free tuition and universal stipends, which limits what it tells you about England.

# Two. A Scottish cohort finds no consistent link between screen time and children's difficulties.

This is the paper behind the methods watch, so briefly. Using the Growing Up in Scotland cohort, from about three thousand eight hundred children at age five down to about two thousand six hundred at fifteen, the Edinburgh team found no association between screen time and parent-rated difficulties at five or at ten once they adjusted for family circumstances. At fifteen, five hours or more of gaming went with roughly two and a half times the odds of elevated difficulties, while an hour or more of social media went with about forty per cent lower odds, the opposite of what the public conversation expects. The authors conclude that guidelines written in hours alone will achieve little.

# Three. Able women are read as hostile by their colleagues, able men are not.

Chawla and colleagues, in the Journal of Applied Psychology, ran two studies in real working groups rather than with vignettes, which is what makes this worth your attention. The first followed four hundred and thirty-two American business undergraduates in semester-long consulting teams, testing cognitive ability early and collecting peer ratings weeks later. The second looked at three hundred and ninety-four employees in small teams at a financial company in China, using the firm's own hiring test.

For women, higher measured ability predicted being rated as more hostile by teammates, and those ratings in turn predicted more victimisation, such as colleagues withholding information, and less help offered. For men there was no such association in the first study, and in the second it ran the other way: higher ability predicted being seen as less hostile, victimised less and helped more.

The explanation offered is prescriptive stereotyping, the backlash a woman meets for violating an expectation of warmth. What is new is that the test uses measured ability in standing teams rather than a description of a fictional colleague. Against it: both populations were pre-screened on cognitive ability, so the range is restricted and the associations are probably understated rather than overstated. Victimisation and help were self-reported, and separating the measurements in time is not random assignment, which you cannot have here because you cannot assign ability.

# Four. Voting at home goes with slightly more consistent vote choice.

Wolak and Stapleton, in Political Behavior, link the twenty twenty American National Election Studies to official state voter records, so how each person voted was verified rather than recalled. They then score what the literature calls correct voting: whether the candidate you chose is the one your own stated issue preferences, partisanship and trait assessments point to. Across about four thousand seven hundred voters, mail voting went with a two and a half percentage point higher probability of a correct vote, around ninety per cent in person against nearly ninety-three by post. Several different matching and balancing approaches put it between about two and five points.

The detail that makes this a choice-architecture finding rather than a curiosity is that in-person early voting was indistinguishable from voting on election day. So the advantage is not in voting early. It is in voting at home, with the ballot in front of you, able to look something up, with nobody waiting behind you. Caveats: this is observational, so selection into postal voting is live, the effect is small, and there is a ceiling because most people already vote correctly. This is recent online-first work rather than something published yesterday.

# Five. Belief in demons tracks structural explanations of poverty, belief in a supportive God tracks the opposite.

The study behind the concept of the day, so briefly. Jung and Chen at Sungkyunkwan and Kim at Ewha Womans surveyed one thousand six hundred and thirty-two South Korean adults in August twenty twenty-four, quota-matched to the national population on age, sex and region, and excluding people who believe in no deity at all. Neither kind of belief predicted individualistic explanations of poverty. Belief in divine support predicted structural explanations negatively, and belief in supernatural evil predicted them positively, which is a double dissociation. No coefficients appear in the accessible report, the design is cross-sectional, and East Asian norms already lean towards situational explanation, which may be suppressing the individualistic associations.

# Six. Partners resemble each other far more in traits than in genotypes.

Gretchen Saunders, writing alone in Behavior Genetics, used the UK Biobank's roughly eighty-two thousand presumed couples to compare two things usually run together. The first is how alike partners are in observable traits. The second is how alike they are in the genetic predispositions to those traits.

The trait similarities are substantial and all in the same direction: about zero point four five for education, zero point four two for weekly drinks, zero point two six for body mass index. The genetic similarities are much smaller: about zero point one six for education, zero point zero eight for height, lower still for smoking and drinking. On average the genetic correlations sit about eighty per cent below the trait correlations. There is a real signal, confirmed by a neat internal check comparing odd and even chromosomes within unrelated people, and it appears to be growing across birth cohorts. But it is a fraction of what the visible resemblance suggests.

Read it carefully, because the inference runs backwards from genetic data to historical mating patterns. It shows nothing about people choosing on genotype, which nobody does. Similarity may equally reflect shared schools, shared neighbourhoods and partners converging on each other's habits over years together. The sample is of European genetic ancestry only, couples are inferred from a shared address, and the cohort comparisons are between different people recruited in different ways, so the apparent trend over time is the weakest part of the paper.

# The methods watch. Three ways to overread a null.

Back to the Scottish screen-time paper, because the useful thing about it is not the result but how easily it can be misread, and nulls are every bit as easy to overread as positive findings.

First, an adjusted null is not an absence of association. The raw associations at five and at ten were there; they dissolved once the team entered income, area deprivation, parental education and health, the child's cognitive scores, household chaos and the warmth and conflict in the parent-child relationship. That is not a finding that screen time is unrelated to difficulties. It is a claim that the relationship is explained by those family circumstances. And adjustment removes only what was measured, which is precisely why the Swedish twin study we discussed on Friday is the stronger design: it differences out the whole family without having to name a single confounder. The two studies agree, incidentally, about the raw association.

Second, multiplicity. The protective-looking forty per cent lower odds for social media survived correction for multiple comparisons only in the one-to-two-hour band, not across the range. And the striking longitudinal result, roughly two and a half times the odds at ten following five or more hours at five, rested on a hundred and nineteen children, did not persist to fifteen, and did not hold under alternative specifications. A result that lives in one specification is a property of that specification.

Third, measurement. The person reporting the exposure changed between waves, caregivers at five and ten and the adolescents themselves at fifteen, while the outcome stayed parent-rated throughout. Only time was recorded, never content, so an hour of homework help and an hour of hostile group chat count the same. And at fifteen the direction is wide open, because an adolescent with an active social life has more to message about.
