# The concept of the day is about the discount rate, and why the task makes the number.

Twenty pounds today or fifty pounds next month. Foundations of Behavioural Science does not treat your answer as a personality trait to be read off. It treats impatience as a parameter, estimated from a pattern of choices, and the policy literature on commitment devices, automatic enrolment and deadlines leans on that parameter behaving well.

The standard model is exponential discounting. Value falls by a constant proportion for each period of delay, so a single number describes the whole of someone's patience, and that number is time-consistent. If today you prefer fifty pounds in thirteen months to twenty in twelve, then in twelve months you will still prefer fifty next month to twenty now. Behaviour fails that test reliably. People are far more impatient about delays beginning now than about the same delay pushed into the future, which hyperbolic discounting captures by letting the implied rate fall as the horizon lengthens. The gap between the two models is present bias, the engine under every intervention that asks you to tie your own hands.

Now the part students skip. A discount rate is not observed. It is fitted. The researcher offers choices between a smaller amount sooner and a larger amount later, and estimates the parameter that best reproduces them. That estimate moves with the design: the amounts, the delays, whether the money is real or hypothetical, whether both options are delayed so that neither is immediate. The same person can be assigned materially different rates by two laboratories using two reasonable tasks.

Which is why today's second item should trouble you. The imaging is described with care: the atlas, the normalisation, the five training splits, the held-out fifth, two figures for variance explained. The behavioural measure that produced the thing being predicted is described in four words. A behavioural delay discounting assessment. Without the amounts, the delays and the estimation method, a model explaining a fifth of the variance is explaining a number of unknown provenance.

So, three questions. What exactly was the choice set, and was the money real. Is the reported quantity a fitted parameter, or a raw count of patient choices. And in which direction does the association run, because a model that cannot say whether more brain tissue means more patience has predicted a number, not a behaviour.

# One. A new scale puts East Asia far above the West, on a construct the old questionnaires may never have measured.

Thomas Talhelm, Liuqing Wei and colleagues, in Nature Human Behaviour, argue that half a century of cross-cultural psychology has measured the wrong thing. Classic collectivism scales ask about generalised warmth towards people at large. The authors propose that collectivism is really about binding duties inside specific close relationships, which they call responsibilism, with weaker obligations to strangers. Their new fourteen-item scale, written in English and Chinese together and then translated into forty-five languages, went to more than sixteen thousand adults, mostly students, of whom about twelve thousand in a hundred cultures were analysed. The East Asia to West gap is over a full standard deviation. One lovely detail. Forty specialists in the field were asked to predict, item by item, which group would agree more with the old questions. Their most popular prediction matched the data on two of fourteen. The scale's comparative claim is today's methods watch.

# Two. Brain volumes predicted about a fifth of the variance in impatience, and the impatience task is never described.

Bryan Youngwoo Yoon, Wonjin Kim and colleagues, in JAMA Psychiatry, scanned a hundred and forty-six students and asked whether brain region volumes could predict delay discounting scores in people the model had not seen. Volumes were taken from an anatomical atlas, normalised to head size, screened inside each of five training sets, then fed to a neural network tested on a held-out fifth, about twenty-nine people at a time. The white matter model explained twenty-two per cent of the variance on average, the grey matter model nineteen. Two right-hemisphere regions were picked by both, the nucleus accumbens and the frontal pole. The senior author, Wi Hoon Jung, calls it a proof of concept and says plainly that brain scans cannot determine how an individual will behave. The concept of the day explains what is missing.

# Three. Watching a losing coach concede made voters likelier to call an unfavourable election fair.

Alex Tolkin, at the University of Pennsylvania, reports in Political Behavior that gracious defeat in sport transfers to political judgement. In a panel of about two and a half thousand Americans, surveyed just before the twenty-twenty presidential election and again the following April, supporters of the losing candidate who often watched team sports recorded a smaller fall in their belief that the election had been fair. The experiment matters more. About a thousand partisans either did or did not watch a basketball summary and a video of the defeated coach's gracious press conference. All of them then read about a fictional mayoral election, picked a candidate, and were told their candidate had narrowly lost. Some then saw a concession speech, edited from real footage with the fictional candidate's name dubbed in by voice cloning. The sports concession raised perceived legitimacy by about half as much as the political one, and the two were additive. This is Bandura's territory, behaviour learned by watching a relatable model, and a rare case of a mechanism carrying across domains. Three honest caveats. The coverage never actually says assignment was random. Not one coefficient or p-value is printed. And the outcome is a stated judgement about a fictional election between candidates the participant had known for minutes.

# Four. Isolation and loneliness track about four months of extra biological age.

Rui Mao, Xin Meng and colleagues at Central South University report in Health Psychology on more than three hundred thousand UK Biobank participants, average age in the mid-fifties. Biological age here is PhenoAge, a clinical formula estimating physiological age from blood markers of inflammation and organ function, and acceleration is the gap between that estimate and your actual age. Isolation was a three-point count: living alone, seeing friends or family less than weekly, no weekly group activity. Loneliness was a two-point count: often feeling lonely, almost never confiding in anyone. After heavy adjustment, the most isolated group came out about four months older biologically than the least isolated, and the lonely about three months older than the rest. Over a median fourteen years, each isolation point went with about fourteen per cent higher all-cause mortality. Hold the headline against the quantity. Biologically older than their years turns out to be four months, in a sample of three hundred thousand. The authors are careful. Biological age was measured once, so nothing here tracks an individual ageing, and they say plainly that this is not proof that loneliness causes ageing. Since smoking, sleep and chronic disease may be pathways rather than confounders, adjusting for them probably understates the association. No confidence interval or p-value appears.

# Five. Men with schizophrenia read anger from moving points of light barely above chance.

Annika Resch, Marina Pavlova and colleagues at Tubingen report in the journal Schizophrenia that difficulty reading emotion extends from faces to bodies. Twenty-eight men with schizophrenia, recruited from inpatient units, and twenty-eight age-matched healthy men, average age thirty-two, judged point-light displays. These are short videos in which a walking figure is made of fifteen moving white dots, and a face of fifty, with clothing, body shape and features stripped away so that only motion remains. That is why they are the standard tool for studying biological motion. On faces the patient group scored about fifty-nine per cent correct against seventy-six. On bodies, fifty-five against sixty-three, where fifty is chance. They were also slower. The authors note it is still unknown whether this is a deficit specific to social cognition or a broader cognitive difficulty. The weaknesses are real and mostly unattributed: a small sample, very few expressions tested, men only, nearly all patients medicated. The groups were matched on age alone. And not one effect size, interval, p-value or named statistical test appears in the coverage.

# Six. Patients in Mumbai rated machine advice favourably and trusted their doctor anyway.

This one is an abstract-only reading of an early-shared accepted version, so exclusions, effect sizes and the authors' own limitations are unavailable. Purva Pandey, Giuseppe Veltri and Ivo Vlaev, at the National University of Singapore, report in Scientific Reports on a factorial experiment with about eight hundred participants in Mumbai, each rating four vignettes that varied the source of medical advice, whether the source was disclosed, and the clinical risk. Doctor advice was rated more trustworthy and more accurate, but both cleared a high bar: eighty-seven per cent said they would follow the machine, ninety-seven the doctor. The interesting part is an equivalence test rather than a significance test. On whether the advice recognised the patient as an individual, the two sources were equivalent within bounds the authors set in advance, which is a claim of no meaningful difference rather than a failure to find one. One worry. The abstract never says whether the wording of the advice was identical across the two labels, and without that this is a messenger-effect experiment only in intention.

# The methods watch, from the statistics and methods course, asks what it takes to compare a measurement across cultures.

Today's first item makes a comparative claim. East Asian respondents score more than a standard deviation above Western respondents on a new scale. Comparing scores presupposes that the instrument measures the same thing, on the same scale, in both places. That is measurement invariance, and it is tested, not assumed.

It comes in three nested steps. Configural invariance asks whether the same items hang together on the same factor in each group. Metric invariance adds equal loadings, so one point on the scale means the same amount everywhere. Scalar invariance adds equal intercepts, so a score of four means the same in Seoul as in Stockholm. Only scalar invariance licenses comparing group averages. Fail it, and a difference in collectivism may be a difference in how the question is read.

Forty-five languages raises the stakes, and translation is the lesser half of it. These are scenario items, and scenarios carry cultural furniture. A vignette about a friend who criticises your midterm grade presupposes midterms, a grading convention, and a script for what friends may say. Two groups can hold the identical disposition and answer differently because one scenario is more recognisable.

What the authors do instead is worth studying. A Hong Kong sample taking the English version still showed a large gap with the United States, which addresses language. And they rest their case on predictive validity, correlating scores with census household size, clan traditions, family blood donation and the rate at which lost wallets are returned. That is a different and stronger argument, because prediction does not need the scale to mean the same thing everywhere, only to forecast the same thing.

But notice the level at which it works. Those correlations run between country averages and country-level outcomes, so they are ecological. The people who returned the wallets were not the participants, and a scale can predict national averages well and individuals badly. Three habits, then. Ask whether a cross-group comparison reports invariance. Ask whether the validity evidence sits at the level of the person or the country. And ask whether the claim needs averages, or whether a ranking would do, because rankings survive weaker assumptions.
