# The concept of the day is descriptive norms, and the difference between changing one and correcting one.

This is Foundations of Behavioural Science, worked through the first of today's items.

Norms are one of the levers you are taught early. They are the N in MINDSPACE. They sit behind the hotel towel card, and behind the tax letters that tell you most people pay on time. The distinction to hold firmly is between an injunctive norm, which is what people believe ought to be done, and a descriptive norm, which is what people believe is actually done. Almost every norm intervention in choice architecture works on the descriptive one, and almost all of them work not by creating a norm but by correcting a mistaken belief about one.

Today's first paper makes three things about that precise.

First, the mistake is measurable, and it has a direction. People put others about seven points above where others actually sat, on a nought to a hundred scale. And their estimate of the group tracked their own view at around nought point seven. That correlation is what projection looks like. So the perceived norm is partly an output of personal belief rather than an independent input to it, which is why the authors are careful about causal order.

Second, the channel matters more than the content. A sentence telling participants that especially many, or only a few, previous participants had found the claims plausible moved nothing at all. A bar chart of what people had actually said moved the estimate of the group by nearly twenty points. The norm had to be made countable, not merely asserted. So "tell people what others do" is underspecified advice. The format carries the effect.

Third, and this is the part worth keeping past the exam, a norm correction only works in the direction the truth happens to point. The authors say so themselves. Where a belief really is widely held, telling people the true figure licenses it. The classic failure is the Schultz energy study, where households told they used less than their neighbours went on to use more.

So a descriptive norm intervention is not a technique you can deploy at will. It is a bet that the facts are on your side. Put two questions to any proposal to use one. Is the norm being corrected, or constructed? And what happens if the real number turns out to be on the wrong side?

# One. People who believe conspiracy claims overestimate how many others agree.

That was the concept, so the study itself can be brief. Jan-Willem van Prooijen and colleagues ran six preregistered studies with about two thousand nine hundred participants in Britain and the United States, published in the European Journal of Social Psychology on Friday. Two were correlational, measuring personal plausibility ratings alongside estimates of what others had said. Two tested whether reading a conspiracy blog post about pharmaceutical companies shifted both. One tested a verbal norm statement, which failed. One tested a chart of the real distribution, which worked, cutting personal endorsement by about a third.

The authors' own caveats are extensive. The others whose views were estimated were fellow online participants, not the public. The mediation analyses cannot establish causal order. And the one intervention that worked was tested once, with no measure of whether it lasts.

# Two. Britain's postwar sugar ration, and dementia sixty years later.

This one carries the methods watch, so again, briefly. Britain rationed sugar until September nineteen fifty-three, and children under two got no ration at all. Xingji Lian and colleagues, writing in npj Aging on Friday, used that end date as an assignment rule inside UK Biobank. About thirty-eight thousand people conceived while rationing was in force were compared with about twenty-two thousand born after it ended.

The exposed group showed roughly a quarter lower dementia risk, and close to half the risk of Alzheimer's disease. Depression and anxiety were down by about a tenth and a fifth. Among nine thousand with brain scans, estimated brain age was about five months younger. The authors say the result cannot inform any recommendation until it is replicated in cohorts where diet was actually measured, and that the brain imaging contrast is preliminary.

# Three. Extreme metal fans find gore less disgusting, and funnier.

Albert Wabnegger and Anne Schienle at the University of Graz ran a preregistered online experiment, reported in Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts yesterday. Seventy-nine extreme metal fans and two hundred and eight non-fans, matched on age and gender down to seventy-four in each group, average age about thirty. Each saw eight images: two disgusting artworks, two extreme metal album covers with the band names removed, two unpleasant everyday photographs, and two scrambled controls.

Fans rated the artworks about thirteen points less disgusting on a hundred-point scale, the album covers ten points, the photographs nine. The control images were near identical across groups. Fans also liked the artworks and covers substantially more, rated them funnier, and showed less of what the authors call negative confusion.

Two cautions. Fans rated the album covers about seventeen points more familiar, so for exactly the stimuli where the effect is largest, familiarity and reduced disgust cannot be separated. And the matching was on age and gender only, not on general disgust sensitivity, which differed between the groups and may be a route into the genre rather than a consequence of it. Nothing on the press page is attributed to the authors as a limitation, so treat every caveat in that coverage as the reporter's.

# Four. Stress comes first, and the mental planning follows.

Elouise Botes, Julia Reiter and colleagues at Luxembourg tracked two hundred and ninety-three British parents through Christmas, with three randomly timed prompts a day for thirty-five days from the thirtieth of November. Fourteen and a half thousand observations. Communications Psychology, yesterday.

They modelled three things: cognitive labour, meaning the mental work of planning and remembering; emotion suppression; and stress. The design separates within-person from between-person effects. Within a person, stress predicted later cognitive labour about six times more strongly than cognitive labour predicted later stress. Stress also predicted later emotion suppression, while suppression did not predict stress. Between people, mothers reported more cognitive labour than fathers, with no gender difference in stress or suppression. Social support measured in November predicted lower emotion suppression in January, but did nothing for stress.

The authors are unusually candid. The data are not dyadic: the mothers and fathers in the sample were not partners, so the division of labour inside a couple cannot be examined. Cognitive labour was measured with a single item whose within-person reliability was about nought point six, which they say probably understates it. And the relationships reported here were exploratory, even though the wider project was preregistered.

# Five. A fortnight of meaning, measured across a papal election.

Stefan Stieger and colleagues ran an experience sampling study through the conclave that elected Pope Leo the Fourteenth. A hundred and seventeen practising American Catholics completed an end-of-day survey for fourteen consecutive days from the third of May last year: four days before the conclave, the conclave itself, and ten days after the election. The Journal of Positive Psychology.

Presence of meaning in life rose by about a fifth of a point on a seven-point scale, from a baseline of around five point two, and stayed up for most of the following ten days. Search for meaning drifted down. The pattern survived adjustment for age, gender, religious commitment, and feelings about the pope's nationality.

The design problems are substantial, and they come from the coverage rather than from the authors. There is no comparison group of any kind. There is no baseline from before the vacancy, so the rise could be a recovery from a dip after the death of Pope Francis. And the nationality was revealed with the election, so the patriotic and the religious response cannot be told apart. The authors themselves say only that the result fits three different theoretical accounts equally well, and that the effects are small to medium. The fieldwork is from May of last year; it is the coverage that is new.

# Six. Education predicts ambivalence about big technology firms, not approval.

Tobias Tober, Marius Busemeyer and Sebastian Koos at Konstanz analysed a survey of nearly twenty thousand adults across Germany, Japan, Poland, Spain, Sweden and the United States, fielded in the summer of twenty twenty-two. Research and Politics, Friday.

Respondents rated six statements about large technology companies, three favourable and three critical. A factor analysis found two dimensions that barely correlated with each other, and that is the methodologically useful part: because the two scales are near independent, a respondent can score high on both, so agreeing with the praise and the criticism at once is a genuine position rather than a fence-sitting midpoint. A clustering analysis then produced five groups, the largest neutral, the second largest ambivalent.

From the least to the most educated respondents, the probability of landing in the ambivalent group rose from about a fifth to about a third, while the neutral share fell correspondingly. The probability of being a supporter, or a sceptic, barely moved. Ideology cut differently: scepticism rose on the left, while ambivalence rose on the right, and most of all among the highly educated right. The authors had expected education to predict approval, and say plainly that their explanation for what they found instead is incomplete.

# And the methods watch, from the statistics and methods course: a natural experiment whose instrument is a calendar date.

Start with what is good about the sugar study, because it is a real design and not a fishing expedition. Nobody chose their exposure. The ration was national policy with a hard end date, which removes the self-selection that ruins almost all nutritional epidemiology. And the authors state plainly that nothing here can inform a recommendation until it is replicated where diet was measured. That sentence is the mark of a careful paper.

Three cautions follow, and you should be able to produce them without prompting.

First, the exposure was never measured. Sugar intake is inferred entirely from date of birth. So the quantity the paper estimates is the effect of having been conceived before July nineteen fifty-four. Whether that is the same thing as the effect of sugar is an assumption, and it should be stated as one.

Second, the date assigns more than sugar. Rationing of the other foods also ended by the summer of fifty-four. The same months carry postwar economic recovery, improving maternal healthcare, and a shift in how infants were fed. An instrument built from a calendar boundary is only as clean as that boundary is exclusive, and this one is crowded. The general test for any natural experiment: write down everything the assigning variable also assigns, and then ask which of those you would have expected to matter.

Third, look at who is in the sample, and at how many events there are. UK Biobank recruited volunteers in middle age. Everybody in both groups survived to recruitment and chose to take part, and the earlier-born group had to survive about two and a half years longer to qualify. Then the counts. Across sixty thousand people there were three hundred and seven cases of dementia, and a hundred and twenty-three of Alzheimer's disease. A forty-six per cent relative reduction resting on a hundred and twenty-three events is a statement about a few dozen people, and a relative risk on a rare outcome moves a long way when a handful of cases move.

So three habits. Ask what the exposure variable actually is, rather than what it is called. List everything the assigning date also assigns. And convert every relative risk into the number of events underneath it.
