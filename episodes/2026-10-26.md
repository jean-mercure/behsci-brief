# The concept of the day belongs to foundations of psychological science, and it is about why a chemical in the blood is not an emotion.

First-year treatments of emotion begin with an argument about ordering. William James held that the bodily change comes first and the feeling is the perception of it. We do not tremble because we are afraid, he said; we are afraid because we tremble. Cannon objected that the internal organs are too slow and too undifferentiated to carry the distinctions we feel. Schachter and Singer proposed the compromise that survived best: arousal supplies the intensity and appraisal supplies the label, so the same bodily state can be read as fear or exhilaration depending on what the person thinks is causing it.

Today's second item shows why that argument is still a methodological one. Anandamide, one of the body's own cannabis-like compounds, rose steadily across roughly forty-three kilometres of running. It overtook the walking comparison by mid-race and stayed high after the finish. A second compound dipped below the walking level early and then climbed in the closing kilometres. Euphoria, meanwhile, was higher while running than walking at several points, but not at twenty-eight kilometres. And here is the number to keep. Of nineteen marathon runners, three said they had experienced a runner's high, eight said they had not, and eight were unsure.

Three things follow, and this is the transferable part. First, the bodily measure and the reported feeling are separate variables, and here they do not move together: the molecule climbs in a straight line while the feeling does not, and most participants did not report the phenomenon the study is about. Second, the team did not test whether the runners with the biggest chemical rise felt the most euphoric. That within-person coupling is what the James position predicts, and without it you have a group-average rise in a lipid beside a group-average rise in euphoria, which is two findings rather than one mechanism. Third, and the authors say this themselves, the measurement was taken in blood and not in brain.

So the question to carry away. When a study pairs a bodily measure with a feeling, has it shown the two moving together within the same person, or only that both are higher in the same condition?

# One. Expecting to fall hurts more than expecting to rise helps.

Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, reported on Saturday. Davide Melita at the University of Granada and four colleagues ran four studies with about two thousand eight hundred participants. Two were surveys, one of American workers about mobility in society generally, one of over fifteen hundred people in Spain about their own prospects. Two were experiments, in which Spanish students and then employed Americans were placed in a fictional society and randomly told they would move up, stay put, or move down, with inequality held constant. Expecting to fall raised status anxiety, and that anxiety predicted lower satisfaction with life. Expecting to rise did considerably less, and only in the American experiment did it reduce anxiety at all.

Two cautions, and the second is the serious one. The Spanish survey threw up a result the framing does not accommodate: expecting to improve was also associated with more status anxiety, even while being directly associated with higher satisfaction. And the asymmetry that gives the study its headline is reported only descriptively. We are told the downward effect was larger than the upward one, but no formal comparison appears, and that comparison is the claim. The authors note that cultural context shapes how people read these scenarios; the remaining caveats carry no attribution at all.

# Two. What happens to the body's own cannabis-like chemicals over forty-three kilometres.

BMC Medicine, reported yesterday. The numbers are in the concept of the day, so this one is confined to the design. Michael Siebers in Essen, with Johannes Fuss as senior author, ran two field studies. In the first, nineteen trained runners covered three fourteen-kilometre loops around a lake, then came back a month or two later to walk the identical route for the identical length of time, which makes each runner his or her own control and is the best feature of the work. Blood was drawn five times. In the second, thirty-six athletes in a non-stop ultramarathon were sampled before and shortly after, at one hundred, one hundred and sixty, and two hundred and thirty kilometres.

The authors are candid: nutrition, rest and time of day were not fully controlled, running always came before walking, and the mood ratings were single items. Danielle McCartney at Sydney, who was not involved, adds that validation of the blood assay is not explicitly reported.

# Three. Narcissism, emotion regulation, and a correlation of almost nothing.

Personality and Individual Differences, reported on Saturday. Ria Tziliaskopoulos, Megan Willis and colleagues at Australian Catholic University pooled twenty-six studies, just over twelve thousand participants and eighty correlations, using a method that handles several effects from the same sample. Pooled across everything, the correlation with difficulty regulating emotion was about point one five, which is weak. Split by type, it changes completely. Vulnerable narcissism correlated about point three seven across eleven studies. Grandiose narcissism correlated point zero four across twenty-one, and was not significant.

For a first-year reader the lesson is about measurement rather than about narcissists. A moderate correlation for one subtype alongside a null for the other is the strongest evidence available that the two are not variants of one trait, and the two commonest questionnaires cannot be treated as interchangeable instruments for the same thing. Willis states the limits plainly. Every study was cross-sectional, so direction cannot be established. Every measure was self-report, which is awkward here in a specific way, because people high in grandiose narcissism may be the least likely to report struggling to manage their feelings. Too few studies reported their subscales for the two subtypes to be compared skill by skill. Heterogeneity is nowhere quantified in the coverage, and the publication-bias tests are mentioned but never named.

# Four. The Americans who never left home.

Also Personality and Individual Differences, also reported on Saturday, and this one carries the methods watch, so the design comes now and the arithmetic later. Dritjon Gruda and Peter Jonason reanalysed survey data from December of twenty twenty-two across thirty-eight American states: just under four thousand adults, of whom about two thousand seven hundred still lived in the state where they grew up and about one thousand two hundred and fifty had left. All three dark-triad traits came from a twenty-seven-item questionnaire on a five-point scale. Among those who had moved, the higher scorers tended to have gone to more densely populated states.

The authors are careful about what mobility data can show. The pattern describes where people ended up and not why they went, and they cannot rule out that living somewhere shaped the scores rather than the scores shaping the move. Two independent researchers are quoted, and one calls the account speculative, noting that individual income and education are missing entirely.

# Five. Air pollution, children's brain networks, and the effect sizes nobody hid.

The Journal of Environmental Psychology, reported yesterday. Katherine Bottenhorn and colleagues took resting-state scans from over six thousand children aged nine to eleven in the large American adolescent brain study, with about two thousand rescanned two years later. Exposure was estimated from annual air-quality models at each child's home address, fifteen chemical components grouped into six sources. Traffic emissions went with weaker connectivity in several sensory and control pairings, ammonium sulfates with stronger connectivity, including in the default mode network.

What makes this worth your time is that the predictive performance is reported rather than buried. The brain measures explained under one per cent of the variation in exposure at ages nine to eleven, and no more than two per cent of the change over two years. More tellingly, the models were trained on all sites but one and tested on the site held back, where they did no better than simply guessing the average exposure. The authors draw the conclusion that follows rather than the one the headline wants: these sources probably play only a small part once other factors are accounted for. Monica Rosenberg at Chicago, who helped develop the method, makes the same point about generalising across locations. One caveat carries no attribution and matters: no correction was applied for the number of connections tested.

# Six. One flagged for later. Does the eye read a word in sequence, or all at once?

Psychological Science, posted online on Thursday, by Hend Lahoud, Titus von der Malsburg and colleagues. This is flagged rather than summarised, because no abstract could be reached this morning. What follows is the title and the method it names, nothing more. It earns its place as the first movement on that journal's online-first list in eleven days.

The question is standard in first-year treatments of reading: are the parts of a single word identified one after another, or all in parallel? The method is what to note in advance. Scanpath analysis works on the sequence of fixations, whereas the conventional measures are averages, such as mean fixation duration or total gaze time. An average throws away the order in which the eye visited the parts of the word, and that order is the only evidence that can separate a serial account from a parallel one. Treat the finding as unread until the abstract appears.

# The methods watch, and it is about working out which model the headline came from.

The stayers-and-movers study is a good case for a habit worth forming early: ask not what a study found, but which of its analyses the reported number came from.

The raw difference in psychopathy between those who stayed and those who moved was nine hundredths of a point. The number the coverage leads with, six hundredths, comes from the strictest specification, comparing stayers against movers who grew up in the same state and were born in the same decade. In the models in between, those adding age, sex, race and state income, the difference was not significant. So the sequence runs: significant with no controls, not significant with some, significant again under the most demanding comparison.

Three observations follow. First, on the size of it. Six hundredths of a point on a five-point scale is about eight per cent of a standard deviation. Nobody could pick a stayer from a mover on that basis, and the honest description is of a faint regularity across a population, not a difference between people.

Second, on specification. A result that appears in one model but not in its neighbours is not thereby a sturdier result. Every choice of control variable is a researcher's decision, and a set of nested models is a small garden of forking paths. There is a perfectly good argument for preferring the strictest comparison here. But it has to be made before the estimates are seen rather than after, and the coverage says nothing about whether any of this was registered in advance.

Third, on the comparative claim. The framing attributes the effect to psychopathy specifically, which is a claim about the difference between three coefficients rather than about any one of them, and it requires those differences to be tested. The authors concede that the psychopathy gap was not reliably larger than the gaps for the other two traits. A difference between two estimates is not established by one of them clearing a threshold while the other fails to.

So, three habits. Find out how many models were fitted and which supplied the headline. Convert every coefficient into standard-deviation units before deciding how impressed to be. And when a paper singles out one trait from a set, check that the comparison was actually tested.
