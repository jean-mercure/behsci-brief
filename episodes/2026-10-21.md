# The concept of the day comes from the statistics and methods course, and it is what variance explained means when a model is asked to predict.

The first-year account of R-squared is a statement about one sample: the proportion of variance in an outcome accounted for by the predictors, computed on the same data the model was fitted to. A predictive model is a different object. It is fitted on one set of people and scored on people it has never seen, so the figure is out of sample and cannot be inflated by overfitting the way an in-sample figure can. That is a real improvement, and also where the reading usually stops.

Today's first item shows that out of sample comes in grades, and that the grade does most of the work. Held-out people from the same dataset, by cross-validation: ten to forty per cent. A different dataset entirely: five to eleven. A different age band: five to six per cent when a model trained on adults is asked about adolescents, and one to four in the other direction. The same models, the same measurements, the same organ, and a fivefold fall. Generalisation is therefore not a property of the model but of how closely the second sample resembles the first, which is a fact about recruitment and scanners and age ranges rather than about brains.

This is Sunday's concept from the other end. There, a design that widens the range of predictor values manufactures explained variance. Here, the figure travels only as far as the sample resemblance does. Both are one discipline: ask which population a number was computed in before asking how large it is.

Two cautions against over-crediting the improvement. Prediction is not explanation, and the authors say so: high predictive performance does not guarantee neurobiological relevance, and a model riding on age, education and sex can predict well while explaining nothing. And the headline figure is almost always attached to the broadest and least interesting outcome. Crystallised ability is predicted at thirty-eight per cent and fluid at twenty-six, then learning and memory at two per cent and processing speed at about one. The quotable number is for the construct nobody wanted to measure in particular.

Three questions for any reported accuracy. Was it computed in sample or out of sample. If out of sample, out of which sample, and how like the training one. And would it survive the removal of the known confounds.

# One. Brain measures predict how well people think, until the model meets a different sample.

That was the concept of the day, so this can be brief. A review in Communications Psychology, published yesterday, with Emily Dhamala as senior author, gathers what the large neuroimaging datasets have achieved when brain measures are used to predict cognitive performance, across youth, young adults and older adults. Models explain roughly ten to forty per cent of variance, and the structure of that range matters more than its midpoint. Task-based connectivity beats resting-state connectivity in younger people, twenty-eight per cent against eight, while in older adults structural measures take over. Stacked models combining several modalities reach thirty-two per cent where a single modality manages seven. And ridge regression performs comparably to deep learning, a useful corrective to the assumption that the elaborate method wins. The disciplining number is cross-dataset generalisation, at five to eleven per cent.

# Two. Considerate people begin each decision a little less certain that the object is theirs.

Consciousness and Cognition, reported on Monday. Self-prioritisation is one of the sturdier effects in social cognition: people classify objects assigned to themselves faster and more accurately than objects assigned to someone else. In a laboratory at Aberdeen, ninety-one adults classified pens and pencils as their own or a named best friend's across two hundred trials. Own objects were classified at five hundred and forty-two milliseconds against five hundred and fifty-seven for the friend's. The interesting part is the computational decomposition. Fitting a model to the response-time distributions located the effect not in how efficiently self-relevant information was processed, which was unrelated to social mindfulness, but in where the decision started. Participants began each trial already leaning towards mine, and that starting bias was weaker in socially mindful people. Social mindfulness accounted for about five per cent of between-person variance in speed, so this is a modest effect located precisely. Only one limitation is attributed to the authors, that culture may shape both measures. That the design is correlational and that nobody checked whether participants believed in the other player are the reporter's framing.

# Three. Twenty-one genetic associations in women and five in men, from samples of very different size.

Psychological Medicine, reported on Monday, and it carries today's methods watch, so the description stays short. A genome-wide study of four hundred and fifty-five thousand people of European ancestry compared those with a history of anxiety against those with no mental distress diagnosis. The combined analysis identified fifty-six variants. Analysed separately by sex, twenty-one reached significance in the women and five in the men, with one variant common to both. Mapped genes implicated chromatin regulation and olfactory transduction in women and lipoprotein metabolism in men. Polygenic scores were then tested in three independent cohorts, and that is where the paper is most careful, and where the methods watch picks it up.

# Four. Horror fandom predicted resilience during the pandemic, and did not in Lebanon.

BMC Psychology, reported on Monday. During the first months of the pandemic, a widely cited study of American adults found that horror fans, and more strongly fans of apocalyptic fiction, reported greater psychological resilience. That was read as evidence for threat simulation: rehearsing fear in fiction as practice for the real thing. A survey of two hundred and thirty-five Lebanese university students, during war and economic collapse, finds nothing of the kind. Horror preference and resilience correlated at point zero five six. The only detectable genre association was negative and weak, for alien invasion, which is the genre family the American study found most protective. What did replicate was the internal pattern: morbid curiosity correlated with liking for crime, horror and thriller at about point five, and not at all with romance, so the construct behaved as expected while its supposed consequence did not. The authors suggest that prolonged crisis may exhaust the capacity to convert fictional fear into coping, and call that speculative, because exposure to the crises was never measured. They also note that horror tastes were skewed, which would make any association harder to detect. One number is worth checking against the paper: morbid curiosity and resilience correlated at point one five six, which the authors report as not significant, and in a sample of two hundred and thirty-five that would ordinarily clear conventional thresholds.

# Five. People high in dark traits want those traits in a partner, and nothing redeems a violent one.

Evolution and Human Behavior, reported on Monday. Four hundred Brazilian adults rated twelve personality items for themselves and then for an ideal partner, and were each given one of six written profiles: a prosocial control, a dark-triad character with no criminal record, the same character with a physical assault, and that profile enhanced with fame, with wealth, or with extreme good looks. Two results, pointing in different directions. Self-ratings and partner preferences correlated at about point six across the three traits, and were closer to the person's own scores than to those of randomly paired participants. The second result is a clean null against the idea that antisocial traits are tolerated because they travel with status and attractiveness. Willingness ratings ran at five point nine for the prosocial control, two point four for the dark character with no record, and one point five once an assault was added. Fame, wealth and good looks all left it there. None differed from the plain criminal profile. The authors note weak internal consistency on two of the three trait scales and no attention checks. Two design problems are the reporter's: the ratings sat near the floor, which can inflate an apparent similarity, and every enhanced profile also carried the assault, so a law-abiding but manipulative partner was never tested.

# Six. Heart-rate variability before a task tracked verbal and visual creativity in opposite directions.

Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, reported on Monday. A hundred and fifty-seven students wore a portable electrocardiogram for five days and were prompted twelve times a day to produce either unusual uses for everyday objects or drawings built from abstract shapes, yielding nearly seven thousand usable measurements of heart-rate variability in the five minutes before a task. Lower variability preceded more original verbal ideas, and higher variability preceded more original figural ones. The gap between verbal and figural originality was clearest when variability was low, and not distinguishable when it was high. The association survived adjustment for age, gender, body mass index, physical activity and heart rate. The design is worth attention for a different reason than the finding. Because each person contributes many observations, the comparison is within-person rather than between people, which removes the obvious confounds of trait physiology and temperament. The price is that nothing was manipulated, so the arrow is unidentified. Ideas produced at home were rated more original than those produced elsewhere, a reminder that an ambulatory design imports the whole of daily life as a covariate. And not one limitation on the press page is attributed to the authors.

# And the methods watch, from the statistics and methods course. The difference between two subgroups is not itself a result.

Item three invites a particular error, and the invitation is almost irresistible. Twenty-one associations in women, five in men, one shared. Read naively, that is a discovery about the genetic architecture of anxiety differing by sex.

Now look at the denominators. The female sample was two hundred and eighty-two thousand and the male a hundred and seventy-four thousand. Power to clear a genome-wide significance threshold rises steeply with sample size, so the larger sample will return more hits even if the underlying effects are identical in both sexes. The hit counts are therefore consistent with a real sex difference and equally consistent with none, and nothing in the comparison distinguishes the two.

The general principle is worth memorising. That an effect is significant in group A and not in group B is not a test of whether it differs between A and B. The test has to be on the difference itself: an interaction term, or a formal comparison of the two estimates carrying its own standard error. Two estimates can fall on opposite sides of a threshold while their confidence intervals overlap almost entirely, and two can be indistinguishable from each other while one is significant and the other is not. The significance verdict for each group separately contains no information about the contrast.

Note where this paper did test a difference. The polygenic-score analysis compared predictive performance across three independent cohorts, after matching the sample sizes, which removes exactly the artefact just described. Almost nothing survived. One difference remained: the score derived from women predicted anxiety in women better than the male-derived score did, in one cohort. That is a defensible claim, and much narrower than the hit counts suggest.

One point of candour. The source here is press coverage, which does not say whether the paper itself ran a sex-by-genotype interaction test, so the criticism is of the reported claim rather than necessarily of the analysis. And the quantity that would actually answer the question is the genetic correlation between male and female anxiety, estimated with an interval.

The habit to build. Whenever a result is reported as holding in one group and not another, find the test of the difference. Without one, what is on offer is that two separate analyses returned two different significance verdicts, which is a weaker and much less interesting claim.
