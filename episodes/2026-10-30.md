# Friday the thirtieth of October, and six items on a generous day.

Good morning. This is the Behavioural Science Daily Brief for Friday the thirtieth of October. Six items today, all new, and a generous day. Five came through the aggregator PsyPost, which has run clean for ten days. The sixth came from a Nature Communications abstract, and I will say what was missing. Two other article pages were refused outright. The lead item is the big one: the main result of a replication project running since two thousand and nineteen, and about as close to a set text as this brief will ever carry.

# First, the concept of the day, and it is what a replication rate actually measures.

Start with a sentence you have certainly read: about half the findings in some field failed to replicate. It sounds like a fact about a literature. It is a fact about a literature and a criterion jointly, and today's lead item is the best demonstration I have seen.

The same two hundred and ninety-six replication attempts were graded under thirteen definitions of success. The rates ran from about twenty-nine per cent to seventy-five. Forty-six percentage points of spread, with the data held constant. Nothing changed but the question asked of them.

The most generous criterion combines the original study and the replication meta-analytically, so the original contributes to the estimate used to judge it, and a large original effect can carry a weak replication. The strictest criteria penalise a replication whose effect differs from the original in either direction, perversely punishing one better powered than the study it tests. A reader handed only the headline cannot tell which question was asked, nor that another reasonable choice would have halved the number or doubled it.

Now the quantity reported less often, which tells you more. The median correlation fell from about a quarter in the originals to about a tenth in the replications, and four papers in five produced a smaller effect second time round. A significance test throws away magnitude by construction, so a claim can be recorded as replicating on a quarter of the effect first reported. So read the effect sizes alongside the success rate.

Three more things. Inspect the denominator: about forty-nine per cent of papers replicated while fifty-five per cent of claims did, and that gap is pure arithmetic: a paper with several claims appears once in the first figure and twice over in the second. Then power, which is what makes a failure mean anything. The replications ran at over twice the original sample sizes, with a better than ninety-nine per cent chance of detecting the original effect. Without that a failure tells you nothing. Then selection, where such a headline is most vulnerable. The papers were sampled at random; those actually finished were not. Expensive studies dropped out, qualitative work and null results were excluded by design, and psychology's share rose from a quarter to over a third.

So when you next meet that sentence, three questions follow. Which criterion produced the fraction. What happened to the effect sizes. And how were the studies chosen.

# One. About half of social science findings held up when they were tested again.

The concept of the day has the numbers, so I will give you the shape of the project instead. It is the replication arm of a programme funded by the American defence research agency and run from the Center for Open Science. It began with a random sample of three thousand nine hundred quantitative papers published between two thousand and nine and two thousand and eighteen, across six social science fields. It finished with two hundred and ninety-six attempts covering two hundred and seventy-four claims. Hundreds of researchers in thirty-one countries took part, every analysis plan was registered publicly beforehand, and the original authors were invited to comment. Brian Nosek, the senior author, separates three reasons a replication can fail: the original was a false positive, it was true but exaggerated, or the replication itself went wrong. That is in Nature.

# Two. Education-linked genetic scores behave differently inside a family.

A child's genotype is correlated with both parents' genotypes, and with the family environment those parents build. So an association between a child's polygenic score and the child's behaviour is not a direct genetic effect without something to separate the two. This paper uses three thousand two hundred mother, father and child trios from the British Millennium Cohort Study, modelling the child's and both parents' associations together, with separate scores for the cognitive and non-cognitive components of educational attainment. Both were associated with externalising behaviour, meaning hyperactivity and conduct problems, and with internalising behaviour, meaning anxiety and low mood, across childhood and adolescence, more strongly with the former.

Now what is missing, because it is most of what matters. Only the abstract was served; the methods, results and discussion were not, and other routes to the full text are closed from here. So there are no effect sizes, no confidence intervals, no account of how the transmitted and non-transmitted parts of the parental score were separated, and crucially nothing on whether the child's own association survives adjustment for the parental component. The authors' own limitations are unavailable too, so nothing I have said is their view of the weaknesses. Nature Communications, last Saturday.

# Three. A grammatical choice predicts how nasty the replies will be.

Two features of how a political group gets named were measured across forty-six thousand American Facebook posts and three quarters of a million replies. The first is whether a party is named with a noun or an adjective: Senate Republicans against Republican senators. The second is whether a statement is generic: Democrats want climate action against some Democrats do. Noun labels raised the odds of a hostile reply by about thirty-nine per cent before controls and fifteen after adjusting for the post's own hostility and the page it sat on. Generic statements gave about twenty per cent adjusted. In predicted terms, gaps of two and three points. The pattern held for either party, and within a single user's comments across posts.

Five language models classified the wording, trained on human-coded examples and six hundred synthetic posts built as matched pairs differing in one feature. A separate classifier scored every comment for hostility aimed at a political group rather than a named individual, catching about three-quarters of them. Carsten Sander, the first author, puts the observational design first among the limitations himself, and notes that uncontrolled differences in post content could drive both wording and replies. Those percentages are model predictions, not observed rates. Social Psychological and Personality Science.

# Four. A threat to masculinity, and the men it did not move.

Nearly a thousand heterosexual American men answered items presented as a measure of their masculinity, then were randomly told they had scored at the top of either the average female or the male range. The outcome was a questionnaire on agreement with traditional masculine norms, where the threatened group scored about a fifth of a point higher out of seven, roughly a sixth of the typical spread. Beforehand they had completed a new scale of what the author calls hybrid masculinity: progressive gender attitudes and emotional expressiveness. Among high scorers the effect of the threat shrank towards zero.

The design point is the one the headline obscures. The threat was randomly assigned, so the main effect carries causal licence. The moderator was measured, not assigned, so the interaction the finding consists of is correlational. That matters, because the high scorers were also considerably more liberal and rated masculinity as less central to their identity, so strategic blending cannot be told apart from caring less about the thing being threatened. There are no test statistics or effect sizes anywhere in the coverage, no mention of preregistration, and the outcome is a reported attitude. Social Psychology Quarterly, where the author is quoted twice and not one caveat is attributed to him.

# Five. A partner's background and a political label, with the arrow undetermined.

An analysis of about twelve and a half thousand Americans in a long-running cohort study finds that people whose current or most recent partner's racial background differed from their own had about thirty-seven per cent greater odds of calling themselves politically liberal, adjusting for race, sex, marital status, age and education. About fifteen per cent were in such a relationship, and were much less likely to be white. I give it short treatment for what it illustrates rather than the size of the finding: ideology here is a single five-point self-description, partner composition a single yes-or-no, and the analysis uses one wave of data, so it cannot tell whether liberal attitudes come before the choice of partner, whether the relationship shifts them, or whether something else moves both. The data are also from two thousand and eight. That thirty-seven per cent is the only effect size reported anywhere. The Journal of Race, Ethnicity, and Politics.

# Six. Social media came second out of fifteen predictors of reported depression.

Eleven annual waves of an American survey panel, from two thousand and fourteen to two thousand and twenty-four, gave about a hundred and eighty-three thousand adults. Depression was a yes-or-no answer within a list of thirty-one health conditions, and reported prevalence rose from fifteen per cent to twenty-two across the decade. Each additional hour of daily social media use went with roughly seventeen per cent higher odds of reporting depression, and gaming and general internet use were as strong or stronger. The methods watch takes up the ranking, so I will take the numbers there and leave this item with the one caution the authors make themselves: a high importance ranking can reflect how much a behaviour varies across a population, not that changing it would reduce depression.

# And now the methods watch, on what a variable-importance ranking is and what it is not.

Among the top predictors is a narrow and precise claim, almost always read more widely than the analysis supports. Five things are worth separating.

First, the denominator. Fifteen candidate variables were entered: nine demographic and six measures of media time. So second place means second of fifteen, nothing more. Sleep, prior mental health history, personality, employment, life events: none were in the list. A ranking cannot promote a variable it was never given, so ask what the candidate list was and who chose it.

Second, what the importance measure measures. It decomposes a model's prediction for one person into contributions from each input variable, an attribution of the model's output rather than a measurement of the world. Fit a different model and the attributions change: what is reported is a property of the fitted model, not of the people in it.

Third, importance tracks variance as much as leverage, and the authors say so themselves. A variable can rank high because it differs greatly between people here, while one identical for everybody carries no importance, even if it matters enormously. That is why a rank is neither an effect size nor a causal estimate, and why the step from second most important predictor to reducing it would reduce depression is not one the analysis licenses.

Fourth, correlated predictors make the ordering fragile. Gaming and general internet use carried associations as strong as social media or stronger, as you would expect of three overlapping measures of screen time. When predictors overlap, importance is divided among them in ways that depend on the algorithm, so a one-place difference means nothing. And no uncertainty is reported on these ranks. No bootstrap, no confidence interval, no stability across folds. A rank with no error bar is a point estimate dressed as a fact.

Fifth, a rank can move because the population moved, not the variable. Social media went from ninth place in two thousand and fourteen to the top four from two thousand and nineteen. At least three readings fit, and importance alone cannot tell them apart: it became more harmful, it became more prevalent or more variable, or the other predictors weakened. And the model behind these ranks identified only about half of those who reported being depressed, while what it predicted is a single yes-or-no item among thirty-one conditions.

So: count the candidate predictors and ask who chose them, look for uncertainty on the ranks, and ask whether a high rank merely reflects how much that variable varies.
