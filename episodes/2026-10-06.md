# Behavioural Science Daily Brief, Tuesday the sixth of October

Good morning. Six new items today, all published yesterday. Nature Human Behaviour ran two research articles, the National Bureau of Economic Research added a working paper on sugary drink restrictions, and there is a natural experiment from Denmark worth your attention for its design as much as for its finding. Behavioural Public Policy and Retraction Watch were both quiet.

# Concept of the day. Quasi-random variation

Start with the method, because one of today's papers is a textbook case of it and the design is the whole of its credibility.

A team of economists wanted to know whether having a daughter changes how a man runs his business. The obvious approach would be to compare firms run by men who have daughters with firms run by men who do not. That comparison is worthless. The kind of man who has a daughter, and the kind of firm he runs, may differ from the outset in a hundred ways you cannot observe, and any difference you find could be those hundred things rather than the daughter.

What rescues the question is a fact about biology. The sex of a first child is very close to randomly assigned by nature. So among men who have a first child, those who get a daughter and those who get a son are, on average, balanced on everything else: their ambition, their politics, their industry, their firm's size, their unobservable attitudes. The researcher does not have to model the selection, because nature has already broken it. This is what is meant by quasi-random variation: not an experiment anyone ran, but variation in the world that behaves as though someone had.

The comparison then goes further. Rather than comparing daughter-fathers with son-fathers in the cross-section, you look at each manager before and after the birth, and use the son-fathers as the control group. That absorbs the general effect of becoming a father, which is considerable, and isolates the part attributable to the child's sex.

Three questions to put to any design of this kind. First, is the variation really random? First-child sex essentially is. Later-child sex is not, in populations where parents who want a son keep trying until they get one, which makes family size a function of the earlier children's sex. Second, does the variation affect the outcome only through the channel you have in mind? A daughter is supposed to change the manager's beliefs about women at work; if having a daughter also changed, say, his working hours in a way that affected hiring, the estimate would be contaminated. Third, and most often forgotten, is the population for whom the variation is random the population you actually care about? Hold that thought for item three.

# The items

# One. Algorithm-assisted risk messages raise flu vaccination, and saying the word algorithm costs nothing

Nature Human Behaviour, yesterday. This one belongs to Foundations of Behavioural Science and to the methods course.

A team led by Gail Rosenbaum, with Christopher Chabris among the authors, ran three preregistered randomised field trials inside a single American health system, covering about thirty-six thousand, forty-two thousand and thirty-nine thousand patients respectively. A machine-learning model identified patients at elevated risk of serious complications from influenza, and those patients were then sent a message telling them they were at high risk.

The nudge worked, modestly and reliably. Vaccination rose by between one and three and a half percentage points depending on the study and on which control group you compare against, a relative improvement of between three and fifteen per cent.

The result worth remembering, though, is a null. The trials also varied whether the message disclosed that a computer algorithm had identified the patient, and whether it gave personalised reasons for the risk rating. Neither mattered. Arms that named the algorithm did no better and no worse than arms that concealed it, and the personalised reasons added nothing.

That bears directly on the standing objection to nudging: that these interventions work only because people do not notice them, and that disclosure would therefore destroy them. Here it did not. This is the second well-powered piece of evidence in that direction we have covered in a fortnight. Note the limitation, which is real: one health system, one country, one behaviour.

# Two. Restricting what a benefit may buy changes behaviour even when it should not

National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, entered the series yesterday. Foundations of Behavioural Science.

Ten American states excluded sugar-sweetened beverages from the food stamp programme this year. David Frisvold and Felipe Lozano Rojas measured what happened, using a commercial panel of household grocery purchases running from July last year to June this year. Soda purchases fell about thirteen per cent for the average participating household.

The interesting number is the second one. For what economists call inframarginal households, those whose total grocery spending exceeds their benefit and who are therefore buying some food with their own cash at the margin, the fall was eighteen and a half per cent. Standard demand theory says that group should not respond at all. If you are already spending your own money on groceries, and the state tells you that a particular ten pounds of your grocery money may no longer buy soda, you can simply buy the soda with your own ten pounds and the benefit with something else. Money is fungible. Nothing should change.

Something changed. The authors read it as mental accounting: the benefit is mentally earmarked, kept in a separate pot, and a restriction on the pot binds even when the arithmetic says it should not. That is Thaler's mental accounting doing real policy work, and it is a rare case where a behavioural mechanism is not just decorating an effect but is required to explain its existence.

Short-run evidence, two months after implementation, and scanner panels under-record the single bottle bought at a corner shop, which is exactly where substitution would hide.

# Three. Daughters change what male managers do

Forthcoming in the Review of Economic Studies, reported yesterday. This is the natural experiment from the concept of the day, and it speaks to the methods course and to behavioural science both.

Maddalena Ronchi and Nina Smith followed six thousand seven hundred employment spells of male managers at Danish firms small enough to have a single manager, whose children were born between nineteen ninety-two and two thousand seventeen. After a first daughter arrives, the share of women among employees rises by roughly one percentage point, an increase of about three per cent on the base, and a measure of earnings equality within the firm rises by around four per cent.

Small numbers in absolute terms. But the authors put them at between a fifth and a half of the entire observed gap between male-run and female-run firms, which reframes that gap: a meaningful slice of it is not about men and women as managers, but about what particular men have happened to experience at home.

Now the third question from earlier. The variation is random among men who become fathers while running a small single-manager Danish firm. Whether it tells you anything about a large corporation with a formal human resources function and hiring committees is a separate matter, and the paper cannot answer it. Note too that the manager's change of belief is inferred from his hiring, never measured directly.

# Four. A quantity rule, not the incentive, kept Irish consultants treating public patients

Nature Human Behaviour, yesterday. Foundations of Behavioural Science.

Ireland reformed its hospital system in twenty fourteen to permit private patients in public wards, which looks like an invitation for consultants to tilt towards the better-paying work. Xidong Guo and colleagues examined two point three million inpatient discharges from two thousand nine to two thousand fifteen, with a difference-in-differences design.

The private share did not rise. What held it was not the incentive structure but an administrative rule: consultants were contractually required to treat three to four public patients for every private one, and the ratio bound. Public admissions actually rose, but by way of shorter stays, which the authors read as a possible cost in quality.

A useful corrective. Financial incentives are the first thing a behavioural economist reaches for, and here a plain quantity constraint did the work while the incentive pushed the other way. The quality inference rests on discharge records rather than patient outcomes, so treat it as a flag rather than a finding.

# Five. Two decades of American panel data do not support the gender-equality paradox

Published yesterday across two journals, Social Psychology and Psychological Reports. Foundations of Psychological Science, and the methods course.

The gender-equality paradox is the claim that psychological differences between men and women grow larger, not smaller, as societies become wealthier and more gender-equal. Evolutionary accounts predict it, and the cross-national correlations appear to show it.

Gabriele Prati, at Bologna, tested it a different way. Instead of comparing countries at one moment, he followed Americans over time, using two long-running panels: seven thousand adults tracked across three waves from the mid-nineteen-nineties to twenty fourteen, and six and a half thousand adolescents and young adults from nineteen ninety-four to twenty eighteen. Across that period the United States grew richer and more gender-equal. Gender gaps in personality stayed broadly flat, and the gap in depression narrowed.

Be candid about what this does and does not establish. One country. Two decades may be short relative to the cultural change at issue. Several constructs rest on single survey items, and the measure most central to the theory, interest in things as against interest in people, is simply absent from both datasets. Even so: a within-society longitudinal test of a hypothesis that is usually defended with between-country cross-sections is a real contribution, because the two designs are vulnerable to entirely different confounds.

# Six. A crime video moves liberal voters' policy thresholds but not their politics

The Journal of Social Psychology, yesterday. Foundations of Psychological Science, with a policy edge.

Violet Cheung-Blunden and colleagues randomly assigned participants to watch footage of retail theft or a tourism video. Liberal participants who saw the crime footage went on to propose markedly lower dollar thresholds for charging theft as a felony, while their broader description of their own politics did not shift at all. A specific policy preference moved; an identity did not.

Read this one with the brakes on. Effect sizes are not reported, which is a real omission. The samples are small, two hundred and then under a hundred, and drawn from San Francisco, which is not a representative place to study liberal opinion about crime. The outcome is a mock policy exercise, not a vote. And the mechanism the authors favour, mortality salience, is not distinguished from ordinary concern about personal safety.

# Methods watch. Convergence is not quite replication

A pairing worth noticing. Today's food stamp paper finds a thirteen per cent fall in restricted beverage purchases. Four days ago, a different team, Hunt Allcott and colleagues, also at the National Bureau, found twelve point four per cent. Two independent groups, different data assembly, almost the same number. That is worth considerably more than either estimate alone, and it is the kind of corroboration psychology has spent a decade learning to demand.

But be precise about what has been demonstrated. Both teams analysed the same ten state policies over the same few months using the same kind of commercial scanner data. They therefore share every peculiarity of that period, that policy design, and that measurement instrument, including its blindness to small-format purchases. Their agreement rules out one team having made a coding error. It does not rule out a bias they both inherit from the setting.

Independent replication in the strict sense requires new variation, not new analysts applied to the same variation. The distinction is easy to lose when a literature converges quickly, and quick convergence is exactly when it matters most.
