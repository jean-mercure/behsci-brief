# The concept of the day comes from Foundations of Psychological Science, and it is the dimensions of person perception, and the instrument that creates them.

First-year teaching on this arrives in a tidy form. People form impressions of character from a face within a fraction of a second, those impressions are consensual across observers, and they organise onto two broad dimensions, usually labelled warmth and competence. The account is attractive because it is simple and because it keeps recurring.

Today's first item is a corrective, and the corrective is methodological rather than substantive. Every study that produced the two-dimensional account asked participants to rate faces on adjectives chosen by the investigator, and then ran a factor analysis on the ratings. A factor analysis cannot recover a dimension that none of its input variables measures. If the adjective list holds four words for warmth and four for competence, a two-factor solution is close to guaranteed before any data are collected, and it will be reported as a discovery about minds when a good part of it is a fact about the questionnaire.

The remedy is to take the words away. Ask people to arrange faces in space according to how similar the faces seem in character, with no labels offered at all, and the geometry of the judgements emerges from the judgements themselves. The number of dimensions becomes something to be estimated rather than fixed in advance. Five came out, twice over, and the labels attached afterwards are the authors' reading of the recovered axes.

That is a real gain, and not a clean win. A sorting task has its own structure: how many faces are on screen, how many can be held in mind at once, the constraints of the display. Word-free is not assumption-free. And the dimensions recovered in the second experiment shifted when the racial composition of the faces changed, so the solution is partly a property of the stimulus set in this method too.

Three questions to carry into any paper reporting the structure of a psychological judgement. What were participants given to respond with, and could the reported structure have come out of that alone. Was the number of dimensions estimated, or assumed. And if the answer changes when the stimuli change, is the finding about people or about the materials.

# One. Ask people to sort faces instead of rating them, and the trait space changes shape.

That was the concept of the day, so the study itself can be brief. Nature Human Behaviour, yesterday, from Yue Xu, Umit Keles and Ralph Adolphs. Two experiments, more than three thousand participants between them, arranging unfamiliar faces by similarity of character with no adjectives supplied. The first, using predominantly White faces, recovered five dimensions: approachability, a gender-specific warmth factor, competence, morality and youthfulness. The second, using White, Black and Asian faces, also recovered five, and there race-associated stereotypes left a clear mark on the solution. The authors conclude that trait judgement has somewhere between two and five dimensions rather than a settled number. One caution. The published paper could not be read, so this comes from the preprint, which gives no per-experiment sample sizes and no limitations in the authors' own words.

# Two. Practising a letter upside down improves writing it the right way up.

Nature Human Behaviour, yesterday, from Zekun Sun, Zhiran Xie and Samuel McDougle at Yale. Eight experiments, four hundred and ninety participants, mostly recruited through Prolific, copying unfamiliar single-stroke characters with a mouse or a stylus. Training on characters rotated by ninety, a hundred and eighty or two hundred and seventy degrees improved the upright copy about as much as practising the upright form did. The controls are what make it interesting. Participants with aphantasia, who report no mental imagery, improved as well, so imagery is not doing the work. Watching without moving produced no reliable gain, and nor did tracing other characters. Tracking a moving disc along the rotated path, which gives the movement but no visible character at all, worked at least as well as rotated copying. And reversing the order of the movement cut the effect roughly in half, which points to sequential structure as part of what is being learned. The authors' own limitations: a single short session, so nothing is known about whether it lasts; no neural measures; an artificial copying task rather than a continuous skill like music or sport; and the first four experiments not preregistered. This too comes from a preprint.

# Three. A lineup beats a one-to-one comparison when the suspect is innocent, and loses when he is guilty.

Psychological Science, earlier this month. Facial recognition software has contributed to wrongful arrests by handing an examiner a single candidate photograph and asking whether it matches footage of a crime. Camryn Yuen and colleagues borrowed the logic of the eyewitness lineup and asked whether embedding the suspect among five known-innocent fillers would discipline the decision. Two experiments, about nine hundred students between them, each judging twenty-eight trials, half one-to-one and half six-person lineups. Lineups produced a higher positive predictive value for a declared match, as preregistered, but only under a model in which the fillers are known to be innocent. Under a model in which any of them might be the suspect, the advantage reverses. So the benefit is a property of the fillers rather than of the format, which is the sentence to remember. One-to-one comparison produced the better negative predictive value, meaning fewer innocent people wrongly cleared. The authors are unusually frank about the limits: the advantage needs genuinely fair lineups, no contextual bias was modelled, participants were students judging staged crimes, the base rate of true matches was far higher than in eyewitness research, and the second experiment was not preregistered.

# Four. A network model of face perception yields a marker that predicts depression four years out.

Science Advances, reported last week. Han Lu, Qiang Luo and a large consortium trained a deep neural network with separate visual and conceptual pathways to recognise facial expressions. Moderate regulation by the conceptual pathway gave the best recognition, and excessive regulation produced a negative bias. From that the authors derived a marker they call overregularisation, computed from functional imaging of thirteen hundred nineteen-year-olds watching faces shift from neutral to angry. In the seven hundred or so followed to twenty-three, the marker identified which symptom-free nineteen-year-olds would go on to show high emotional symptoms. It tracked a depression polygenic score but not genetic risk for other conditions, and in a clinical sample it separated patients from controls where conventional activation measures did not. The honest reading is that this is a prediction result and not an explanation. The computational story is a hypothesis the network makes available; the clinical specificity is what currently supports it. The limitations in the coverage are the journalist's rather than the authors': a mostly White sample of middle to high socioeconomic status, group differences small enough to leave little individual diagnostic power, and a need for validation in independent patient groups.

# Five. What people do for a living tells you almost nothing about what they want in a partner.

Personality and Individual Differences, reported last week. Peter Jonason and Dritjon Gruda put a plausible idea to the test and found nothing, which is the useful part. If occupational interests and mate preferences are both expressions of underlying disposition, people in people-oriented work should want different things in a partner. Among nearly two thousand adults across seven Western countries, occupational orientation was essentially unrelated to partner preference on a forced-choice task. Sex and relationship context did far more work: men chose physicality more often than women, women chose compassion and competence more often. Country differences in the choice of compassion were about five times larger than occupational differences. The authors' own limitations: current industry stands in for vocational interest and was never measured directly, since people take jobs for income and location as much as for inclination; non-binary and non-heterosexual participants were too few to analyse; and the forced-choice traits were broad abstractions. Note the method, which is the real reason this item is here. The paper used equivalence tests to argue that the differences are negligible rather than merely non-significant. That is the right move, and a rarer one than it should be.

# Six. Letting people pick their own nudge worked. Assigning it at random did not.

Behavioural Public Policy, published online in March. Stefan Lipman and colleagues ran a field experiment with eight hundred and thirty-nine university students choosing between healthier and less healthy snacks, under three interventions: a financial incentive, calorie information, and a social-norm nudge. Participants either had one assigned at random or chose which they wanted. Self-selection produced a marginally significant increase in healthier choices. Random assignment produced none. Most of those who chose picked the money. This bears directly on how behavioural public policy is evaluated, because the standard evidence base consists almost entirely of randomly assigned interventions, and a policy delivered as a menu is a different object from the same policy delivered as an assignment. It also complicates the inference, because a person who selects an intervention differs from one who is given it in motivation as well as in treatment, and the design cannot separate the two. No limitations here in the authors' own words: the article page was beyond the fetch tool's reach.

# And the methods watch, from the statistics and methods course. A phase two replication, an overall null, and a corrigendum.

Few nudges are better known in behavioural public policy teaching than adding a descriptive social norm, or a statement of what a missed appointment costs the institution, to a hospital reminder. The original findings are widely cited and cheap to implement. Hansen and colleagues set out to replicate them at scale, in a quasi-experimental trial covering nearly twenty-one thousand appointments at a Danish hospital, with the reminder text rotating every two months between a standard message, a social-norm framing and a cost framing. Neither framing reduced missed appointments overall. Effects, the paper says, varied by department.

Four things are worth taking from it. First, what phase two means. A phase one result establishes that an intervention can work somewhere, under conditions the researchers largely controlled. A phase two trial asks whether it works embedded in an operating service, with its own patients, departments and competing messages. A phase one success followed by a phase two null is not a contradiction but information about generalisation, which is what the policy question turns on.

Second, rotation is not randomisation. Assigning the message by two-month block means every patient in a block gets the same text, so the comparison is between periods rather than between people, and anything moving with the calendar is confounded with the treatment: seasonal illness, holidays, staffing. Defensible in a live hospital, and weaker identification than randomising patient by patient.

Third, and this is the part most often got wrong, effects varied by department, offered after an overall null, is a hypothesis and not a finding. With enough subgroups one will show a difference, and the chance of that rises with the number examined. The right response is to ask whether the subgroup was specified in advance, how many comparisons were made, and whether a mechanism behind the pattern would survive a test designed to look for it.

Fourth, the paper carries a corrigendum published in June. Its content could not be retrieved, and the honest thing is to say so. The habit it points to is this: a published paper is a record that can be amended, and checking whether a correction exists is part of reading it. A replication whose authors published an overall null and then corrected their own record is behaving as the literature is supposed to behave.
