# What makes something a perceptual cue, and how you show that one is used.

Today's concept belongs to Foundations of Psychological Science. Reading where another person is looking is among the most precise things human perception does, and the textbook account rests on a single cue: the position of the dark iris and pupil against the white of the eye. It is geometric, high in contrast, and the reason humans are unusual among primates in having unpigmented sclera.

The difficulty is that this cue degrades badly in ordinary conditions. Turn your head and the iris shifts within the socket for reasons unconnected to where your eyes point. Light your face from one side and the contrast carrying the cue collapses. Yet people read gaze accurately regardless, so either the system tolerates the degradation or it has something else to go on.

Gwenisha Liaw and Colin Palmer propose the glint: the mirror reflection of a light source on the wet front of the eye, whose position relative to the pupil depends on both the eye's rotation and the light's direction. In principle it carries information the iris cue does not.

Now the methodological core. Showing that information is present in an image establishes nothing, because the world is full of regularities the visual system ignores. To show a cue is used, you must take it away while holding everything else fixed, and show performance falls. That explains the study's most criticised feature: only a computer-rendered face lets you delete one reflection and change nothing else. The stimulus is synthetic, and that price is real.

The design's best feature is the condition where nothing happened. Face-on and frontally lit, removing the glint made no significant difference. Read carelessly, that is a failed experiment. Read properly, it is the control that gives the other two their meaning: when the primary cue is intact, a secondary cue has nothing left to contribute, so a well-behaved account predicts a null there. The glint earns its keep only once the iris cue is compromised. That is what cue combination means: a cue's weight depends on how reliable its competitors are. Note too that only about half the participants noticed the glints, yet performed much like those who did not, so the information was used without being available to report.

Three questions for any study of this kind. Was the candidate cue removed, or merely allowed to vary? Was there a condition in which the cue should not have mattered, and did it duly fail to matter there? And is the claim about what the system uses, or about what the person noticed?

# One. The reflection on the eye is a cue the visual system uses.

The concept of the day gave you the logic, so the paper can be brief. Liaw and Palmer, at the National University of Singapore, report in Psychological Science on three experiments with forty people each. Half the rendered faces carried glints, half had them removed. With the head rotated twenty degrees while the eyes stayed on the viewer, and with the face lit from forty-five degrees to one side, the faces with glints produced fewer errors in judging gaze direction. One caution about the paper rather than the design: the coverage reports no test statistics, effect sizes or intervals of any kind, and attributes not a single limitation to the authors. Its article number also sits well below every other paper from that journal I have covered this autumn, so it has probably been in press for months.

# Two. One instrument, thirty-one countries, and a tenfold spread in the answer.

A consortium led by Susan Ayers at City St George's, University of London, reports in the British Journal of Psychiatry on about eleven thousand women surveyed six to twelve weeks after giving birth, through routine maternity services in thirty-one countries. Every site used the same twenty-nine-item scale, in English or local translation, the protocol was preregistered and the data deposited publicly.

Averaged across countries, about twenty-three per cent met the scale's definition of a traumatic birth and about seven per cent its symptom criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder following childbirth. But the average is not the interesting number. The country figures for a traumatic birth run from seven per cent to sixty-nine, with the middle half between fourteen and twenty-seven. That is close to a tenfold range on one instrument. Several things could produce it, and the authors say so. Country samples ran from about a hundred and thirty to over sixteen hundred, some sites collected answers on paper, some online, two by telephone, and no measurement-invariance work is reported for any translation, which is what you would want before claiming the scale means the same thing everywhere. The authors note the wide intervals, the pandemic-era hospital restrictions, and the possibility that an instrument developed in English fits non-Western settings less well.

# Three. Liberals rate dominant leaders less likely to win, and the gap may run both ways.

Danbee Chon and Hemant Kakkar report in Political Psychology that people on the political left judge dominant leaders both less likeable and less likely to succeed than people on the right do, across five studies: a two-wave survey of under two hundred American employees rating their actual supervisors, then four experiments of three hundred to a thousand participants using fictional candidates, social media posts and campaign advertisements.

The architecture is the point, because you will meet this shape constantly. Dominance was manipulated; political orientation was measured, mostly by a single seven-point item. So the headline is an interaction between a randomised factor and a measured one, and the causal licence from the randomisation does not extend to the comparison the headline is about. In the last experiment participants saw exit polls putting the dominant candidate at forty-five, fifty or fifty-five per cent, and conservatives barely adjusted their bets, which the authors read as conservatives overweighting a dominant leader's chances rather than liberals underrating them. To their credit, they concede that without an objective benchmark the study cannot say whose predictions are nearer the truth, which is candid in a paper whose title uses the word underestimate.

# Four. People think their opponents are closed-minded.

This one gets short treatment, as a warning. André Mata at the University of Lisbon, with colleagues at Linköping, reports in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology that people underestimate how open supporters of other parties are to opposing views, across two surveys and two experiments. The misperception is the entire finding, and the coverage quantifies it nowhere: no estimated value, no actual value, no signed gap. Compare the norm-correction study I covered ten days ago, which gave you the gap in points and let you see which way a correction had to push. Every outcome is a stated willingness to talk rather than an observed conversation, and the article number is lower than one from the same journal in October, so it is not new work.

# Five. An audit of AI search summaries, with the judge itself unaudited.

Haofei Xu, Umar Iqbal and Jacob Montgomery at Washington University in St Louis have posted a measurement study of Google's AI Overviews as a preprint. Over forty days earlier this year an automated browser ran about fifty-five thousand trending queries and pulled out about ninety-eight thousand factual claims. Summaries appeared on roughly fourteen per cent of queries, rising to about sixty-five per cent when the query was phrased as a direct question. Cited sources were on average more credible than the first page of ordinary results, and almost a third did not appear on that first page at all.

The headline is that about one claim in nine was not supported by the page it cited, mostly by omission rather than outright contradiction. Here is where to slow down. Each claim was checked by a separate large language model reading the cited page. No human coders. No reported agreement with human judgement. No precision or recall for the judge. The authors do flag that pages updating live produced false alarms. But the figure is the output of an unvalidated classifier, not a verified rate, and should be read that way.

# Six. Wildfire smoke in pregnancy and recorded autism.

This item is here for what kind of measurement it rests on, which is the methods watch, so I shall be brief. Stefania Papatheodorou at Rutgers, with a large team, reports in JAMA Pediatrics on nearly nine hundred thousand mother and child pairs in United States Medicaid records. An increase of three quarters of a microgram per cubic metre in wildfire fine particulate matter across pregnancy went with a hazard ratio of one point three for a recorded autism diagnosis, on an interval from about one point one to one point six. The figure for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder was essentially nothing, and the dose-response was not linear.

# The methods watch. When the exposure variable is itself a model's output.

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and for once concerns the independent variable.

In most observational studies the exposure is treated as data, something recorded about each person. Here it is a prediction. Daily fine-particulate concentrations were estimated by a machine-learning framework for every square kilometre of the continental United States, and the wildfire share obtained by subtracting modelled background pollution from the modelled total. So the exposure is a difference between two model outputs, inheriting the error of both. Subtracting two uncertain quantities of similar size is the operation most likely to leave you holding noise, and the wildfire fraction is small: about half a microgram per cubic metre, against more than nine from everything else. That estimated field is assigned to a woman by her postal code, as weekly averages, confirmed only at the start and end of her pregnancy. So the variable is outdoor concentration where she is recorded as living, not what she breathed, as Papatheodorou says herself.

Why this is more than a caveat. Error in an exposure usually biases an estimate towards finding nothing, so one point three might understate the association. But error that varies systematically with place does not behave so conveniently. If the smoke estimate is worse in some regions, and those regions also differ in how readily a child is assessed, then the measurement error and the outcome are entangled rather than independent, and you cannot work out the direction of the bias from first principles. The non-linearity is suggestive: risk levels off at the highest concentrations, which is what you would see if the estimates were least reliable where the smoke was thickest. And the outcome is a proxy too, since autism cases were identified by algorithms scanning billing claims for diagnostic codes, and Papatheodorou notes that access to assessment services influences which children acquire a recorded diagnosis.

Here is the move to admire. The team does not argue about unmeasured confounding in prose. It prices it, calculating that a confounder would have to be associated with both wildfire exposure and autism by a risk ratio of at least one point nine each, on top of everything adjusted for, to explain the finding away. Whether that reassures you depends on whether you can name a candidate that strong, and the obvious one, local diagnostic practice and access to healthcare, they discuss but do not adjust for.

Three habits from this. Ask whether an exposure was measured or modelled, and at what resolution it was assigned. Ask whether the outcome counts a population or a billing system. And read the interval rather than the point estimate, because one point one to one point six is consistent with an effect too small to matter and with one that matters a great deal.
