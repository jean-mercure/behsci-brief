# The concept of the day is one-reason decision making

Today's items carry nothing from Foundations of Behavioural Science, so the concept comes from a paper covered last month. Banavar and Bansak, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, found that a little over half the voters in a candidate-choice experiment behaved as though they had settled on one feature and stopped.

Start with the model your course gives you first: weighted-additive integration. A chooser weights each attribute, multiplies weight by value, adds it up and picks the highest total. That is the engine under expected utility, and under the intuition that a bad score on one thing can be made up for by a good score on another. We call that compensatory.

Against it stands Gerd Gigerenzer's fast-and-frugal programme, which proposes a different class of rule. A lexicographic heuristic adds nothing up. Take The Best searches cues in order of validity, stops at the first that tells the options apart, and chooses on that alone. Everything after is ignored, however large the difference.

Now the hard part, and the reason this argument has run thirty years. The two models usually predict the same choice, because an attribute with a heavy weight is normally also the most valid cue. They come apart only where a later attribute could overturn the first, which a weighted model sometimes allows and Take The Best never does. Building a study that produces enough of those cases is the whole problem.

If that holds for even a large minority, improving an attribute nobody reaches is worth nothing, and the choice architecture question becomes which cue gets searched first rather than how to shift the weights.

Then the caution. Notice the phrase, behaved as though. A model that fits choices does not measure the process behind them, and choice data will often fit both a lexicographic rule and a compensatory one with extreme weights. What separates them is process evidence: what people acquire, in what order, and when they stop.

So three questions. Did the study watch the process, or infer it from the outcome? Did the design contain cases where a later cue could have overturned an earlier one? And is the claim about everybody, or about a proportion?

# One. Three documents on the clock change, and three different answers

PsyPost on Saturday covered three papers on one question, more useful than any one alone. The first, from a University of British Columbia team in the Canadian Medical Association Journal, is a solicited policy analysis: sunrise and sunset times for six Canadian cities, circadian research and simulations, and a recommendation of permanent standard time. The third, in Brain Sciences, is a narrative review of sixty-six studies screened by one author, of which only twelve measured anything during an actual clock change. It too favours permanent standard time.

The middle one disagrees, and is the strongest. Steponenaite and colleagues, in the European Journal of Epidemiology, systematically reviewed over a hundred and fifty epidemiological studies across thirty-six countries, two reviewers grading quality. Only one in five was rated high. They find neither the twice-yearly transitions nor summer daylight saving uniformly harmful: no consistent change in sleep duration after the spring shift, none in psychiatric outcomes after either. They recommend keeping the transitions with targeted measures, while conceding that if they went, circadian evidence would favour permanent standard time.

Notice what has happened. The two documents recommending a policy rest partly on studies the third grades low quality. None of the coverage's caveats is attributed to any author.

# Two. A survey of civil servants that admits what it is

The Behavioural Insights Team, with the Centre for the Governance of AI and the network Apolitical, posted this a week ago Wednesday. Michael Hallsworth and colleagues surveyed British public servants over five weeks in late summer; two hundred responded, a hundred and eighty-five qualified.

Ninety-five per cent had used an artificial intelligence tool for work in the previous month, four in five at least a few times a week, almost all of it Microsoft Copilot. But the pattern by task is the interesting part. More than sixty per cent use these tools weekly for gathering information and synthesising evidence. Fewer than twenty per cent use them for forecasting or decision support, and six in ten never do. Self-reported time saved averages about three hours a week, against roughly two in the government's own earlier trial.

What earns this a place is the limitations section, which is unusually honest. The authors state that the sample is self-selected through three mailing lists, that no response rate can be calculated, that respondents are more senior and more policy-focused than the service as a whole, that the questionnaire was rewritten partway through fieldwork, and that the link between heavier use and bigger savings is correlational, a caveat printed on the chart. Against all that, not one confidence interval or significance test appears anywhere.

# Three. A mediation model in which everything was measured at once

Cimsir at Anadolu University and Hansen-Brown at Bridgewater State, in Personality and Individual Differences, covered by PsyPost on Saturday. Just over five hundred students, roughly half American and half Turkish, filled in online questionnaires for course credit, each rating a parent.

Students who rated a parent higher on vulnerable narcissism reported more emotional enmeshment in childhood, and those reporting more enmeshment reported more depressive symptoms now. The indirect path held in both countries, with no structural path differing significantly. American students reported more enmeshment than Turkish, the reverse of what the authors predicted.

The design point to carry away is this. All three were reported by the same person, by the same method, in the same sitting. The mediator asks about childhood up to sixteen; the outcome about the last two weeks. So current mood is free to colour the recall the model treats as its cause, and nothing in the design rules that out. No instrument is named, no coefficient or probability value appears anywhere, and not one limitation is attributed to the authors. The article number is lower than every other paper from this journal the brief has covered, so it has probably been in press for weeks.

# Four. Undiagnosed attention deficit in women treated for depression and anxiety

Held in reserve since late October and kept short, because its interest lies in what it omits. Civan and Berkol, psychiatrists at a teaching hospital in Istanbul, in the International Journal of Psychiatry in Medicine; the article number marks it the twenty twenty-five series, so it is older than its coverage.

A hundred and seventy women attending as outpatients for depression and anxiety, assessed once, recruitment never described. Beck inventories as an entry screen, cut-offs not given; then two screening scales, one unnamed; then a clinical interview whose instrument is also not named, with diagnoses made on standard psychiatric guidelines and neither diagnostic manual mentioned. Thirty-three met criteria, fifteen of those with no previous diagnosis. No confidence interval, no test statistic, no effect size. Four limitations offered, not one attributed to the authors.

# Five. A trait and a state measured at the same moment

Also from the reserve, read in late October and held back on length. Berg and Richter leading, with Back and Opel senior, in the Journal of Affective Disorders, covered by PsyPost last month. Three hundred and thirty-four psychiatric inpatients at a German university hospital, staying at least four weeks; the number analysed is not given.

This is naturalistic observational data, not a trial: different therapies, different lengths, no control group, nothing randomised. Depression was measured with a twenty-one symptom questionnaire, personality with a very brief fifteen-item Big Five instrument, both on admission, roughly fortnightly, and before discharge. Depression fell substantially, neuroticism fell while conscientiousness and extraversion rose, and steeper falls in neuroticism went with larger reductions in depression. Higher neuroticism went with slower improvement across fourteen symptoms, higher extraversion with faster relief on seven.

The methodological interest is the timing. Personality and symptoms were measured at the same moments, and the symptom-level analysis used each patient's average personality during the stay, not a score from before treatment. So a trait and a state were measured together, and the direction between them cannot be recovered. Exactly one limitation is attributed to the authors, and it is the awkward one: the fifteen-item instrument has reduced reliability, particularly for extraversion, the headline trait.

# Six. Why recorded autism has risen

An explainer rather than a study, published on PsyPost in mid-October, assembling about twenty papers the author calls a selection rather than a survey. I will be brief, because the reason it is here is what it shows about measurement, which is the methods watch. In short: recorded diagnoses have risen steeply almost everywhere, and the one study that held its measuring instrument still finds no rise at all. The vaccine hypothesis sits under causes tested and not supported, on a study of over two and a half million American children.

# The methods watch is a trend measured with an instrument that would not stay still

A trend compares the same quantity at two different times. If the instrument changed in between, part of what looks like change in the world is change in the ruler, and no further data from that instrument separates the two. Autism prevalence is the clearest live example, because every confusion is documented.

First, the category changed. The diagnostic manual's third edition gave way to the fourth, which widened the definition and added Asperger's disorder in the mid-nineties. King and Bearman attribute about a quarter of the Californian increase to criteria changes alone, and Coo found a third of British Columbia diagnoses over eight years were a switch from another category. Not a new case. A relabelled one.

Second, an administrative count is not a measurement of a population. Russell and colleagues report a rise of nearly eight hundred per cent in recorded British primary care diagnoses over twenty years, while the average age at diagnosis rose from under ten to over fourteen, the opposite of what earlier detection would produce. Talantseva and colleagues make the point in one comparison: record-review surveillance returns about one and a quarter per cent, insurance databases a third of one per cent. Same construct, different machinery.

Third, eligibility can create cases. Ranjan and Breunig used the region-by-region rollout of Australia's National Disability Insurance Scheme as a difference-in-differences design, and estimated the scheme itself raised reported prevalence by half a percentage point, roughly a third of the national rise. Substitution explained little, because intellectual disability diagnoses rose alongside autism rather than falling. A threshold effect, not a relabelling.

Fourth, the design that settles it holds the instrument fixed. Kim and colleagues screened every seven-year-old entering school in one South Korean city across twelve birth cohorts using identical methods, sixty-two thousand children in all. Prevalence came out at roughly two to three per cent, flat. That study carries more weight than the twenty registry analyses around it, and its limits deserve equal scrutiny: one city, four in ten screen-positive children completing a full assessment, the rest estimated by a model.

Three habits, then. Ask whether the instrument was the same at both ends of the period the trend covers, and if not, treat the trend as a statement about the ruler. Ask whether the thing counted is a population or an administrative system, because a system can grow while the population stands still. And notice what a constant-instrument null does not give you: a flat line in one city is no estimate of change elsewhere.
