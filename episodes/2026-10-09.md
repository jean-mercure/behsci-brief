# Friday the ninth of October, two thousand and twenty-six.

Seven items today, all published yesterday, and a good day for research design rather than for headline findings. Nature Human Behaviour carried two papers, and one of them, a Swedish twin study, is the best teaching material we have had in a fortnight. It supplies the concept of the day. There was no new working paper batch from the National Bureau of Economic Research, and Behavioural Public Policy has published nothing since July.

# The concept of the day is the co-twin comparison.

This comes from the statistics and methods course, and the twin paper is worth studying as a design even if you never look at the result.

Start with the problem. You want to know whether heavy screen use makes adolescents depressed. You have a cohort, you have the exposure, you have the symptoms, and you find an association. The difficulty is that families where children use screens heavily are not like families where they do not. They differ in income, in parental mental health, in supervision, in a hundred things you measured badly and a thousand you never measured at all. Statistical adjustment only removes the confounders you thought to measure.

The co-twin design removes a whole class of them at once, not by measuring them but by changing the comparison. Instead of comparing heavy users with light users across the population, you find pairs of twins who differ in exposure, and you compare each heavy user with their own co-twin. That pair shares a household, a neighbourhood, a school catchment, two parents and a date of birth. Everything held in common is differenced away without ever being named. Non-identical twins additionally share about half their segregating genes; identical twins share all of them.

What makes this a genuine test, rather than just a tighter comparison, is that the rival explanations predict different patterns. A purely genetic association would disappear in identical pairs and shrink only partly in non-identical ones. A purely shared-environment one would disappear in both. A real causal effect would survive within pairs. The pattern of attenuation is itself the evidence.

Two cautions belong with the technique. Non-shared confounders survive: whatever makes one twin the heavier user, perhaps temperament, perhaps a different group of friends, may also be driving the symptoms. And the design is expensive in statistical power, because only pairs who actually differ in exposure carry information, and there are far fewer of those than there are twins. That second point matters more than people realise, because an attenuated, non-significant within-pair estimate may be a genuine null or an underpowered test, and the two look identical on the page. Measurement error compounds it, biting harder within pairs than across the population, so the design leans towards finding nothing. A within-pair null is strong evidence against a large effect and weak evidence against a small one.

So, the questions to put to any study of this kind. How many discordant pairs were there, actually? Was the exposure measured well enough that a within-pair difference means anything? And does the pattern across identical and non-identical pairs match the explanation offered?

# One. A loading delay of one second measurably reduced learning.

Asaf Mazar and colleagues, in Nature Human Behaviour yesterday, looked at what they call microdelays, the brief lags while an online lesson loads. Two field studies: about twenty-seven thousand American schoolchildren doing online mathematics, and about fifty-five thousand aspiring programmers, most of them in Latin America. Delays of one to two seconds went with fewer assignments completed, longer time spent on the ones that were, more distraction mid-task, and lower scores. Among the programmers, with less active engagement, more multitasking, and a lower chance of reaching a key course milestone.

For the behavioural science course this is friction acting as choice architecture with nobody having designed it. We normally treat friction as an instrument, something a policymaker adds or removes on purpose. Here it arrives through infrastructure, and falls hardest on the learners with the worst connections, which makes it a question about distribution rather than usability. The identification comes from variation in load times within the same person, so stable differences between learners are handled, but time-varying confounds are not: a connection that degrades may coincide with a difficult week.

# Two. The screen-time association survives the cohort but not the twin pair.

This is the paper behind the concept of the day, so briefly. Tong Gong and colleagues followed nearly twenty-two thousand Swedish twins from age nine to twenty-four, linked to national patient and prescription registers. Across all twins, heavy use went with meaningfully higher symptoms of depression and anxiety, and heavy interactive use at fifteen carried roughly a threefold higher risk of a later clinical diagnosis. Within pairs who differed in exposure, those estimates fell by between a sixth and almost all of their size, and mostly stopped being significant once corrected for multiple testing. Passive viewing showed nothing credible at any stage. The honest reading is that shared family background explains most of what the cohort shows. Worth noting that data collection began in two thousand and four, before smartphones.

# Three. Eye contact acted as a gate on word learning in babies.

Wei Zhang and Victoria Leong and their team, in Nature Communications, showed forty-seven infants of around nine months, recruited in Britain and in Singapore, videos of a woman reciting invented languages, with her eyes fully visible, partly visible, or not visible at all. The babies were then tested on real words against nonsense words. Learning appeared only when the eyes were fully visible: there, the infants looked about one and a quarter seconds longer at the nonsense words, the standard signal that they had registered the pattern. In the other two conditions the difference was not significant.

The important detail is what did not vary. Time spent looking at the speaker was essentially the same across all three conditions, around twenty-two seconds, and it did not predict learning. So this is not a story about attention being captured. Something about the gaze itself is doing the work. The team also measured brain-to-brain coupling between the adult on the video and the infant, and that coupling predicted learning better than chance.

But the mediation claim, that coupling is the channel through which gaze acts, needs care, and the authors say so themselves: the coupling measure was chosen because it predicted learning well. That is selection on the outcome, and a measure picked that way will look predictive whether or not it is the mechanism.

# Four. Taking your time over a political judgement improves how your opponents see you.

Zachary Grey, Paul Bloom and colleagues, in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, ran three experiments with a few hundred American adults each. Participants gave their own view on a political issue, then rated the moral character of a stranger who either agreed or disagreed with them, and who had either deliberated at length or answered immediately.

Deliberation helped, but only with opponents. Someone who disagreed with you was rated noticeably better if they had thought about it, and that held in all three experiments. With someone who agreed with you it made no net difference, and the second experiment showed why: looking thoughtful helped, looking uncertain hurt, and the two cancelled out. What earns the credit is visibly doing the work. Mere ambivalence, being unsure without having deliberated, bought nothing.

The effects are small, the stimuli are brief written vignettes with no tone of voice or body language, and the participants and the issues are entirely American. The authors also did not measure whether a deliberating opponent was simply assumed to hold more moderate views, which they cannot rule out.

# Five. The striatum rehearses motor sequences in sleep, without the hippocampus.

Emmett Thompson and colleagues at the Sainsbury Wellcome Centre, in Nature Neuroscience, trained mice on a five-step sequence of nose pokes. During later sleep, striatal neurons replayed the same firing sequences, sometimes compressed in time and biased towards the rewarded ones. Replay running in the forward direction predicted better performance the next day.

Then they did the two things that turn a correlation into something stronger. Blocking plasticity in the striatum during sleep alone sent performance back to earlier training levels. And mice whose hippocampus had been removed learned the sequence perfectly well, with their striatal replay intact. For the psychological science course that is a clean demonstration of multiple memory systems: the textbook account, in which consolidation runs through hippocampal replay, is at best incomplete for skills. This is rodent work, and whatever triggers the striatal replay is still unknown.

# Six. Methylphenidate restored sensitivity to costs, not to rewards.

Mads Lund Pedersen and colleagues, in Psychological Medicine, tested sixty-one adults with attention deficit disorder on their usual medication and on placebo, double blind, against sixty-three controls. The task was deliberately dry: six shapes are worth certain amounts, six colours cost certain amounts, and you see one of each together and decide whether the combination is worth taking.

Unmedicated, the attention deficit group accumulated evidence more slowly and committed on less of it, and weighted the rewards more heavily than the costs, where the controls did the reverse. The medication raised the rate at which cost information specifically was taken in, and left reward sensitivity untouched.

The reason to attend to this one is the modelling. With only an accuracy score you would know that one group did worse and nothing else. The authors fitted a drift diffusion model, which separates three things: how fast evidence comes in, how much of it you require before deciding, and how the two kinds of evidence are weighted. The drug moved exactly one of the three. Against it: the compound may simply have improved memory for the learned values rather than the decision process, and side effects may have let some participants work out which arm they were in, which rather undermines the blinding.

# Seven. Finnish municipalities with more social contact have higher birth rates.

Briefly, and with a warning attached. Eetu Soini and colleagues, in Biodemography and Social Biology, linked a Finnish health survey of about a hundred thousand adults across two hundred and ninety-nine municipalities to those municipalities' birth rates three years later. Places where people expected more social support had higher fertility; general trust, community participation and loneliness showed nothing. But the unit of analysis is the municipality, not the person, so this is an ecological correlation and will not support a claim about individuals, and the direction is unestablished: children themselves generate social contact, through schools and nurseries and youth activities.

# The methods watch. Ninety-seven brains and a political attitude.

A paper in Current Psychology reports that social dominance orientation, roughly the belief that some groups ought to sit above others, correlates with less grey matter in the anterior insula, a region associated with empathy. The sample was ninety-seven Spanish university-campus adults. The correlations were about minus zero point three on each side, and the headline figure is that a model combining insula volume with age and head size accounted for up to twenty-nine per cent of the variance in the attitude.

Treat that figure with suspicion, and understand why. A correlation of minus zero point three accounts for nine per cent of variance, not twenty-nine. The larger number is a multiple regression fitted and then evaluated in the very same ninety-seven observations, which flatters itself unless it is checked against data the model has not seen. And with ninety-seven people the confidence interval runs from about minus zero point five to about minus zero point one, so the data are consistent with an effect a third the reported size. Structural brain correlations with behavioural traits need samples in the thousands before they settle down, and this literature is littered with findings that shrank or reversed.

The paper's own contents make the point better than any outside critic could. It was not preregistered. The amygdala and the prefrontal regions, both predicted in advance and both reported in an earlier Singaporean study, showed nothing. A whole-brain search found nothing either, so the result rests entirely on having restricted attention to the insula beforehand. And empathy, the proposed mechanism, was never measured.
