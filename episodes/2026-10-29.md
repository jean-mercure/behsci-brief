# The concept of the day. Your body is a guess, and there is more than one of them.

Here is something that sounds like a party trick and is not. Sit someone at a mirror, hide one hand behind it, and place the reflection of the other where the hidden hand should be. Stroke both in time, and the brain accepts the reflection. This is a cousin of the rubber hand illusion, the most reproduced demonstration in the psychology of the body, and the lesson of both is the same. Your body is not delivered to you as a fact. It is a running inference assembled from vision, touch and the sense of where your limbs are, and when those three disagree the inference bends.

The syllabus then adds a complication that is easy to recite and hard to believe. There is not one representation of the body but two. The first is the body image: the conscious picture of what your body is like, the one you can introspect on and report in a questionnaire. The second is the body schema: a working map used by the motor system, specifying where your limbs are and what they can reach, and to which you have almost no conscious access. It is hard to believe because in most experiments the two move together, and if everything shifts at once there is nothing to see.

Today's first item pulls them apart, and that, rather than the extra finger, is what to remember. The questionnaire tapped the body image: on a nought to ten scale, the feeling of having six fingers came out at about four and a half under the illusion against about one in the control. The second measure tapped the schema: participants judged the smallest gap their closed hand could squeeze through, and that judgement shifted too. Both measures responded. Neither responded with the other. The correlation between what people said and how they judged the gap was not significant, and nearly half the participants felt little or no sixth finger while the group's spatial judgement moved anyway.

Three things follow. A dissociation tells you more than a correlation, because two measures that answer the manipulation but not each other are evidence of two systems rather than one. A subjective rating is therefore a poor stand-in for a perceptual change: someone who says nothing happened may still be measuring space differently. And watch the baseline, because these estimates were underestimates throughout, where the older literature found the opposite.

So when a paper tells you an illusion worked, ask which measure says so, whether the measures agree, and what it would mean if they did not.

# One. A sixth finger, and what the hand thought it could do.

The study is in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, led by Denise Cadete at Birkbeck in London. Fifty adults, each tested in both conditions in a counterbalanced order, with the design and main analyses registered before any data were collected. The illusion is the mirror arrangement just described, with the final stroke landing on empty table just beyond the reflected little finger at the moment the hidden finger was touched. The control was identical, except that the stroke landed on the finger itself. The concept of the day has the numbers, so I will leave them there. What the item adds is an honesty problem: only one caveat is traced to the authors, and the coverage gives no test statistics, no p-values and no effect sizes for any comparison at all.

# Two. Horror is not just a lot of fear.

Pamela Marie Taylor, at Akita International University, writing in Cognition and Emotion, proposes that what separates horror from fear is not how much you feel but a particular judgement: how abnormal the event seems. Two studies, sixty American adults in each, recruited online. Everybody read four short tabloid headlines about people being hurt, held for seven seconds each. Two things varied: whether the harm healed or was permanent, and whether the wording was vague or concrete. Horror was scored by combining ratings of horrified and shocked, fear by combining scared and afraid.

Permanent rather than moderate harm lifted horror by about a point and fear by under half a point. Concrete rather than vague wording lifted horror by about seven-tenths and fear by about two-tenths. Perceived abnormality accounted for most of that gap, and the second study replicated the pattern.

The main prediction was registered in advance. The mediation analyses were not: the coverage says plainly they were added after the results were known. There is also a measurement worry nobody on the page owns. Horrified sits next to shocked, and shocked sits next to finding something unusual, which is the mediator, so some of that pathway may be built into the wording rather than discovered in the data.

# Three. Who issued the fact-check mattered more than what it said.

This went online this morning, in Scientific Reports, from Alexandra Morozova and colleagues at the Higher School of Economics in Moscow. Fifty-three participants rated medical headlines for trustworthiness and for willingness to share them, first cold, then again after a label reading Truth or Fake was attached. Each label was credited either to a credible medical source or to a shaky one. Brain activity was recorded throughout.

The asymmetry is the finding. A label saying Truth raised both trust and willingness to share, but only when a credible source had issued it. A label saying Fake pushed ratings down whoever had issued it. The debunking travelled on its own; the endorsement needed a messenger. That is the messenger effect from Foundations of Behavioural Science turning up somewhere you can watch it arrive: credible sources produced a larger early electrical response within a few hundred milliseconds, before the label's content could have been digested.

Two cautions, both large. This is an early-share version of an accepted manuscript, not the final text. And only the abstract was reachable, so I cannot tell you whether assignment was random, whether the brain measures were specified in advance, or what the authors say about their own limitations.

# Four. Looking at something you find beautiful slows you down.

From Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, by Paolo Barbieri, Irene Ronga and colleagues at the University of Turin. Two experiments on separate groups, thirty people and then twenty with electrodes on the scalp. The materials were a hundred and eighty black-and-white landscape photographs, matched for brightness, contrast and size. Each participant rated every image for how much they liked it, and the comparison was made inside each person, between their own favourites and their own least favourites.

The behavioural result is a nine-millisecond delay in pressing a key, about three per cent. The electrical result is that the slow build-up of activity preceding a voluntary movement was smaller before a liked image, and the rhythms associated with holding still were stronger a couple of seconds beforehand.

Barbieri is careful in the coverage that liking was measured subjectively, so this concerns individual preference and not objectively beautiful pictures. The unattributed cautions bite harder. Thirty and twenty are small groups. Liking was measured and not manipulated, so some other property of the images could be doing the work. No correction for multiple comparisons is mentioned anywhere, in a study reporting several time windows and two frequency bands.

# Five. What people take the Bible to say about the poor depends on what they earn.

Samuel Perry, a sociologist at the University of Oklahoma, writing in the Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion, took a nationally representative American survey from twenty twenty-two, about five thousand seven hundred adults, and one question from it. When Jesus and the prophets spoke of caring for the poor, did they mean charity by individuals, or an obligation to build a fairer society.

The answer is an interaction rather than a main effect. Among the poorest respondents, being more religious went with the structural reading. Among those earning a hundred thousand dollars or more, attending more often went with the charity reading. Among the least religious, income made no difference. Religion, on this account, does not push everyone the same way; it amplifies the position the person already occupies.

Two cautions. The first is unusual and serious: the coverage reports no numbers whatsoever, no coefficients, no percentages, no p-values, so there is no judging how big any of this is. The second is the design. One survey at one moment, household income as a thin proxy for social class, and an outcome that is a single forced choice, which rules out by construction the likeliest possibility, that most people hold both readings at once.

# Six. Tomato paste and the brain, in a trial built to measure something else.

Published in the journal Antioxidants and covered two days ago. Forty-seven healthy Spanish adults in their forties and early fifties, forty-two of whom finished, spent three months eating about two tablespoons of tomato paste a day and three months avoiding tomatoes and other sources of the same pigment, with a month between. Attention and processing speed improved by something like four per cent of their starting scores. Associative memory barely moved on one measure and not at all on the composite. Executive function did not move. The methods watch takes up the design, and the design is the whole story, so I will take the numbers there.

# The methods watch. A trial powered for one thing and reported on another.

Item six is a randomised trial, and the word randomised does a great deal of work on a listener. So take it apart.

Start with what the study was built to detect. The sample size was calculated to find a change in a blood protein, not a change in cognition. A power calculation is a commitment. It names the outcome the study can actually speak to, and that is much of what licenses reading a significant result as a finding rather than a possibility. Every other outcome is along for the ride. And the protein it was sized for moved only borderline.

Now count the outcomes. There are at least five cognitive scores. Two moved, one moved a little, two did not. No primary cognitive outcome is nominated anywhere, and no correction for multiple comparisons is mentioned for the cognitive results, though one is mentioned for the brain scans, where the findings did not survive it. Five outcomes with no nominated primary and no correction is not the test of a hypothesis. It is a search. A search is respectable; the only requirement is that it be reported as one.

Three further features make it worse rather than better. The trial was open label: participants knew which phase they were in, and so did the staff running the cognitive tests, which measure effort as much as capacity. There was no placebo. The comparison was an active diet that took away tomatoes, watermelon, papaya, grapefruit and guava, so the contrast is between eating a thing and being told not to eat several, and the authors themselves say the restriction may have contributed.

And then the per-protocol analysis. Drop the fourteen participants whose blood suggested they had not kept to the diet, and the effects nearly double. That analysis is no longer randomised. Adherence is a property of the person, so the moment you select on it you are comparing compliers with compliers, and those are not the groups the coin toss created. The randomised estimate is the smaller one, which is the general rule: a per-protocol analysis almost always flatters the intervention, and it is never the trial's answer.

None of this makes the result false. It makes it a result of a particular kind. So, four habits. Find the power calculation and see which outcome it names. Count the outcomes and look for a nominated primary. Check whether the headline comparison is the randomised one or a subset. And ask who was blinded.
