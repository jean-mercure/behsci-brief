# The concept of the day is the difference between normative and informational influence

This belongs to Foundations of Behavioural Science, and it is the oldest distinction in the study of conformity.

When people change their behaviour to match those around them, two quite different things might be happening. The first is informational influence. You look at what others are doing, you treat it as evidence about an uncertain world, and you genuinely update what you believe. The second is normative influence. You go along with the group because agreement is comfortable and dissent is costly, and your private belief does not move at all. You say the thing. You do not come to think the thing.

The classic demonstration is Solomon Asch and the line judgements, in the nineteen fifties. Participants gave an obviously wrong answer about the length of a line because everyone else in the room had given it first. The crucial part is not the error rate. It is the debriefing afterwards, in which many participants said they had seen the lines correctly all along and had simply not wanted to be the one who disagreed. Without those interviews we would read the study as evidence that the group changed what people saw. With them, we know that for many it changed only what people said.

Now hold that against the fourth item in today's brief, in which three hundred and sixty people were dropped into group chats with artificial agents voicing consistently liberal or consistently conservative politics. People shifted towards the room. The authors are careful, and they say plainly what their outcome variable was: the position a participant typed into a conversation. That is expressed opinion. A shift in it is equally consistent with a real update, with ordinary social accommodation, and with simple politeness. The one clue pointing towards the normative reading is that dissenters reported irritation and anger, because informational updating does not usually make people cross.

Why this matters beyond the laboratory. Norms are the cheapest instrument in the choice architecture toolkit, the N in MINDSPACE, and norms-based interventions are routinely evaluated on stated intentions, or on behaviour measured while the norm message is still in front of the person. An intervention that only moves expression will decay the moment the audience leaves the room.

So two questions to put to any norms result you meet this term. Was the outcome measured where the norm could still see it? And would the effect survive if nobody were watching?

# One. A parenting programme looked like a failure for a decade, and then the schools closed

Shruti Jha, John List, Rebecca Royer and Anya Samek have a working paper out this week that should unsettle anyone who evaluates early childhood programmes.

Parents in Chicago Heights were randomly assigned to a parenting programme, to a preschool programme, or to a control group. Ten years later, on the eve of the pandemic, the parenting effect had faded to nothing. That is the familiar story in this literature, and it is why such programmes are so hard to defend at a finance ministry.

Then came the school closures, which handed the authors a natural experiment they had not designed. The groups came apart again. Children whose parents had been through the parenting programme lost about a tenth of a standard deviation of test score. Children in the control and preschool groups lost between a quarter and three tenths. Roughly three times as much.

The authors call this latent human capital, and their reading is that parental input and school input are substitutes rather than complements, so what the parents acquired became visible only once the school stopped supplying the same thing.

Be careful with the size of it: one shock, one town, and comparisons chosen after the pandemic rather than before. The methodological point survives regardless. A programme judged on an average difference measured in a calm year can be scored at zero while carrying real insurance value, and we have no routine way of pricing that.

# Two. Bad news sticks in every topic, but not equally

Isha Agarwal, Wentong Chen and Eswar Prasad built monthly sentiment measures for twelve economic subjects out of thirty-five years of the Wall Street Journal, and asked how long a shock to sentiment lasts.

Negative shocks produce a larger cumulative response than positive ones in all twelve topics. Every one. This is negativity bias, which you meet in Foundations of Psychological Science as a feature of attention and memory, showing up in the aggregate textual record of a newspaper rather than in a laboratory task. The asymmetry is largest for security and human rights, corporate strategy, health and safety, and real estate.

The finding I would underline is the one the authors did not expect. Topics with dense and frequent data releases, financial markets above all, correct their negative narratives no faster than anything else. More information does not, by itself, dissolve a bad story.

One limitation to carry away. This is a study of newspaper text, not of readers: it shows persistence in coverage, not in beliefs.

# Three. Cap the stake and the mispricing stays

Eric Zitzewitz has a working paper on PredictIt, the regulated American political prediction market, covering twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two, when traders were limited to eight hundred and fifty dollars per contract.

Bid prices across the possible outcomes of a single question routinely added up to more than a hundred and ten, which means there was free money on the screen, an arbitrage profit available even after fees. And the overpricing of unlikely outcomes, the favourite longshot bias, ran worse than on comparable platforms.

Here is the reading that matters. The obvious inference is that these traders were unusually irrational. The better inference is that the mechanism which normally cleans up irrationality had been switched off, because correcting a mispricing requires somebody to bet against it at scale, and the position limit made scale impossible. Bias in the participants and bias in the price are two different things, and the bridge between them is arbitrage.

# Four. People restate their politics to match the room, even when the room is made of chatbots

We have used this one already, so briefly. Cleone Mitsui and Yuta Kawamura ran two experiments, published yesterday, with a hundred and eighty participants each. People in conservative-leaning chats expressed more conservative positions than people in liberal-leaning chats, and the gap survived controlling for where each participant started.

The honest part of the result is in the proportions. A participant's own prior ideology accounted for about twenty-two per cent of the variation in what they expressed. The chat environment accounted for four. The room matters, but not nearly as much as the person does. And the limitations are the authors' own: participants knew they were talking to machines, there were no social consequences, and the agents announced their politics bluntly rather than by implication.

# Five. One patch of cortex may encode gender across faces, bodies and objects

Wenjie Liu and colleagues scanned twenty-two adults while they viewed male and female faces, male and female bodies, and objects conventionally associated with one sex or the other. Decoding the activity with machine learning, they found a cluster in the right middle temporal gyrus carrying gender information across all three categories, apparently drawing on mid-level features such as contour and texture. The authors read this as an abstract representation of gender.

Treat it gently, and here is why it is interesting anyway. This is an eLife reviewed preprint, so the peer reviews are published alongside it and you can read them. The reviewers call the statistics weak, flag differences between the image sets as a confound, and ask the authors to temper the claim. I include it less for the finding than for the sight of peer review happening in public.

# Six. No link between newborn circumcision and autism in a cohort of nearly three thousand boys

Monica McGrath and a large team report in JAMA Pediatrics, yesterday, on two thousand seven hundred and seventy-one boys across fourteen American research sites, combining medical records with parent-reported assessments.

The raw figures run against the hypothesis before any statistics are applied. Six per cent of circumcised boys had an autism diagnosis, against nine per cent of those not circumcised. After adjustment, circumcision predicted neither of the two autism-related measures.

One caveat about what the study can and cannot test. The mechanism sometimes proposed involves paracetamol given during the procedure, and paracetamol was recorded in only four per cent of cases, so the paper is poorly placed to examine that pathway even as it answers the headline claim. The cohort is observational and the questionnaires are screening instruments rather than diagnoses. For the statistics and methods course it is a clean example of an adjusted null arriving where the unadjusted association already pointed away from the alleged harm.

# Seven. Eight weeks of salsa classes reduced depressive symptoms in a randomised trial

Brennan Delattre, Joshua Buckman, Catherine Harmer and Susannah Murphy randomised a hundred and twenty-one people aged eighteen to twenty-four, all with mild to moderately severe depressive symptoms, either to an eight-week salsa dancing programme or to a waiting list.

Depression scores fell by about three points in the dancers and by under one point in the waiting list, a gap of roughly two and a half points, which clears the usual threshold for a clinically meaningful change. Social anxiety fell, daily happiness ratings climbed more steeply, and social anhedonia worsened over time in the waiting list while staying flat among the dancers.

A likeable result. It is also today's methods watch, so hold on.

# The methods watch. A trial can be properly randomised and still overstate its effect

The salsa trial is a good specimen precisely because it is honest work. Three features that inflate an estimate are all present, and the authors disclose all three.

First, the comparator is a waiting list, and a waiting list is not nothing. It is an instruction to wait, and the finding that social anhedonia deteriorated in that group is consistent with the comparator having been made worse rather than simply left alone. Measure against a deteriorating group and the difference comes out larger than it would against an active alternative, such as a weekly social meeting with no dancing in it.

Second, and this is the big one, the dropout was lopsided. Seventeen of sixty-six left the dance group. Two of forty-seven left the waiting list. Randomisation balances groups at the moment of allocation, not at the moment of analysis. If the people who walked away were disproportionately those getting no benefit, the dancers who remain are a favourably selected group and the comparison you are reading is no longer the randomised one. This is exactly why intention-to-treat analysis, which keeps everyone who was allocated whether they completed or not, is the default in trial reporting, and why differential dropout belongs in the abstract rather than a footnote.

Third, the outcomes were self-reported, everyone knew which group they were in, and one of the researchers taught some of the classes. Expectancy plus an unblinded investigator is the standard recipe for a demand effect on a questionnaire.

None of that means the effect is absent. The honest reading is that eight weeks of dancing probably helps somewhat, by less than two and a half points, and that the next trial needs an active comparator and a blinded assessor.

One postscript on how much weight a single celebrated finding can bear. Retraction Watch reported last month that Dan Ariely's two thousand and two paper on procrastination and deadlines has been retracted, after analysis indicated the data had been tampered with. It is a standard citation for commitment devices, and worth knowing before it goes into an essay.
