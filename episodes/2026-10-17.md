# The concept of the day, the person-by-situation interaction, and why oxytocin is not a prosocial hormone

This belongs to Foundations of Psychological Science, and the paper at item four is the occasion for it.

You will have met oxytocin described as the bonding hormone, or the trust hormone, or the prosocial hormone. Notice the shape of all three. Each says: administer this compound and, on average, across people, behaviour moves in one direction. That is a main-effect claim, and fifteen years of attempted replication have left the main-effect claims in poor condition.

The salience hypothesis is a different kind of claim, and that is what makes it interesting. Oxytocin carries no valence of its own: it does not make social information pleasant, it makes social information louder, and what follows depends on what that information is and who is receiving it. The compound supplies the amplification, the person the content.

Today's paper is a clean illustration, because the same substance produced different outcomes selected by the person. Among participants high in attachment anxiety, recollections of their mothers came out more negative and less positive under oxytocin than under placebo. Among those high in avoidance, the emotional content did not change at all; what changed was how much they were willing to say, and they said less.

Now the design point, which is the part to remember. The obvious rival explanation is simply that anxiously attached people have gloomier memories of their mothers, and a between-subjects study cannot exclude it, because the comparison it makes is between different people. This study used a crossover: every participant attended twice, once on oxytocin and once on placebo, so each served as their own control. A stable individual difference in how someone remembers their childhood cannot produce a shift between that person's own two visits. The within-person change is what licenses the causal reading.

Then the conceptual payoff. An interaction is not a weaker main effect but a different proposition, and it predicts that the effect has opposite signs in different people. Which means it also predicts that a large study pooling everybody together will find approximately nothing, because the two halves cancel. So a null main effect is evidence against the bonding-hormone claim and entirely consistent with the salience claim.

Finally, the discipline. The human raters and the two automated scoring programs did not agree about which group the effect appeared in, and an interaction whose direction depends on the instrument is to be held loosely. So, three questions for any claim that hormone X causes behaviour Y: in whom, against what comparison, and measured how.

# One. Most voters decide on one feature and stop

In the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, reported yesterday, Nidhi Banavar and Kirk Bansak at Berkeley put three thousand seven hundred American adults through ninety choices each between pairs of options described by six features, across three domains: which immigrant to admit, which primary candidate to support, and which climate policy package to adopt.

They then fitted a model that classifies each individual by the decision rule their pattern of choices implies. Roughly half the participants decided on a single feature and stopped, a rule the literature calls take the best. About three in ten weighed everything together. The remainder simply counted up how many features they liked on each side.

Two things make this more than a taxonomy. Two thirds or more of participants used different rules on different issues, so this is not a personality trait but something summoned by the problem. And the average preference for a female candidate in a study like this is a composite of people who prefer women, people who prefer men, and a large group who barely weight gender at all. The mean describes none of them.

The authors are careful about the limits. The model classifies patterns of choices, not processes in the head, and someone who weighs everything but has one overwhelming priority will be misclassified as using take the best. Simulations also indicate that random guessers get filed as counters, so counting is rarer than the figures imply.

# Two. A child's own genes, not the parents' home, carry attention deficit traits by the age of eight

In Behavior Genetics, reported the day before yesterday, Daniel Wechsler at King's College London and colleagues used a Norwegian cohort of mother, father and child trios, about twelve and a half thousand at age five and about twelve and a half thousand at age eight. This item carries today's methods watch, so the result in brief: at age eight the best-fitting model showed effects of the child's own genotype and no detectable indirect effect running through either parent, while at age five the models could not settle the question at all. It is the design and the ambiguity that matter more than the finding, and both are discussed at the end.

# Three. Where relationships are easy to leave, people choose whose reality to share

In Social Psychological and Personality Science, two days ago, Abdo Elnakouri at Houston, with Eli Finkel at Northwestern and Abigail Scholer, reports five datasets on relational mobility, which is how easy it is in a given place to form new relationships and leave old ones.

The expected result would be that where leaving is easy, people invest less in agreement with others. That is not what they found. Overall shared reality across a network did not go down; what changed was the gap between the contacts who were useful to them and those who were not.

The experiment is the cleanest part. Five hundred and ninety adults each named a contact who had helped their career, one who had not, and a newer acquaintance, then wrote about that acquaintance either drifting away or staying close. Those who wrote about drifting reported less shared reality with the less helpful contact, three point seven against four point zero on a seven-point scale, and no change for the helpful one.

The limits are substantial and stated. Three of the five datasets are correlational, so the causal claim rests on the one experiment. The archival sample was recruited through social media and was eighty-six per cent women, the network studies were also mostly women at one university, one study measured shared reality with a single item, and the helpful-versus-unhelpful gap reached significance in only some of the analyses.

# Four. Oxytocin made anxiously attached adults recall their mothers worse

In Emotion, reported yesterday, Melissa Shemirani and Jennifer Bartz, with colleagues, gave seventy-seven men and women intranasal oxytocin at one visit and a placebo at another, then asked them to recall and talk about childhood memories involving their mothers. This item carries the concept of the day, so briefly: the effect of the compound depended on the person's attachment style rather than running in one direction for everybody, and the two automated scoring programs disagreed with the human raters about which group showed it. The sample is young and predominantly White and Asian, and it concerns mothers only.

# Five. Smartphone dependency displaced study time, and the stress pathway did not survive

In Behavioral Sciences, reported on Wednesday, Joungmin Kim at Soonchunhyang University in South Korea followed one thousand one hundred and sixteen adolescents from a national panel at ages fourteen, fifteen and sixteen. Dependency on the phone at fourteen predicted lower school adjustment, poorer self-regulated learning and lower resilience at sixteen, and two routes between those points were tested.

One survived and one did not. Time displacement held up: more gaming and less independent study at fifteen, then worse self-regulated learning at sixteen, even after adjusting for how stressed the child already was at fourteen. The academic stress route did not survive that adjustment, which the author reads as phone use and stress being concurrent vulnerabilities rather than one causing the other.

That contrast generalises. A mediator measured only at the second wave absorbs whatever was already present at the first, so controlling the baseline is what distinguishes a genuine pathway from a correlated starting point. Everything here is self-report, and the baseline wave was collected in twenty twenty-one, during the pandemic, which plausibly raised screen time and academic stress together.

# Six. Sleep loss ended the lift before the muscle was finished

In the International Journal of Psychophysiology, reported yesterday, Chengcheng Zhu, Puyan Chi and colleagues had thirty-five male students who train with weights hold an arm contraction at thirty per cent of their maximum until exhaustion, once after normal sleep and once after being kept awake until three in the morning, with recordings from the scalp and from the muscle throughout.

Endurance fell, but the muscle recording showed no difference in peripheral fatigue, which points to the failure being central rather than in the muscle. Connectivity between the movement networks and the resting networks rose at rest and weakened during the task. One finding needs care: under normal sleep a weaker connection between those networks predicted better endurance, and after restriction the relationship reversed. A sign reversal in thirty-five young healthy men, after one acute night, is to be noted and waited on rather than built upon.

# Seven. Flagged rather than summarised, are age effects in inhibition just general slowing?

Psychological Science published online, earlier this week, a paper by Jacob Sellers, Han Zhang, John Jonides and Taraz Lee asking whether age effects in inhibitory control are explained by general slowing. No abstract can be found anywhere, so nothing is reported here about what they concluded. The question is worth carrying all the same: if older adults are slower at everything, a larger age difference on an inhibition task than on a neutral one may be arithmetic rather than a deficit.

# The methods watch, the within-family design and the null that was never powered

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the trio study at item two is the case.

Start with the confound, because it is structural rather than statistical, and that distinction is the whole point. A child's genotype is correlated with the parents' by descent, and the parents also build the environment the child grows up in. So a genetic score measured in unrelated people pools two quite different things: the child's own genotype acting on the child, and the parents' genotypes acting indirectly through the home they make. No increase in sample size separates them, because the problem is in the structure of the data, not the precision of the estimate.

Trios break it. Within a family, the child's genotype varies around the average of the two parents' according to which copies happened to be passed on, and that variation is independent of the environment the parents provide. Putting the parents' genotypes in the model therefore isolates the child's own contribution. If the move sounds familiar, it should: it is the same one as the comparison between twins in a pair, which carried the concept of the day on the ninth, and the comparison between siblings that Wednesday's brief relied on. Three designs, one idea.

Now the restraint the result requires. At age eight the model found direct effects and no indirect ones, which is a reasonably clean answer. At age five it is not an answer at all: a first model suggested maternal indirect effects of nearly five per cent against direct effects under two, and then formal model comparison preferred a model with no genetic effects whatsoever. That is not the discovery that genetic nurture is absent at five, but a demonstration that the age-five data could not discriminate. Which is Thursday's methods watch by another route: a procedure that cannot tell two hypotheses apart has not chosen between them.

Two measurement points compound it. The outcome was a total score, whereas earlier work located indirect effects specifically on hyperactivity and inattention, and aggregating across symptom types averages a real effect in one component towards zero. The ratings came from mothers only, so rater variance sits inside the measure, and a mother's own traits colouring how she rates her child risks reintroducing a version of the very parental pathway the design was built to isolate.

So the habit, on meeting a null from a within-family design: ask what interval the estimate carried, and ask whether the outcome was measured at the grain at which the effect was expected to appear.
