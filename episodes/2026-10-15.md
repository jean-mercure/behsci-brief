# The concept of the day, heterogeneity and the average treatment effect

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the commentary at item three is the occasion for it.

A mean describes a population. It does not describe any member of it, and it does not have to resemble any member of it. That sounds obvious stated baldly, and it is routinely forgotten the moment an average treatment effect appears in a results table.

Take the case in front of us. Suppose you are designing a policy to shift household energy use, and you have measured the average household's patience and its aversion to risk and to loss. You calibrate an instrument to that average: say, an upfront cost against a long stream of savings. If the population is in fact two groups, one impatient and loss-averse and one patient and comfortable with risk, the average sits between them and corresponds to neither, and your instrument can be wrong for both halves at once. That is simply what a mean does when a distribution has two humps.

Three distinctions are worth keeping apart, because they get run together.

The first is between the average effect and the distribution of effects. A trial reporting a modest positive average is consistent with everybody being helped a little, and equally consistent with half the sample being helped a great deal while the other half is harmed. Those are very different policies, and the same number describes both.

The second is between heterogeneity in the effect and selection into treatment. If a scheme works better for the patient, that is heterogeneity. If the patient are also the ones who sign up, that is selection, and the two produce the same pattern in observational data while implying quite different things about what would happen if you scaled the scheme up.

The third is between heterogeneity you measured and heterogeneity you did not. The answer to the first is to state in advance which groups you will split the sample by, and how many such splits you will make, so that a subgroup result means something when you find one. The answer to the second is humility.

The point underneath all three is that heterogeneity is a question of design, not an afterthought in the reporting. If you want to know for whom an instrument works you have to measure the characteristic you think matters, in advance, and power the study to split on it. You cannot recover that from a sample that was only ever large enough for a mean.

So, two questions for any policy evaluation. For whom was this effect estimated? And would the policy still look sensible if the distribution, rather than the average, were the thing you cared about?

# One. Automatic imitation appears in all twenty-two countries tested, and culture barely moves it

In Communications Psychology yesterday, Oliver Genschow and colleagues report two studies on automatic imitation, the tendency to be faster at a hand movement when you are watching the same movement than when you are watching a different one. The first was preregistered, with just over four hundred participants in Vietnam and the United States. The second covered nearly four and a half thousand participants across twenty-two nations.

The effect appeared in all twenty-two. The hypothesis under test, that it would be stronger in more collectivist cultures, did not: the interaction came out non-significant, with a probability value of about point one six and a Bayes factor just under two in favour of no effect.

This item carries today's methods watch, and the reason is that a Bayes factor of just under two is not evidence of anything. The authors say so themselves. Note also that about one participant in five was excluded, and that the proposed mechanism, how much attention people pay to the distinction between self and other, was never actually measured.

# Two. A national art contest shows that memorability can be designed, and a neural network reads it better than people do

In Psychological Science, reported the day before yesterday, Trent Davis, Yifei Evie Chen and Wilma Bainbridge at the University of Chicago took advantage of an unusual natural setting: a national art competition in which some entrants were explicitly trying to make work that would stick in the mind.

Two findings. Artists' intentions showed up in how well their work was later recognised, which means memorability is not purely a property of the image that happens to the viewer, but something that can be aimed at. And a trained neural network predicted which pieces would be remembered better than human observers did, and better than the artists themselves did.

The second result is the more interesting one, because it separates two things that are easy to conflate: having a property and knowing you have it. The network has no insight into art and no access to intentions. It is reading regularities in the image that people cannot report and artists cannot fully control.

One real caution: the published abstract gives no sample sizes at all, for the artworks or the viewers, so the precision of any of this cannot be assessed from what has been released.

# Three. The average household that energy policy is written for does not exist

In Nature Reviews Psychology, in a Journal Club piece the day before yesterday, Marjan Nikoloski at the Free University of Amsterdam discusses work in which about two thousand two hundred Dutch household heads completed six choice tasks over energy options, alongside incentivised measures of risk aversion, loss aversion and present bias. This carries the concept of the day, so one line only: the preferences that policy instruments assume are distributed, not uniform, and designing for the mean of a spread-out distribution is a choice with consequences. It is a commentary rather than a results paper, so take the underlying numbers from the original.

# Four. Morning anger and sadness track the same day's relationship satisfaction, for both partners

In Communications Psychology yesterday, Lisa Birthelmer and colleagues, with Anita DeLongis and Christiane Hoppmann, followed eighty-nine couples in which one partner had had a stroke, with both members reporting for up to fourteen consecutive days.

The design is worth a sentence, because it is what makes the result possible. Each person is measured many times, so each acts as their own comparison, and each is measured alongside their partner, so the model can ask whether my morning mood predicts your evening satisfaction as well as my own. That is the actor-partner interdependence model, fitted here across two levels.

Anger and sadness reported in the morning went with lower relationship satisfaction that same day, for the person feeling them and for their partner. The effects were within-person, which rules out the simple explanation that unhappy couples are just unhappy.

The limits are plain and the authors state them. It is observational, so a bad morning and a bad day may share a cause. Eighty-nine couples is small for a model of this complexity. And the proposed moderator was measured only once, after the diary period, which puts it in the wrong temporal position to explain anything that happened during it.

# Five. Drafting with AI changed what petitions said and left what they achieved alone

Nature Human Behaviour published a research briefing yesterday on work by Joshua Corpus, Sarah Gilbert and Mor Naaman concerning petitions drafted with the help of a language model. The finding, as summarised, is a split: the texts changed in identifiable ways, while the outcomes they achieved did not. If that holds up it is a corrective to the assumption that better-written advocacy is more effective advocacy. A research briefing carries no method and no sample sizes, so this is flagged rather than assessed.

# Six. Safe reinforcement learning as a shared problem for brains and machines

In Communications Psychology the day before yesterday, Pranav Mahajan and Ben Seymour at Oxford set out a review arguing that biological and artificial learners face a common difficulty: how to learn by trial and error when some of the errors are catastrophic.

Standard reinforcement learning explores, and exploration means occasionally taking the bad option to find out that it is bad. An animal cannot run that algorithm, because some options kill it. The review works through the strategies that get round this, among them learning from observation rather than action and treating certain states as off-limits before anything is known about their value, and argues that pain and fear are better understood as parts of such a mechanism than as mere signals of damage.

It is a review and not new evidence, but as a framework for how an organism learns about danger without being destroyed by the lesson it is well matched to the psychological science course's material on learning and on emotion.

# Seven. The department has published two essays on its own blog

Two pieces appeared the day before yesterday on the LSE psychology and behavioural science blog, both by students and both squarely on the psychological science syllabus. Molly Wong's, on whether the sky is really blue and how languages carve up the colour space, is the better of the two: it handles a genuinely contested area carefully, and is a good model of how to write about a question the evidence does not settle.

Yuxi Tao's, on the claim that the brain cannot tell the difference between rejection and a broken bone, is worth reading with more scepticism than it asks for. The overlap in brain regions responding to social and to physical pain is a genuine finding. But the regions in question are involved in a great many things, so shared activation is weak evidence that two experiences are the same kind of thing, and the step from overlapping activity to identity of experience is exactly the reverse inference that imaging work is repeatedly warned against.

# The methods watch, what an inconclusive Bayes factor licenses

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the imitation paper at item one is the case.

The headline hypothesis there failed. The interaction between collectivism and imitative compatibility gave a probability value of about point one six, and a Bayes factor of about one point eight in favour of there being no effect. The conventional reading is that a Bayes factor needs to reach something like three before it is worth interpreting in either direction. One point eight is not three. The authors state this plainly, and the fact that they do is the reason this is worth dwelling on.

Here is what the number buys you. A probability value above point zero five says you failed to reject the null, and cannot distinguish two quite different situations: that there is no effect, and that your study was never in a position to find one. Absence of evidence and evidence of absence collapse into the same output. A Bayes factor separates them, because it compares how well the two hypotheses predicted the data you actually got. A large value says the data favoured the null. A value near one says the data did not discriminate at all, which is itself worth knowing and which a probability value will not tell you.

The paper contains an instructive illustration of how unstable this can be. In the first experiment, the interaction with nation gave a Bayes factor of about one point four under one analysis and about ten point seven under another, on the same data. The first is uninformative, the second is reasonably strong evidence for the null. Nothing changed except the model. That should make anyone cautious about quoting a single Bayes factor as though it were a property of the data rather than of the data and the analysis together.

And there is a ceiling on what any null here can mean, which is the exclusion rate. Roughly one participant in five was dropped. Whatever the remaining sample shows, it shows about four-fifths of the people recruited.

So the habit: when a paper reports a null, look for the Bayes factor or the confidence interval, and ask whether the study could have discriminated at all before asking what it found.
