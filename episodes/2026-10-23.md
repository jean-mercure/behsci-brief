# The concept of the day is the difference between episodic and semantic memory

This is from Foundations of Psychological Science, and it is Endel Tulving's distinction. Episodic memory holds events. They have a time and a place, and recalling one feels like travelling back. Semantic memory holds facts, known without reference to the occasion on which you learned them. These are not two filing cabinets. A single memory of your first week at school contains both, and the distinction is as much about the mode of retrieval, re-experiencing against simply knowing, as about where anything sits.

A study in Psychology and Aging works it three ways. It is today's second item, so I will keep that one short later.

First, it separates how much from what kind. The older adults did not recall less. They spoke for longer and gave more details per memory, eleven against seven. But a smaller share of those details were episodic, roughly two thirds against four fifths, and a larger share were semantic facts. What gets reported as age-related memory decline is often a statement about the mixture rather than the total.

Second, retrieval mode becomes visible when you ask for it directly. Being told to produce a unique event sets a different search than being told to produce a repeated routine. When the instruction changed unpredictably from trial to trial, accuracy fell from about eighty per cent to about seventy, by the same amount in both age groups. That equality is the useful part: it separates the general cost of switching strategy, which everybody pays, from the age effect, which is something else.

Third, the age effect runs the opposite way to the textbook shorthand. Asked for a specific one-off event, the two groups were level. The older adults fell behind only when asked for the repeated routine. The familiar line is that ageing erodes the specific and spares the general. Here the general was the harder request, because answering it well means suppressing the particular episode that arrives first.

One caution, continuing Monday's theme: every finding here is a proportion within a coding scheme of four categories, over a denominator that is each participant's own output, which differed by age.

So, the questions to carry. A proportion of what, and with what denominator. Within a person or between people. And does the word decline name a loss of capacity or a change of strategy, given that a life story weighted towards gist rather than episode may suit the job of telling somebody about your life.

# One. Intelligence, charitable giving and voting in the Swedish registers

The lead item is in The Economic Journal, by Mikael Elinder and Oscar Erixson. They took cognitive test scores from Swedish military enlistment, more than one point two million men tested between the late nineteen sixties and the late nineties, and linked them to three things people did rather than said: charitable donations recorded in the tax registers, validated turnout in the two thousand and nine European election, and ownership of an electric, ethanol or gas-powered car.

A one standard deviation increase in score went with a forty per cent higher chance of donating, about thirty per cent for voting, and about fifteen for the car.

Then the useful part. Among nearly six thousand twin pairs, comparing the twin who scored higher with the twin who scored lower, the associations roughly halve, to about twenty-five per cent for giving and thirteen for voting. Adjusting further for education, income, marriage and children leaves giving and voting standing, while the car association disappears.

For Foundations of Behavioural Science the interest is the measurement. These are revealed behaviours rather than stated preferences, so nobody is over-reporting their own generosity, and the twin comparison then strips out most of what families share. Not one limitation on the source page is attributed to the authors. The caveats offered are that this remains an association, and that three financial and political acts are a narrow measure of prosociality.

# Two. How older adults tell the story of their lives

That was the concept, so the study itself can be brief. It is in Psychology and Aging, from Greta Melega, Louis Renoult and colleagues at the University of East Anglia. Thirty-seven adults aged sixty to eighty-five and thirty-seven undergraduates were given twenty-one cue words online and asked, trial by trial, for either a one-off event or a repeated routine, then spoke for up to two minutes. The numbers are in the concept. What belongs here is the weakness. The design was not preregistered. The baseline block always came first, so tiredness and strategy are tangled up with the block. And the older participants scored as well as the undergraduates on attention and working memory, which makes them an unusually healthy group to generalise from.

# Three. Lying down raises the pressure at which pain begins

Psychophysiology, from Gustavo Reyes del Paso at the University of Jaen with colleagues in Spain and Austria. Eighty-three healthy students went through a fixed sequence of sitting, standing, lying and standing again, while a machine pressed with steadily rising force on one fingernail. Pain threshold and tolerance were higher lying down than standing, though the thresholds themselves shifted only from about three point four to three point six kilograms, and the authors say the clinical relevance of that is uncertain. The proposed mechanism moved much more sharply: baroreflex sensitivity, the reflex that links blood pressure to heart rate, roughly trebled from standing to lying. Across participants, higher resting blood pressure went with lower pain ratings, which is a known and counter-intuitive finding the lead author says the public is largely unaware of.

Two cautions. Ratings of the stronger pressures behaved as predicted, but the weakest ones ran slightly the other way. And the posture order was not randomised, so the last standing phase always followed lying down, and the authors themselves suspect the rush of blood on standing up may have blunted the very effect they were measuring.

# Four. Childhood cognitive ability and the pace of adolescent brain change

Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, from Phoebe Thomson, Philip Shaw and Divyangana Rakesh at King's College London, using the American Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study: about ten and a half thousand young people scanned at four points from age nine or ten into the middle teens. Children with higher fluid cognition at the start lost cortical surface area and volume faster over the following years, most clearly in the frontal lobe, while cortical thickness was unrelated to anything. White matter went the other way, with higher reasoning scores predicting a higher starting point and a slower rise. And all three cognitive measures predicted how connectivity between brain networks changed, with lower scorers becoming more connected and higher scorers separating faster.

Note what the headline is doing. Faster shrinkage is being read as refinement rather than loss, and that reading comes from the wider developmental literature, not from anything measured here. The authors also had to fall back on straight-line models because the final wave of data was incomplete, which is awkward in a study about rates of change in a process known to be curved.

# Five. One in three teenage boys, and what that number is counting

This one carries the methods watch, so here is only the study. Psychology of Men and Masculinities, from Erica Scharrer at Massachusetts Amherst with Monique Ward and Brenda Volling at Michigan. Six hundred and one American boys aged thirteen to seventeen, recruited through commercial online panels, answered privately on their own phones. A third said they follow Andrew Tate at least a little, and about one in nine follow Jordan Peterson. Boys who named a figure of that kind among their favourites scored higher on traditional masculinity beliefs, and following Tate survived the controls. The authors note that endorsing masculine norms is not the same as hostility towards women, which they did not measure.

# Six. Diverse samples, converging interpretations

The last item is a comment published yesterday in Nature Reviews Psychology by Yusuke Moriguchi at Kyoto, Yasuhiro Kanakogi at Osaka and Taro Murakami at Tokoha University. Their argument is that psychology's long campaign to widen its samples beyond the usual Western undergraduates may be undercut from a direction nobody was watching. Generative writing tools are used most heavily by researchers outside English-speaking institutions, which is to say by exactly the people whose samples are broadening the evidence base, and those tools pull wording, framing and interpretation towards a narrow centre. Sampling diversity, they conclude, will not broaden behavioural science if interpretive diversity is lost.

Two honest caveats. Only the abstract is readable without a subscription, so I am reporting the claim rather than the case for it. And it is an argument, not a result. The empirical work it leans on is real, though, including a study showing that writing assistants push prose towards Western styles.

# The methods watch, from the statistics and methods course, is about what a prevalence headline is counting

The fifth item is the case, and the point is not that the study is weak. It is careful, it reports its own competing predictor honestly, and the lead author says plainly that a single-wave survey cannot show causation. The problem is what happens to a number on the way to a headline.

First, the threshold makes the figure. One in three is the proportion of boys who said they follow Andrew Tate at least a little, which is to say every boy who did not answer never. The same study contains a second measure of the same thing: asked instead to name their favourite social media figures, with no names supplied, about nine per cent produced someone from the researchers' list. One sample, two measurements, a factor of nearly four between them, and the whole of the difference is the question. Neither number is wrong. Only one of them is a headline, and it is the one with the lowest bar.

Second, a quota-matched panel is not a probability sample. These boys came from commercial online panels balanced to match national proportions on age, race and region. That fixes the sample's composition on three variables and leaves untouched everything else that decides which families join a panel and answer a survey about masculinity. A quota-matched panel supports the sentence, a third of the boys in this sample. It does not support, a third of American teenage boys. That is the difference between a quantity with a sampling distribution and one without.

Third, the variable in the headline is not the variable in the model. Following Tate did predict traditional masculinity beliefs with the controls in place, and that is a real result. But in every model the strongest predictor was what the boy's parents had told him about being big and tough, carrying two to three times the weight. The study's best single predictor of how a boy thinks about manhood is his parents. That is a less arresting sentence, which is why it is not the one that travelled.

And since this is a single wave, a boy who already held rigid views and a boy who caught them from an influencer look identical in the data.

This extends yesterday's case, where the finding a study reported was not the finding it was built to test. A headline names the variable that is interesting, not the variable that is largest, and the two are rarely the same. Three habits, then. Before quoting a prevalence figure, ask what counted as a case. Ask whether the sample was drawn or assembled. And when a predictor is named in a headline, look for the coefficient it was competing against.
