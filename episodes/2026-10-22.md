# The concept of the day comes from Foundations of Behavioural Science, and it is Ego, the element of MINDSPACE that explains why a good argument can push someone backwards.

MINDSPACE is normally taught as a checklist of nine levers, and Ego usually gets the shortest treatment of the nine: people act in ways that support a flattering view of themselves. Today's first item shows what that element is for.

The obvious way to design a message against meat eating is to make the strongest case, and moral framing is the strongest case available. Isaac Chung and colleagues show that strength of argument is the wrong design criterion, because the person receiving a message is not only evaluating it. They are also managing what it implies about them.

Compare two criticisms carrying the same request. One says you do not care about the environment and you lack moral decency. The other says you do care, but you lack the cooking knowledge. Only the first requires the listener to revise their opinion of themselves in order to comply, and the finding is that they defend the opinion instead. They rate the critic's motives as worse, they derogate the critic, they plan to avoid them, and their stated intention to eat less meat does not move.

The mediation result is what turns this from a mood into a mechanism. The defensiveness ran through the feeling of being morally devalued, and people who felt more morally judged reported weaker intentions to cut back. There is a subtler finding underneath. Once that feeling of devaluation was statistically accounted for, the moral critique was associated with slightly stronger intentions. So the argument works. The threat to self-image bundled in with it cancels the argument out and then some. That is the practical content of Ego, and it is why behavioural policy on diet, smoking, energy use and debt is built around defaults, convenience and norms rather than moral exhortation, even where the moral case is strong.

Three questions to put to any behaviour change message. What does complying require the person to concede about themselves, and is there a framing that asks the same of them at a lower cost to self-image. Has the evaluation measured defensiveness as well as intention, since one message can raise both. And what exactly was the outcome: here it was stated intention about an imagined exchange, which makes this a mechanism rather than a policy result.

# One. A moral critique of what somebody eats produces defensiveness, not a change of diet.

That was the concept of the day, so the study itself can be brief. Two preregistered experiments in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, out this week, on British meat eaters who use social media. Three hundred in the first, assigned at random to a moral or a competence critique of a food photograph they had chosen to post. Three hundred more in the second, all reading the identical moral critique, half imagining it as a public comment and half as a private message. The twist worth keeping is that the private version produced more offence, more derogation and more retaliation than the public one, which is the opposite of the intuition. The authors suggest that a private message from a stranger reads as deliberate rather than performative. They say plainly that they did not test that.

# Two. A pay rise is followed by small changes in personality, and those changes are followed by a pay rise.

Psychological Bulletin, this week. Wen-Dong Li and colleagues pooled the raw individual data from eleven national panel studies in the United States, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway and Australia, covering up to a hundred and thirty-five thousand working adults over spans of four to twenty years. Rises in income were followed by small increases in conscientiousness and emotional stability, and by smaller decreases in agreeableness and extraversion. Openness showed nothing. Running the other way, rises in conscientiousness and emotional stability predicted later income gains. A formal comparison found the two directions not detectably different in size, which is the paper's main theoretical claim: this is reciprocal, not one-way. Both effects traced an inverted U over time, peaking at a lag of roughly fifteen months and then fading across the following decade.

That lag is the most interesting result and also the most fragile. No contributing study measured the same people less than a year apart, so the rise up to the peak is produced by the model's assumed shape rather than observed. And none of the limitations in the coverage are attributed to the authors, which is becoming a recurring note in this brief.

# Three. The famous low road to the amygdala turns out to be several roads, and not the famous one.

NeuroImage. Diffusion imaging and tractography in a hundred and thirteen healthy adults, twenty-four of them rescanned a fortnight later to check reproducibility. The target is the rapid subcortical route from thalamus to amygdala, the one introduced in first-year teaching as the reason a startle arrives before recognition does. It reconstructed not as one route but as a family of them. The most reliable ran from the inferior colliculus through the medial geniculate nucleus to the amygdala, and appeared in nearly everybody. The negative result is the one to notice. The direct route from the inferior colliculus straight to the amygdala, bypassing the geniculate, which is the shortcut the standard story leans on hardest, could not be reliably reconstructed at all.

The authors caution that one prominent connection may carry signals away from the amygdala rather than towards it, and that a left-sided asymmetry may be an artefact of method. The caveat that matters most is unattributed: tractography both invents pathways and misses them, particularly where fibres cross, so none of this is settled until it is confirmed in tissue.

# Four. Petting the dog did not speed recovery from stress, and seemed to change the next stressor instead.

This one carries the methods watch, so the description can stay short. Biological Psychology. Sixty-four dog owners in a laboratory in Innsbruck, each doing two blocks: a stress test of rapid questions and backward counting under hostile video monitoring, then a five-minute recovery period with their own dog, with the dog either free to be petted or held on a short leash out of reach. Brain activity, heart rate and heart rate variability throughout. The preregistered prediction was that touch would speed recovery. It did not. What the authors report instead came out of the second stress test, and out of an analysis they had not planned.

# Five. A weekend with a simulated infant changed the way testosterone responded to looking after it.

Human Nature. Thirty couples aged twenty to forty, living together, planning children within five years, and with no parenting experience. On the Friday each pair was trained, named a programmable infant simulator and did a ten-minute caregiving task. They took it home for the weekend, half with a version that woke crying several times a night, and on the Monday repeated the identical task at the same hour. Saliva testosterone before and after each one.

On the Friday the task did essentially nothing. About a one per cent change, not distinguishable from zero. On the Monday the same task produced a drop of around ten per cent, in men and women alike. The sleep disruption made no difference. Neither performance nor reported stress nor reported enjoyment explained the shift. What did track it was how comfortable people had been around babies beforehand, with the least comfortable falling most steeply.

The authors note the sample is small and that nothing follows yet about real infants. The caveat they do not state, and which the coverage supplies without attribution, is the one that bites: there was no comparison group who did the two laboratory sessions without a weekend in between, so familiarity with the task and the room is not separated from the doll.

# Six. Held over from Monday, people who score higher on cognitive tests rate their own looks slightly lower.

Evolutionary Psychology. Adrian Furnham and David Semmelink had people rate their own physical attractiveness on a single scale from nought to a hundred, averaging around fifty-eight. Eight hundred working-age adults in the first study, drawn from people who had completed workplace assessments through a commercial test publisher, and just under three hundred in the second, with a timed reasoning and spatial test added. Men rated themselves three to four points above women, and graduates about the same margin above non-graduates. In the second study, religiosity stopped predicting self-rated looks once intelligence was entered. Cognitive ability itself was negatively associated with self-rated looks, and added about one per cent to explained variance.

Furnham's own caveats are the right ones. Nothing here is a large effect, though the findings replicated across the two studies, and an intriguing nationality effect, with the British rating themselves lowest, rests on groups too different in size to test properly. Two things the coverage does not raise. There were no observer ratings at all, so this is a study of self-perception throughout. And the second author works for the test publisher whose assessment pool supplied the first study.

# And the methods watch, from the statistics and methods course. What to do when the preregistered prediction fails and the headline comes from the analysis nobody planned.

The dog study is well designed, and the finding it reports is not the finding it was built to produce. Those two facts need separating, because the honest conclusion is neither that it found nothing nor that it found what the headline says.

The preregistered prediction was directional and specific: touching the dog would speed physiological recovery. Outcome, comparison and direction were fixed before any data existed. It failed. Both conditions returned towards baseline at the same rate. That is a result, and it is the one the study is entitled to state with full confidence.

What is being reported instead comes from the second stress test, where the people who had the touch condition first did better on three measures, with effect sizes running from about a third to two-thirds of a standard deviation. Those are not trivial. But the analysis was unplanned, which changes what the numbers mean. Exploratory analysis is not improper, and the authors say outright that this needs confirming in a study designed to test it. The difficulty is that the probability attached to a result depends on how many ways the data could have been examined before it was found. A preregistered test has one way. This design has many: two conditions, two sessions, three physiological measures, self-report, and order. The interval printed beside an exploratory estimate is calculated as though only one comparison had been possible, so it is too narrow, by an amount nobody can recover afterwards.

There is a sharper point here. The finding is specifically about the people who did the touch condition first, which makes it an order effect, and neutralising order effects is precisely what a within-subject design is for. Two readings fit. Touch may genuinely have altered how the second stressor was met, which is the authors' reading and a real claim about carry-over. Or the two orders differ for reasons with nothing to do with the dog, because once order becomes the grouping variable the comparison is between people rather than within them.

This is the same shape as Tuesday's case, where an overall null was followed by a claim that effects varied by department. The habit to build is simple: find what the study set out to test, and read that result first.
