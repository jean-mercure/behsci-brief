# The concept of the day, what a randomised trial actually identifies

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the working paper at item two is the occasion for it.

Start with what randomisation buys you. Assigning treatment by chance makes the treated and untreated groups exchangeable in expectation, so a difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment rather than to whatever else distinguished the people who got it. That is internal validity, and it is a genuine achievement. The canonical case in the discrimination literature is the correspondence study: send matched job applications differing only in the name at the top, and because qualifications are held fixed by design, any difference in callbacks is attributable to the name.

Now the harder point. A randomised experiment identifies the effect of what was actually manipulated, and nothing else. A correspondence study randomises the signal of race on a single application. It does not randomise race, and it certainly does not randomise the institutional arrangements that constitute systemic discrimination: school funding formulae, credit scoring, residential sorting, inherited wealth. Those are not assignable to individuals at all, which is not a practical difficulty to be engineered around but a conceptual one.

The second limit is interference. Experiments assume one person's treatment does not affect another person's outcome. For systemic processes that assumption fails directly. If an employer's behaviour depends on the composition of the applicant pool, then changing some applications changes the environment facing the rest, and the control group is contaminated by the treatment.

The third is that internal validity is silent on external validity. An experiment can be flawlessly executed and still tell you nothing about any population other than the one it ran on.

So the question to put to any experiment, before asking whether it was well conducted, is what quantity it was capable of estimating in the first place. The authors propose the honest alternatives: manipulate the salience of systemic forces rather than the forces themselves, use policy variation as a natural experiment, and study how people anticipate discrimination and act on that anticipation.

# One. Attending to an allergic reaction appears to dampen it

In Nature Human Behaviour, reported yesterday, Shira Mizrachi, Nancy Agmon-Levin Rottem and Ruth Rozenkrantz at Bar-Ilan University ran three preregistered experiments on a total of about seventy-five adults, mostly young women. Each participant had a histamine skin prick on the forearm, then spent twenty minutes either attending inwardly to the itching and burning, or distracted by video.

Under internal attention the swelling measured three and a half millimetres against five millimetres when distracted, the redness about eleven millimetres against fourteen, and the reaction subsided faster. The pattern held in roughly nine in ten participants. The second experiment matched the visual input across conditions, and the third applied a local anaesthetic, where the effect persisted, suggesting peripheral sensory feedback is not necessary for it.

The limitations are serious and the authors state them. The samples are very small, the participants healthy young adults, the model a single acute allergy-like reaction. No immune cells or signalling molecules were measured, so the mechanism is inferred rather than observed, and the heart-rate measure offered as evidence of vagal involvement is an indirect proxy. The anaesthetic session was not counterbalanced against the others. The lead author cautions explicitly against the reading that one can think away inflammation.

# Two. What randomised trials can and cannot establish about systemic discrimination

This is the paper behind the concept of the day. Working paper thirty-five thousand eight hundred and eighty-two, from Dania Francis, Rob Gillezeau, Damon Jones and Angelino Viceisza. It is a conceptual essay with no empirical data: it builds a model of systemic racial discrimination from existing definitions, then works through seven areas where experiments might bear on it. The conclusion is that randomised trials remain the best instrument available for direct discrimination and are structurally ill-suited to the systemic variety.

# Three. Boredom raises the appetite for stimulation, and stimulation can take an aggressive form

In Personality and Individual Differences, reported yesterday, Heng Li at Sichuan International Studies University reports three studies. A survey of about three hundred and seventy Chinese and Vietnamese students related boredom proneness to hostility. A laboratory experiment on a hundred and twenty-four adults randomised people to twenty-five minutes of a man folding laundry or twenty-five minutes of a park documentary, then measured sensation seeking and had them punch a bag, with force and apparent comfort scored by independent raters. A third experiment on two hundred and seventy-seven Americans used a boring video, then asked about support for military action in a hypothetical commander-in-chief scenario.

Sensation seeking mediated the effect in both experiments, which is the author's proposed mechanism: boredom is not read here as a cause of hostility but as a state that raises the appetite for stimulation, with aggression being one of the things that happens to supply it.

The crucial caveat is that the mediator was measured and not manipulated, so the causal chain is modelled rather than tested. The outcomes are a laboratory proxy and a hypothetical, no effect sizes appear in the accessible report, and baseline self-control was not assessed. The author notes, reasonably, that boredom is usually discharged harmlessly.

# Four. Maternal depression predicts special educational needs, and the prescription adds little

In PLOS Medicine, reported yesterday, a team led from Swansea and Glasgow followed a hundred and sixty-seven thousand children born in Wales between two thousand and nine and two thousand and sixteen, linking primary care, maternity and school records. Recorded maternal depression was associated with higher rates of special educational needs. An antidepressant prescription added a small further increase. Crucially, rates of autism and attention deficit disorder did not differ significantly between the children of treated and untreated depressed mothers, and learning and behavioural difficulties differed by about one extra case per hundred children. This paper carries today's methods watch, so I will come back to it.

# Five. Scientists who expect technology to fix the climate act on it less

From the LSE department, published last week, with Fabian Dablander as lead author and the paper in Environmental Research Letters. More than nine thousand scientists in a hundred and fifteen countries were asked whether technology will largely solve the problems caused by climate change. About a quarter agreed and a little over four in ten disagreed.

Those who agreed were roughly a fifth less likely to take civic climate action and a little less than a fifth less likely to make high-impact lifestyle changes. Petition signing, protest, advocacy, flying less, driving less, eating less meat: all lower among the techno-optimists. The detail that makes the pattern interpretable is where the difference vanished. On technology-oriented actions, buying an electric vehicle or installing home renewables, the two groups were indistinguishable. So this is not a general difference in engagement; it is specific to the collective and the sacrificial.

The study is cross-sectional self-report, so belief and conduct cannot be ordered in time, and self-reported climate action is exposed to social desirability.

# Six. Long-term single adults' life satisfaction proved stable over eleven years

In Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, Elaine Hoan, Geoff MacDonald and Matthew Johnson used a German panel study to find people who were never married and unpartnered at every one of eleven consecutive annual surveys, about two thousand nine hundred of them across three birth cohorts.

About nine in ten of each cohort stayed above the midpoint of the life-satisfaction scale throughout, with a slight dip in the younger groups. A low-starting minority improved among the younger cohorts but not the middle-aged.

The selection is the thing to notice. Requiring eleven consecutive waves of continuous singlehood qualified two thirds of the youngest cohort but only one in seven of the middle-aged, so the older group is a far more unusual set of people than the younger one. Outcomes were single items, the data are German only, and the oldest participants are still only in their late forties.

# Seven. An LSE project on curiosity as a lever against information avoidance

Also from the department, last week: Yana Litovsky was runner-up in the Spark Award at the LSE Impact Prize for work on curiosity interventions to counter information avoidance. Laboratory experiments are reported to show that brief curiosity-arousing prompts reduce avoidance of useful health information, cancer risk findings and blood sugar warnings among them, and shift subsequent choices, including towards sugar-free drinks. The prize funds a field study on live health platforms, with possible extension to finance and the environment.

This is a departmental announcement rather than a peer-reviewed report, so the laboratory evidence cannot be appraised from it. The idea is still worth holding onto, because of where it intervenes. Most attempts to counter information avoidance either supply more information or make it cheaper to obtain. This one manipulates neither, only the anticipated reward for acquiring it.

# The methods watch, confounding by indication

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the Welsh cohort at item four is the case.

Suppose you want to know whether antidepressants taken in pregnancy harm the child. The naive comparison, treated mothers against all untreated mothers, is hopeless, because the reason for treatment is depression, and depression is itself a cause of the outcome and is partly heritable. Any difference you find is confounded by the indication for the drug. This is confounding by indication, and it is pervasive wherever treatment is allocated by need.

The design response is the active comparator: compare treated depressed mothers with untreated depressed mothers. That is what lets this paper say anything at all, and it is why its central finding, a null on autism and attention deficit disorder, is worth attending to.

Three residual cautions. First, severity of depression was not recorded, and untreated mothers are likely to have been less severely affected. So residual confounding runs against treatment, which makes a null more credible rather than less. Notice what has happened there: the bias has not merely been named, it has been signed, and knowing its direction turns a weakness into an argument. Second, exposure is a prescription record, which does not confirm the medicine was taken, and the antidepressant group will contain prescriptions for migraine and chronic pain. Misclassification in both directions attenuates towards no difference. Third, special educational needs is an administrative category that reflects local assessment practice as much as the child.

And the standing caution: a non-significant difference is not evidence of no effect. A hundred and sixty-seven thousand children is well powered against large effects and much less so against the clinically small ones.
