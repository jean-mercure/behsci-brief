# The concept of the day is triangulation, and what it cannot remove.

This one belongs to the statistics and methods course. Almost everything you are taught about research design is a catalogue of what a design cannot rule out. An observational association cannot rule out reverse causation. Triangulation is the response. You answer one question with two or more designs chosen so that their assumptions fail in different directions, and then ask which conclusions survive in all of them. That is not replication, which runs the same design twice and repeats the same blind spot twice.

Today's lead item does it properly. The first design exploits the fact that a very large cohort contains relatives of differing genetic closeness, which lets shared genes be modelled rather than assumed away. It pays for that with assumptions about who marries whom and about relatives sharing environments equally. The second design is adoption. Genetic transmission from the rearing parent to the child is broken outright, which is a far stronger guarantee, and it is bought with a sample of about five hundred and sixty families, all screened and approved to adopt, and therefore unusual.

Neither is the better design in the abstract. The first could be wrong because its genetic model is wrong, the second because its families are unusual, and those are not the same error. The direction of effect held in both, and that is the claim to take seriously. The finding about warm parenting held in only one.

Now the sharper lesson, which is about what triangulation leaves alone. It removes the biases that differ between your designs and keeps every bias they share. Both samples here rested largely on mothers reporting on themselves and on their own children. The authors' own check is the tell: ask the father about the parenting and the mother about the child, and the associations vanish. Two designs with different genetic assumptions and one common measurement problem will agree with each other about the measurement problem.

So, the questions to carry away. What does each design assume, and what does it buy. Which conclusions survived every design, and which survived only one. And the one that matters most: could both studies be wrong in the same way.

# One. Aggressive children appear to produce inconsistent discipline, rather than the other way round.

This is the lead item, reported on Thursday in Nature Communications, by Joanna Bright and colleagues at King's College London. The design is the concept of the day, so the study itself can be brief. Fifty-three and a half thousand Norwegian children, with parents reporting at age five, set against five hundred and sixty-one American adoption triads assessed between four and eight years old. In both, aggression predicted later inconsistent discipline and not the reverse. Roughly half the modest link between inconsistent discipline and aggression in the Norwegian sample was down to genes shared by parent and child. The authors note narrow measures, mostly mothers' reports, and relatively advantaged families.

# Two. A fortnight of feeling forgiven, measured from the day of the act.

Richard Cowden at Harvard and Kaye Cook at Gordon College, in Psychology of Religion and Spirituality, reported on Thursday. They screened almost two and a half thousand practising American Christians and enrolled the hundred and seventy-five who had done something they considered sinful in the previous day and felt no assurance it had been forgiven. Those people were surveyed on days one, two, four, eight and sixteen. Both repentance and a sense of absolution rose along a logarithmic curve, quickly at first and then flattening in the second week. Seeing God as benevolent, and a general disposition to feel forgiven, made no difference at all to the rate of change, which is the more interesting null: traits measured once did not predict the shape of a fortnight. Then two design points. Anchoring to the incident is what makes the time axis mean anything, because each person's day one is their own. The screening is the price: everyone began unforgiven by construction, so the curve describes recovery from a selected starting point. The authors call the work observational and suggestive. Those who finished all five surveys were the more devout, so attrition was not random.

# Three. Six-year-olds and adults talking to a chatbot, with different control networks engaged.

Tzipi Horowitz-Kraus and colleagues at the Technion, in Cerebral Cortex, reported on Thursday. Fifteen first-graders, average age about six and a half, and sixteen adults held a five-minute spoken conversation with ChatGPT inside a scanner. The adults engaged their control, salience and dorsal attention networks more strongly than the children did. The clearest difference was between networks rather than within them: coupling between the control network and the dorsal attention network was about three times stronger in adults. The authors read this as adults filtering and steering what the model says in a way young children do not yet. Now the candour. The lead author states that there is no comparison condition, so an age difference cannot be separated from anything specific to artificial intelligence, and she says plainly that the study does not show ChatGPT reduces children's cognitive control or harms their brains. One further detail the coverage underplays. The children were told they would be speaking to the smartest computer in the world. The adults were told only that they would be speaking to ChatGPT. That is a difference in instructions, not only in age.

# Four. Higher progesterone goes with less distinct neural patterns for emotional control.

Macia Buades-Rotger at Barcelona and colleagues, in Psychological Medicine, reported on Thursday. A hundred and sixteen naturally cycling young women gave a saliva sample, then did a task approaching and avoiding happy and angry faces, half the trials requiring the instinctive response and half the opposite. Blurrier has a precise meaning here. A classifier trained on the odd-numbered trials was asked which of four conditions each even-numbered trial belonged to, against a chance rate of one in four. Higher progesterone went with poorer classification in the lateral frontal pole and the striatum, correlations of about minus a quarter. Sharper frontal patterns went with better mood over the preceding week, and a preregistered mediation model found a small path from progesterone through pattern distinctness to mood, worth about seven per cent of the variation. Oestradiol was unrelated to anything. Now the candour. A hundred and one women with complete data against a preregistered target of a hundred and twenty, leaving about seventy per cent power. No manipulation of progesterone, so no causal claim. And one sample and one scan each, so a passing hormonal state cannot be told apart from a stable trait.

# Five. Anger does not mobilise everybody, and not into the same acts.

Sara Morell and Marzia Oceno, in Public Opinion Quarterly, reported on Thursday. A survey of about six and a half thousand American adults around the twenty-twenty presidential election, plus a panel of three and a half thousand measured after that election and again after the midterms. A single general item, how angry or irritated you feel about how things are going in the country, was related to electoral acts such as donating or campaigning, and to non-electoral acts such as protesting, petitioning and boycotting. Anger predicted more campaign involvement among white men and among both white and Black women, but not among Black men. Black women reported the highest average anger and the largest rise in engagement, and were the only group for whom anger predicted attending a protest in both waves. The authors note that a general anger measure cannot say which issues produced the anger, and suspect it has to combine with a sense of shared fate or a direct personal stake before it turns into action. The weakness is worth naming: a single emotion item, self-reported behaviour in the same questionnaire, and no coefficients anywhere in the coverage.

# Six. A blood marker said to predict who responds to an antidepressant.

Molecular Psychiatry, on Thursday, from Grigorios Papageorgiou and colleagues. Baseline levels of a circular RNA abundant in the brain were higher in patients with major depression who had not responded to sertraline after eight weeks than in those who had. Responders' levels rose during treatment and non-responders' did not. Baseline levels did not predict response to a placebo. What the study as reported does not contain is any figure for how well it sorts an individual patient, which is today's methods watch, so I will come back to it. Three of the authors, including the first and the last, work for a company developing tests of this kind.

# Seven. The template that guides visual search is often deliberately inaccurate.

Nancy Carlisle and Geoffrey Woodman, in Nature Reviews Psychology, yesterday. A review arguing that the textbook account of visual search, in which you hold an accurate picture of the target in working memory and attention is drawn to whatever matches it, is incomplete in four ways. Long-term memory often does the guiding. The guiding representation is frequently not accurate, because a deliberately shifted template works better when target and distractors resemble one another. Attention is steered away from distractors as well as towards targets. And what you have searched for before shapes guidance regardless of intention. Only the abstract was readable here, the body being behind a subscription wall. But the distinction is worth carrying into an essay for the foundations of psychological science. Attention is guided by a template, and attention is guided by an accurate template, are two different claims.

# And the methods watch. A prediction claim with no prediction metric.

First, what is solid in the circular RNA paper. The molecule is a sensible candidate, the mouse and cell work gives it a mechanism, the authors disclose their commercial interest and volunteer the group in whom the marker fails. The problem is that the claim being made is a claim about individuals, and the study as reported contains no number describing how well individuals are sorted.

A difference between group averages is not a test. Non-responders had higher baseline levels: that compares two distributions defined after the outcome was already known. Two such distributions can differ with real statistical reliability and still overlap so heavily that no cut-off anywhere separates them usefully. To get from a group difference to a test you have to pick a threshold and then say what happens at it. Sensitivity. Specificity. The area under the curve. And above all the predictive values, which depend on how common non-response actually is. Nothing in the reporting rules out a marker that is right only three times in five, because no figure of any kind is given.

Two further things to look for. First, whether the rule ever met data it had not already learned from. Baseline samples came from two clinical trials, but nothing says a model was fitted in one dataset and then scored, untouched, in another. A rule tuned and graded on the same data will always flatter itself. Second, the exclusion that improved the accuracy. Dropping patients with a family history of mania may well be the right clinical decision, but it was taken after seeing that the marker did badly in them, and a choice made with the answer in view has to be specified in advance and then tested afresh before the improvement means anything.

This pairs with yesterday's case, where a headline used the lowest available prevalence threshold. The generalisation holds in both: the number a headline leaves out is usually the number that would settle the question. So, three habits. When a study says a test predicts, find the figure saying how often it is right, and treat its absence as a finding about the report. Ask whether the model has ever seen unfamiliar data. And notice who is reporting the accuracy, not because a commercial interest invalidates a result, but because it tells you who has not yet had a chance to check it.
