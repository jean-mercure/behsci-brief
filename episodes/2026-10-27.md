# The concept of the day comes from the statistics and methods course, and it is the cell that was never run.

An experiment earns its right to talk about causes from random assignment. Participants go into conditions by chance, so the only systematic difference between conditions is the thing the experimenter changed. A factorial design crosses two or more of those changes and asks whether one depends on the other. What looks similar, and is not, is a manipulated factor crossed with something merely measured about the participant. There the interaction is a correlation even when the main effect is causal, because nobody assigns anyone their politics.

Today's first item shows it cleanly. Rather more than six thousand Americans were randomly assigned to read either a neutral description of a scientific journal, or that description followed by a real editorial endorsing a presidential candidate. Trust in science fell. Perceived impartiality fell. That part is an experiment, and the causal claim is secure.

But the headline is the interaction. The loss of trust was larger among moderates and conservatives. Political outlook was measured, not assigned, so that part is correlational. And underneath it sits something sharper. Every editorial used in the study endorsed a Democrat or criticised a Republican. The design therefore holds one cell of a two-by-two and not the other, because the direction of the endorsement was never varied. Two quite different claims fit the data equally well. Either conservatives are more sensitive to scientists taking political sides, or everybody dislikes seeing the other side endorsed and the conservatives were the only people shown one. Those are different findings, with different implications for how journals ought to behave, and this design cannot tell them apart.

Three points follow. A main effect and an interaction in one paper can rest on quite different evidence. A missing cell is not a statistical problem; no adjustment recovers a condition nobody ran, and the remedy is design. And this one was bought with something valuable, because real editorials have an authenticity no synthetic version could match, and exist in only one direction. Realism purchased the confound.

So the question to carry away. When a study tells you an effect was stronger in one group, ask whether that group was assigned or merely measured, and whether every group saw the same version of the manipulation.

# One. Scientific journals endorsed a candidate, and trust in science fell.

The numbers and the design are in the concept of the day, so this item is confined to what else the study found. Three experiments were run, two around the twenty-twenty American election and one around twenty-twenty-four, using real editorials from Science, The Lancet, Nature and The Economist. Alongside general trust, they measured perceived impartiality, good intentions and competence. Impartiality fell in all three experiments; the other two fell in some. But stated willingness to follow practical health or safety guidance did not move. So the damage registered in how people regard science as an institution rather than in what they said they would do about a health instruction, and that distinction is worth keeping. The paper is in Group Processes and Intergroup Relations.

# Two. Anger makes a dominant face more appealing, and fear makes a trustworthy one.

Just under two hundred American adults, split evenly between Democrats and Republicans, picked from a list of sixteen political issues the one that made them angriest and the one that frightened them most. For each, they made thirty-six forced choices between pairs of computer-generated faces varying in how dominant and how trustworthy they looked. Angry participants chose the more dominant face about half the time, against about forty-six per cent when frightened, a gap of some three percentage points. The more interesting result sits inside each condition. Under fear, looking trustworthy helped a face and looking dominant did nothing measurable. Under anger, the trustworthiness advantage shrank and dominance gained ground. Two cautions. How intensely someone felt the emotion was unrelated to what they chose, which is awkward for any account in which the emotion drives the preference. And party told you more than emotion did: Republicans chose dominant faces about fifty-three per cent of the time against roughly forty-four for Democrats, a nine-point gap, three times the emotional effect. The emotion was also not induced. Participants chose their own issues, so the feeling is tangled up with the subject that produced it, and there was no neutral condition. Not one of those cautions is attributed to the authors. Cognition and Emotion, the day before yesterday.

# Three. Why some people enjoy being frightened.

Two studies, the first on more than eight hundred people and the second on four hundred and twenty-five Canadian undergraduates, had participants watch a three-minute rollercoaster clip and three minutes of a horror video game in random order, then rate nine frightened and nine excited words. People scoring higher on a long self-report psychopathy questionnaire reported more positive reactions to the horror clip, with correlations around three-tenths, and slightly less negative ones, with correlations under one-tenth. The proposed explanation was a belief in personal invincibility, measured by asking people their chances of walking away unharmed from twenty dangerous situations. It partly explained the reduced fear. It did not explain the enjoyment in the first study, and in the second, where it accounted for a little of both, the authors call the effects small and borderline. A rival explanation, boredom proneness, explained nothing. Note what the measure is not. There is no single score for enjoying fear; it is inferred from two separate word ratings. The sample was students and online workers, so nothing here speaks to forensic populations, and three minutes of a video game is not danger. The authors concede one result may be a fluke. The rest, including the absence of any prediction recorded in advance, comes from the journalist. Psychological Reports.

# Four. Political beliefs barely moved, and the brain claim is doing more work than the data.

A preregistered Polish study scanned forty-six politically engaged adults, all strongly left-wing, while they read sixteen statements, half political and half not, each followed by five counterarguments, and rated their belief afterwards. The behavioural result is in the methods watch, because the interpretation of the brain data is today's caution. The authors' own honesty deserves a mention here. Five limitations are attributed to them directly, including that no comparison with right-wing participants was made, so nothing here shows the political left is more stubborn than anybody else, and that neuroimaging does not directly display a psychological process. That is the best-attributed page of the run, and a credit to the researchers. Scientific Reports.

# Five. Thirty-six risk genes, and the number that is missing.

Sequencing the protein-coding DNA of nearly four thousand people with obsessive-compulsive disorder, chronic tic disorders or both, against roughly four thousand controls, found thirty-six genes in which rare damaging mutations raise risk, thirty of them with support in both conditions. Carriers had on average fifty-seven times the odds, and for some individual genes more than two hundred times. Keep those multiples next to their denominator. Each disorder affects one or two people in a hundred. Only about seven or eight per cent of patients in the family samples carried a contributing new mutation, and about three per cent of the unrelated patients a rare disruptive inherited one. Hardly anyone carries a mutation in any one of these genes. So a very large odds ratio sits on a very small number of people, and the paper reports no absolute risk for a carrier at all. Two further points. The thirty-six is the union of three analyses, each at a false discovery threshold of one in ten, rather than one test, and the authors project hundreds more such genes exist. And there was no independent replication; the cohorts were pooled. Four genes do match earlier work, which is reassuring without being replication. Nature Neuroscience.

# Six. Attachment, the fear of death, and what death is taken to be.

Six hundred and forty Italians between eighteen and twenty-two, over four-fifths of them women, completed measures of adult attachment style, death anxiety, whether they conceive of death as a passage or as annihilation, and four kinds of internalising symptom. With all four attachment styles considered together, a preoccupied style went with more death anxiety and a dismissing style with less; secure and fearful styles were unrelated. The interesting part is the moderator. For anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and bodily symptoms, death anxiety was tied to symptoms only among those who see death as annihilation, and the indirect path from preoccupied attachment ran through that group alone. For depression, the conception of death made no difference and the path held either way. The design is one questionnaire at one moment, which the lead author says plainly, and the authors add that mediation estimated at a single time point can overstate an indirect path, that the effects were modest, that seeing death as a passage is not simply a proxy for religiosity, and that a dismissing style should not be read as healthy. Six limitations from the authors themselves. Scientific Reports.

# The methods watch is a caution about reading psychology off a brain image, and it is called reverse inference.

Reverse inference means reasoning backwards from where activity appeared in the brain to the psychological process that must have produced it. The Polish belief-change study is a textbook case. Political challenges produced more activity than non-political ones in the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate, which the authors read as identity-related self-reflection.

The argument runs: this region is active during self-referential thinking; this region is active here; therefore self-referential thinking is going on. The problem, named by Russell Poldrack two decades ago, is that the inference needs the region to be active more or less only during that process. Those two are among the least selective regions in the brain. They are active during thinking about oneself, remembering one's past, imagining the future, retrieving ordinary facts, reasoning about others, and lying still doing nothing. What the imaging literature gives you is the chance of activation given the process. What the inference needs is the chance of the process given the activation, and for a region that does many things, that is low.

An outside commentator said so. Paul Zak of Claremont Graduate University, not involved in the study, named the problem directly and called the interpretation thin and over-generous. The authors are more careful than the headline, the lead author saying identity protection is not the only possible explanation.

Two further things. The strongest result in the paper needed no scanner at all. Non-political beliefs fell by a standardised difference of about one and one-tenth, and the shift was still there a week later. Political beliefs moved by six-hundredths, which is nothing, and a week later had drifted very slightly upwards. And the authors' own caution cuts at it from another angle: the political statements were value claims and the non-political ones testable facts, so the dissociation may be about the kind of proposition rather than about politics. Add that the behavioural sample was forty-three, the imaging sample is not separately stated, and nothing is said about correcting for the many comparisons imaging makes.

Three habits. When a paper tells you what a brain region reflects, ask what else that region does. Where a study has both a behavioural and an imaging result, let the behavioural one carry the claim. And check that the comparison the headline implies was actually run.
