# The concept of the day, the gap between the two sides of a silence

This belongs to Foundations of Psychological Science, and the paper at item four is the occasion for it.

Start with the mechanism, which is egocentric anchoring. When we judge what is going on in someone else's mind, we begin from our own state and adjust outwards, and the adjustment is almost always too small. The course meets this under three names that turn out to be one thing. The false consensus effect: we overestimate how many people share our opinion. The illusion of transparency: we think our nervousness is more visible than it is. The liking gap: after a first conversation, people reliably underestimate how much the other person enjoyed it. Three literatures, one habit of mind.

What yesterday's paper adds is an asymmetry. Consider a message that goes unanswered. The person who did not reply knows exactly why: they were busy, the moment passed, answering got harder the longer it was left. They hold a situational explanation effortlessly, because it is simply their own experience. The person left waiting has none of that. They have the silence and nothing else, and so they do what people do with a behaviour and no context, which is to infer from the behaviour to the person. That is the correspondence bias, and here it has an unusual target: in the study, about a third of those left waiting assumed they themselves had caused offence. The bias has been turned on the self.

So the gap is not a failure of sympathy but a failure of information, and the distinction matters because the two call for different remedies. If people were simply unkind you would have to change something about them. If one side is reasoning from an absence, supplying almost any context should help. That was tested. A single short line, to the effect that the sender could not talk just then, moved the share who assumed they had annoyed the other person from roughly a third down to roughly one in five. No apology, no explanation. Just enough to break the inference.

Two habits follow. When you find yourself reading meaning into someone's behaviour, ask what you would need to know about their situation to stop reading it that way, and whether you have any reason to think you know it. And when you are the one who has gone quiet, remember that your reasons are invisible, that silence is read as a message, and that the cheapest intervention available to you is about eight words long.

# One. The paracetamol signal fades as the evidence improves

In Developmental Medicine and Child Neurology yesterday, Hoda Tayebi-Hillali at the University of Santiago de Compostela and colleagues pooled seven meta-analyses covering more than three million participants, and then went behind them to reanalyse the primary studies according to how much bias each carried. This item carries today's methods watch, so briefly: the association between paracetamol in pregnancy and later autism or attention deficit disorder gets smaller at every step towards better evidence, and the best-quality estimates are small and, for autism, not statistically distinguishable from nothing at all. The authors are explicit that a synthesis of observational studies cannot establish causation by itself.

# Two. A trial of genotype-guided prescribing misses its target and hits something else

In JAMA Network Open yesterday, Kathryn Blake at Nemours Children's Health in Florida and a large team reported a randomised trial across nine American health systems. Fourteen hundred and sixty people with depression lasting three months or more were randomised either to usual care or to having their clinician told what their variants in two drug-metabolising genes implied for the choice of drug and dose. Clinicians were free to ignore the advice. The main analysis covered the six hundred and ninety-two, slightly under half, for whom the guidelines recommended a change.

At three months, the primary outcome, the two groups were indistinguishable. At six months, remission was forty-eight per cent in the genotype-guided group against thirty-nine in usual care. Read that in the right order: the trial missed its primary endpoint, and a secondary outcome appearing after a primary one has failed is a hypothesis, not a finding. Note also that nobody was blinded. Patients could see their own genetic results in their records, and the outcome is a questionnaire they fill in themselves.

# Three. The gender gap in children's risk preferences dissolves once guessing is modelled

A working paper from Cesar Chavez Padilla and James Heckman at Chicago, with Shuaizhang Feng and Zhe Yang, gave tablet-based choices between a certain payout and a gamble to two thousand two hundred fifth-graders in China, aged ten and eleven, alongside a non-verbal intelligence test and teacher ratings of personality.

A simple model reproduces the familiar result that girls are more risk-averse than boys. The authors then fit a model that does two further things: it allows risk aversion to change with the size of the stake, and it treats some choices as errors rather than preferences. Three kinds of error, in fact, distinguished by their statistical signature: momentary lapses, a persistent individual noisiness in deliberating, and outright guessing. The clue they exploit is people switching back and forth within a list where a genuine preference would switch once.

With that in place the overall gender gap disappears. What remains is a crossover: boys are the more risk-averse at small stakes, girls at the largest ones. And about thirty per cent of all choices are classified as mistakes rather than preferences, split roughly evenly between the three kinds.

Treat the thirty per cent as the finding. If nearly a third of what a preference-elicitation task records is noise, then every gap measured by such a task is partly a gap in how consistently two groups answer. Higher intelligence predicted less guessing in both sexes. The estimate depends on the model being the right one, and the data are from a single moment in these children's lives, so nothing here speaks to how preferences develop.

# Four. People who leave a message unanswered feel worse about it than the waiting party imagines

In the Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin yesterday, Sophia Li at Berkeley and Coral Zheng at Cambridge, with Juliana Schroeder, ran three studies. This item carries the concept of the day, so the headline only: in a recall study of a hundred and ninety-five people and an imagined scenario with six hundred and one, those who had gone quiet reported or expected markedly more discomfort than the waiting side predicted, and the gap was wider when the silence had been unintentional.

Then the honest part, which the authors put in the paper themselves. The third study was a live eight-minute online chat in which one partner could simply decline to return, and there the main pattern did not appear at all. Skippers felt about as bad as their partners had guessed. The original effect came back only in an unplanned analysis restricted to people who said they thought they had been ghosted. The most realistic of the three designs is the one that failed, and that should temper the whole account.

# Five. An LSE team reads a hundred and fifty-seven thousand complaint responses for signs of learning

In Safety Science, reported by the department yesterday, Kathleen Goddard, Tom Reader and Alex Gillespie of the LSE psychological and behavioural science department applied automated text analysis to a hundred and fifty-seven thousand responses written by healthcare organisations to complaints, asking whether the reply shows the organisation engaging with the complaint and whether it shows any sign of the organisation having learned something.

Engagement could be detected reliably. Learning could not. Two readings are available and the method cannot separate them: either the replies rarely contain evidence of learning, or learning is not the kind of thing that leaves a reliable trace in a letter written to close a complaint. What is being measured throughout is the document, not the organisation. That is a limit on the instrument rather than a flaw in the study, and it is worth noticing in any work that reads institutional behaviour off institutional text.

# Six. Autism's genetic profile differs more by age at diagnosis than by sex

In Behavior Genetics at the weekend, Sophie Breunig at Colorado Boulder and colleagues, including Andrew Grotzinger, applied genomic structural equation modelling to genome-wide results for four groups: autism diagnosed early, autism diagnosed late, autism in males and autism in females. The effective samples were about thirty-three thousand six hundred for early diagnosis and about twenty-one thousand eight hundred for late.

The genetic correlation between male and female autism was zero point eight one, which is to say they look much the same. Between early and late diagnosis it was zero point four two, which is to say they do not. Across sixty-eight other traits, only two differed by sex. Thirty-one differed by age at diagnosis, with late diagnosis overlapping more with depression, post-traumatic stress and attention deficit disorder, and with loneliness and dissatisfaction with friends and family.

The authors then do something careful. Much of the late-diagnosis link to attention deficit disorder turns out to run through a general distress factor: adjust for it and the overlap falls to near zero. And they state plainly that because late-diagnosed autism is genetically very similar to both the male and the female profiles, their own design cannot tell whether its psychiatric associations belong to diagnosis timing or to sex. All of this is European ancestry only, and a genetic correlation is a population-level quantity that says nothing about any individual.

# The methods watch, what it means when an estimate shrinks as the design improves

This belongs to the statistics and methods course, and the umbrella review at item one is as clean a case as you will meet.

Line up the paracetamol estimates in order of how much bias each carries. For autism: one point one seven across all the meta-analyses, one point one seven when the weakest reviews are dropped, one point one zero in the single high-confidence meta-analysis and no longer statistically significant, and one point zero five in the primary studies at lowest risk of bias. For attention deficit disorder: one point two nine, one point two eight, one point one six, one point one seven. Both series walk towards one, which is the value meaning no association at all.

That pattern is itself evidence, and it is the lesson. A real causal effect has no reason to get smaller as you remove bias from the way it is measured. A bias does exactly that, because you are removing the thing that produced it. So a monotone slide towards the null across increasing study quality behaves like an artefact and not like a cause. Set beside it the separate line of evidence from sibling comparisons, which hold family background and much of the genetics fixed by design, and which have generally not reproduced the associations that cohort studies reported.

Three cautions, so the lesson does not get overdrawn. The better estimates are also the fewer: the most rigorous numbers rest on one high-quality meta-analysis and two or three primary studies, so precision falls as credibility rises, and a small residual association is not ruled out. What remains has an obvious non-causal reading, which is confounding by indication: women take paracetamol for fevers, infections and pain, and those conditions are themselves candidates for affecting fetal development, so the exposure is not randomly distributed with respect to the outcome and no adjustment makes it so. And the review could say nothing about dose, duration or timing, and did not formally test for publication bias.

So the habit, on meeting any observational association: ask for the estimate at each level of study quality, and watch which way it moves.
