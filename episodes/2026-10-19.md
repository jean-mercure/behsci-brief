# The concept of the day comes from Foundations of Behavioural Science, and it is the messenger effect.

The MINDSPACE framework begins with the letter M, for Messenger: we are heavily influenced by who tells us something. In most teaching the examples are mild. A letter signed by a doctor works better than the same letter signed by an administrator. A reminder that appears to come from a neighbour works better than one from a council. The messenger changes how much notice we take, and the content is assumed to pass through unchanged.

Item three today gives a sharper version, because there the messenger does not merely adjust the volume. It decides whether the message arrives at all. In a hundred and seventy-five New Zealand families, hostile sexism travelled from fathers to their children, sons and daughters alike. Benevolent sexism, the patronising kind dressed up as chivalry, travelled from mothers. Fathers' benevolent sexism went nowhere. Mothers' hostile sexism reached sons only. Two parents in one household, two closely related belief systems, and four different routes.

Three things follow. First, messenger effects are specific to the message. A source who carries one thing credibly does not carry everything credibly, so the pairing of messenger to message is something a campaign has to establish by testing rather than assume by intuition, and the right pairing is often not the one the designer would have guessed.

Second, the mechanism here is probably identification rather than credibility or authority, and that distinction matters because you build the two differently. If the question is what is true, the right messenger is a recognised expert. If the question is what someone like me believes, the right messenger is someone the audience takes itself to resemble, and expertise buys almost nothing. That is a different proposition from the normative and informational distinction we did on the seventh of October, which was about why people comply, not about whom they copy.

Third, keep the size of this in proportion. The study is correlational, the association is modest, and the arrow may run partly from child to parent rather than the other way.

So, the questions to carry into any intervention that depends on a messenger. Who is the messenger. What group does the audience take that messenger to belong to. And has the design quietly confused being believed with being imitated.

# One. Intelligence tests predict job performance less well than the figure everyone quotes.

Saron Demeke, Paul Sackett and colleagues, in the Journal of Business and Psychology, reported yesterday. The team pooled three recent meta-analyses, stripped out overlapping samples and anything before nineteen ninety, and were left with two hundred and twelve samples covering about a hundred and nine thousand workers. The corrected correlation between general mental ability and supervisor-rated job performance comes out at about point two zero. The benchmark that has anchored personnel selection for forty years is point five one. This is today's methods watch, so I will come back to what those two numbers actually are and why the gap between them matters.

# Two. The cognitive half of an education gene score carries the link to children's behaviour.

Lyydia Alajaasko and colleagues at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, in Behavior Genetics, reported yesterday. Using a cohort in the northern Netherlands, the team built polygenic indices for educational attainment in about four thousand children and adolescents aged four to seventeen, and then split each index into a cognitive and a non-cognitive component.

Children with a higher index had fewer attention problems and fewer stress-related physical complaints, and almost all of that ran through the cognitive half rather than the non-cognitive half. The pattern was strongest in girls. In boys it did not hold up once the parents' own gene scores were put into the model, and that comparison is the one that separates a child's own genetic effect from the family environment the parents build around the child.

The authors are careful. They may not have had the power to detect very small non-cognitive effects. Executive function, the mechanism they propose, was never measured, so that part is speculation. The cohort is of European ancestry, from one region, and better educated and healthier than the population. Parent-reported behaviour partly measures the parent. And they say in terms that none of this supports genetic determinism, because a child's traits shape how other people treat the child.

# Three. Fathers pass on one kind of sexism and mothers pass on another.

Oksana Voznyuk and Nickola Overall at Auckland, with colleagues, in Sex Roles, reported on Saturday. A hundred and seventy-five families, each with a mother, a father and an eight-year-old. The parents filled in standard sexism questionnaires. The children judged statements about men and women spoken by a soft toy, rating each one as sort of or really right or wrong. The transmission asymmetry was the concept of the day, so I will not go over it again. Two further results are worth having. Parents' hostile sexism also tracked lower scores on the children's theory of mind. And the effects throughout are small, which the authors say themselves.

# Four. All four dark personality traits predict gossip, but they gossip for different reasons.

Adam Davis at Canadore College, with Tracy Vaillancourt and Steven Arnocky, in Behavioral Sciences, reported yesterday. Five hundred and sixteen North American adults, recruited online back in twenty twenty-one, completed measures of the dark tetrad, that is narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy and sadism, along with how often they gossip and why.

Every one of the four predicted gossiping more, with psychopathy the strongest, in a model accounting for about half the variation. The interesting part is the motives, which came apart cleanly. Gathering information was predicted only by Machiavellianism. Enjoying oneself socially was predicted by Machiavellianism and narcissism, and not at all by psychopathy or sadism. Damaging someone's reputation was predicted by psychopathy first, then sadism and Machiavellianism. Protecting the group was predicted by psychopathy and Machiavellianism.

The weaknesses are the ones you would expect, and the authors list them. One time point, so association only. Everything self-reported, and since callous people may answer unflattering questions more honestly than the rest of us, the associations could be inflated rather than real. The frequency measure lumps kind gossip in with cruel gossip. And it is a crowdsourced, mostly White, North American sample.

# Five. Breastfeeding and later attention problems, tested against genes and within families.

Berit Skretting Solberg at Bergen and colleagues, in Biological Psychiatry, reported yesterday. A Norwegian cohort of children born between nineteen ninety-nine and two thousand and nine: about thirty-seven thousand children in the first analysis, about eighteen thousand families in which both parents had been genotyped, and about eight thousand sibling pairs, of whom more than five thousand differed in how long they had been exclusively breastfed.

Each additional month of exclusive breastfeeding went with slightly lower attention and hyperactivity symptom scores at three, five and eight, fading a little by eight. It survived adjustment for the child's own genetic risk. And it largely survived the sibling comparison, which is the strong test here, because comparing siblings holds constant everything a family shares, although those sibling estimates weakened when boys and girls were analysed separately.

The authors are unusually plain about size. The reduction for any individual child is small and would not be clinically visible. Their claim is a population one: slightly fewer children falling over a diagnostic threshold. Symptoms were parent-reported, participation was voluntary with drop-out along the way, and the study remains observational. The genetic and sibling analyses reduce confounding. They do not abolish it.

# Six. Support for free speech is not one attitude.

Nejc Plohl at Maribor, with Dean Korosak and Boris Podobnik, in Personality and Individual Differences, published on Thursday. Five hundred and sixty-three European adults answered twenty-four questions about tolerating four different things: offensive speech, disagreement, heterodox opinion, and speech that divides people socially.

The first result is the measurement one. A factor analysis supported four separate dimensions and gave no support at all for a single general attitude to free speech, which is how survey questions normally treat it. A latent profile analysis then sorted people into three groups. Conditional tolerance, around three in five. Low tolerance, around one in five. And broad tolerance, around one in four, permissive about everything.

The uncomfortable finding concerns that last group. The broadly tolerant had the highest belief in conspiracy theories and the lowest trust in science, politics and journalism. The conditionally tolerant had the highest trust and the least conspiracy thinking. Men and the more educated were overrepresented among the broadly tolerant, and women among the least tolerant.

Hold it loosely. The items describe hypothetical categories rather than real cases, the data are cross-sectional, so nothing here settles which way the arrow runs, and both ideology and conspiracy belief were measured very briefly.

# The methods watch, from the statistics and methods course. What a corrected correlation is, and what it is not.

Back to item one, because the two numbers in it are the whole lesson. The correlation actually observed between test scores and supervisor ratings in the modern samples is about point one four. The headline figure of point two zero is what is left after two corrections. The first is for range restriction: applicants and job-holders are a selected group whose test scores vary less than the general population's, and less variation drags an observed correlation down. The second is for unreliability in the outcome, because a supervisor's rating is a noisy measure of performance.

Corrections of this kind are not cheating. They estimate what the correlation would have been in an unselected population measured without error, which is the quantity a theory of ability wants to know about. It is not the quantity an employer faces. Those two get quoted as though they were the same number, and they are not.

Four habits. Read the uncorrected figure first, because it is what was actually seen, and only then ask what the correction assumes about how people were selected. Notice which corrections were applied, because this paper's own answer moved between point one eight and point two two depending on the method. Prefer the range to the single number, since most settings falling somewhere between point zero seven and point two nine is a very different claim from about point two zero. And look at the weighting. One dataset of roughly sixty-five thousand workers would have swamped a sample-weighted average, so the authors weighted the samples equally, called it a judgement call, and said so in the paper, which is exactly what lets a reader disagree with them.

Two last things. Suspect the outcome as much as the predictor: supervisor ratings partly measure the supervisor, which puts a ceiling on any validity you could ever observe. And the paper's interaction between mental and manual job demands came in at p equals point zero five nine, in a secondary analysis, which makes it a hypothesis for the next study and not a finding to repeat.
