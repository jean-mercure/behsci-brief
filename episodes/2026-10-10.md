# Saturday the tenth of October, two thousand and twenty-six.

A thin day, and worth saying so plainly. Six items rather than the usual seven. No new working papers from the National Bureau of Economic Research, which does not post at weekends. Nothing new in Nature Human Behaviour since Thursday, nothing from the department at the School since the middle of September, and nothing in the psychology section of Retraction Watch for nearly a month. What the day did bring is one genuinely useful piece of applied work from the Behavioural Insights Team in Singapore, and it carries the concept of the day.

# The concept of the day is the gap between what people say they prefer and what their choices reveal.

This belongs to the behavioural science course, and the Singapore study is close to a textbook demonstration.

The team began by interviewing eight human resources professionals and hiring managers, who described their hiring as merit-based and said parental status played no part in it. The team then put more than two thousand two hundred working professionals, including people in those same roles, through six rounds of choosing between pairs of fictitious project managers. The recruiters among them were less likely to pick either mothers or fathers than non-parents. General employees showed no such pattern.

The thing to hold on to is that nobody lied. Self-report is pulled towards the answer the respondent believes is approved of, and it is limited by what people can actually observe about their own reasoning, which is much less than they imagine. The interview answers are not falsehoods. They are simply not evidence about behaviour.

What makes the gap measurable is the instrument. In a discrete choice experiment, parental status sits among attributes that are randomly varied across candidates, so the weight the chooser puts on each can be recovered from the pattern of choices rather than from anything they say about themselves. That inference is not available from an audit of real hiring decisions, because real applicants differ on everything at once and you can never be sure which difference did the work.

Now the part that matters most, which is the second half of the result. The revealed penalty fell equally on mothers and fathers. The stated perceptions were sharply gendered: six in ten agreed that mothers are less available for last-minute or overtime work, against four and a half in ten for fathers, while fatherhood was linked to career motivation more often than motherhood. So a study with only one of the two measures would have misled, in either direction. Stated attitudes alone would have produced a story about mothers. Revealed choices alone would have missed that the beliefs people hold about mothers and fathers differ sharply, which is what any campaign would have to work on.

Two limits belong with the method. A hypothetical candidate establishes that a preference exists; it does not establish its size in a real labour market, where a penalty may be amplified by shortlisting or washed out by competition. And a favourable average can sit on top of an unfavourable norm. Nine in ten flexible-working requests here were approved, which sounds settled, until you see the reasons ranked by deservingness: childcare and other caring about two thirds acceptable, a hobby under two in ten, no reason given six per cent. Flexibility is being treated as a concession rather than an entitlement, and that shapes who feels able to ask.

# One. Women choose workplaces by who already works in them.

Researchers at Emory, led by Stella Lourenco, ran four studies with one thousand six hundred and eighty American participants. Women reported more interest in a job, and expected to belong more, where most employees were women. In some conditions that preference outweighed whether the advertisement framed the work as needing brilliance or needing dedication, which is the framing most of this literature has concentrated on. Men were largely indifferent to who was already there.

The design escalates nicely, and that is worth noticing. It starts with estimated gender ratios across thirty real occupations, moves to composition merely implied, then to stated majorities of three quarters, then to explicit splits of sixty forty, seventy-five twenty-five and ninety ten. Only the fourth study was preregistered, the jobs were hypothetical, so this is expressed interest and not application behaviour, and the effects are small to moderate.

# Two. The mental load tracks satisfaction better than the washing-up does.

Carolin Klein and colleagues, in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, looked at the invisible half of domestic work: the noticing, the remembering, the planning and the deciding, as against the physical tasks. The first study, preregistered, surveyed four hundred and fifty-two British women living with a male partner, using a new seventeen-item scale. The second looked at a hundred and fifty-six couples in Britain and the United States.

The mental load predicted women's relationship and sexual satisfaction more strongly than the physical chores did. Women were most satisfied where it was shared equally. Men reported more relationship satisfaction when their partner carried more of it, though their sexual satisfaction was highest at an equal split.

Several reasons for caution, and they matter here. Both studies are cross-sectional, so we have correlations of about a quarter and about a seventh and no direction of causation. The scale is new and not yet validated, and one outside commentator has suggested it may be partly capturing how responsive a partner seems rather than how much thinking they do. Paid working hours were not measured, which is a serious omission in a study about who carries domestic cognitive work.

# Three. Fraud accusations landed where the Black population was largest, but the experiment was equivocal.

Kevin Morris and a colleague, in Political Behavior, bring three designs to one question. First, about two point seven million posts on Twitter from twenty twenty mentioning voter fraud, of which some ninety-five thousand named an American city of a hundred thousand or more. After the election, mentions concentrated on cities with larger Black population shares, and that share was the only reliable predictor. How the city had actually voted was unrelated. Second, a panel of about four thousand three hundred White respondents either side of election day, in which racial resentment among Trump supporters predicted a further fall in confidence in the election, with no equivalent movement among Biden supporters.

Third, and this is where it comes apart, a survey experiment on fifteen hundred White adults in twenty twenty-four. A fraud story raised suspicion in general, but participants did not rate a fictional Black inner-city area as more fraudulent than a fictional White suburb. A gap did appear among White Republicans, but that subgroup analysis was not preregistered, which is exactly the kind of result that should be treated as a hypothesis for next time.

The authors describe their own evidence as mixed, which is the right word and a good sign. The observational work cannot separate posts promoting a fraud claim from posts debunking it, bot amplification is possible, and the fictional city may simply not have carried the association the researchers intended.

# Four. A childhood diagnosis of attention deficit disorder predicts lower religious attendance in midlife, but not less prayer.

Laura Upenieks and a colleague at Baylor used a long-running American cohort of nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five adults, grouping people by whether they were diagnosed before eighteen, after eighteen, or never. A childhood diagnosis went with roughly half the odds of attending religious services regularly in midlife. An adult diagnosis showed no association at all.

The useful part is the dissociation. Prayer, and stated importance of religion, showed no surviving association once the authors adjusted for childhood circumstances, family structure, early-life health including depression, and adolescent religious involvement. So what separates is public behaviour from private practice, which points at the demands of the setting, sitting still and quiet in a room for an hour, rather than at belief.

Limitations are real. The diagnosis is self-reported and binary, so severity is unmeasured and anyone undiagnosed sits in the never-diagnosed group. Women were historically under-diagnosed, which distorts the comparison. The religious measures are largely Christian. And the authors' explanations for the larger declines they find among Black and Asian American adults are interpretive rather than tested.

# Five. Autistic and attention deficit traits take different routes into poor mental health.

An unusually good dataset, this one. Hashimoto and colleagues, in Psychological Medicine, surveyed every public elementary and junior high school in one Japanese municipality, annually from the fourth to the ninth grade, across eleven cohorts from twenty twelve to twenty twenty-three, with reports from students, parents and teachers. Nine thousand six hundred and nineteen children were analysed.

The two trait dimensions ran down different paths. Autistic traits predicted later peer problems, strongly, and later internalising symptoms more weakly, but not academic difficulty or conflict with teachers. Attention deficit traits predicted later externalising behaviour and later academic difficulty, most strongly in the fourth and fifth grades, and that academic difficulty then predicted internalising symptoms later still. Peer problems and internalising symptoms fed each other in both directions.

One finding deserves particular attention. Peer problems predicted higher parent-rated autistic traits, and externalising behaviour predicted higher parent-rated attention deficit traits. The authors suggest this may be the parents' perception shifting rather than the child's traits changing, which is a reminder that in this literature the measure and the thing measured are not easily separated.

The authors are explicit that temporal order is not causation. These are group-level patterns, in dimensional traits across a general school population rather than in diagnosed children, most effects are small to medium, the autism items cover only the Asperger type, everything is questionnaire-based, and no intervention was tested.

# Six. Singapore recruiters say they hire on merit, then penalise parents.

The study behind the concept of the day, so briefly. Beyond what I have already described, two details are worth having. Recruiters at larger firms penalised parents more than those at smaller firms, which the authors attribute to hiring volume and the heavier reliance on shortcuts that volume forces. And the recommendations are sensible and specific: transparency about flexible working at the recruitment stage rather than after an offer, caregiving support built to be gender-neutral, callback rates tracked so that bias can be detected rather than merely denied, and equal access for employees without children, since a policy framed around parenthood invites exactly the deservingness ranking the study found.

# The methods watch. Five students, three conditions and no significant differences.

A paper in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience asks whether listening to music you like helps you remember. Yoshiko Tojo scanned students retrieving visual memories under three conditions: their preferred Japanese pop music, a composite of a siren and an earthquake alarm, and scanner noise alone. Recall was about eighty-two per cent with preferred music, seventy-six with the alarms, seventy-one with neither. Subjective focus was highest with music, and activity in the anterior insula was higher with music too.

After exclusions, the sample was five people. And none of the differences was statistically significant.

Three lessons, and they generalise well beyond this paper. First, an eleven-percentage-point descriptive gap on a test that is not significant is not a finding, and an ordering across three conditions at five participants carries almost no information at all: with that many people the recall figures would reorder themselves on a different Tuesday. Second, and this is the more serious problem, a different set of photographs was used in each condition, so the condition is confounded with how hard the material was. No increase in sample size would fix that; it is a design fault, not a power problem. Third, a pilot this small cannot estimate an effect size for a later power calculation, however tempting it is to use it that way. Carrying a noisy observed difference forward into a sample-size calculation is the standard route to an underpowered confirmatory study, and then to a second null that nobody publishes.

Credit where it is due. The author states plainly that these results do not show that preferred music improves memory. The distance between that sentence and the confident headlines written about the paper is the thing to notice.
