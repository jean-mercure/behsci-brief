# The concept of the day is what a stimulation study establishes that an imaging study cannot

Much of Foundations of Psychological Science is the localisation of function: which part of the brain does what. Almost all the evidence you meet is correlational. A region lights up while someone reads a word, and the claim that it is doing the reading is an inference, not an observation. The region may be incidental to the task, or it may simply receive the output of whatever is doing the work.

Two kinds of evidence close that gap by taking the region away instead of watching it. Lesion studies supply necessity, and have given us most of what we know about language. But a lesion is not assigned, is rarely confined to one structure, and the patient has usually had years to reorganise around it. Magnetic stimulation supplies what a lesion cannot: interference that is brief, reversible, and above all assigned.

So look at how today's third item pays for its inference. The target was not a coordinate from an atlas but each person's own semantic peak, found in their own scan, which strips out the anatomical variation that blunts group studies. The comparison was within person, the same twenty-five people receiving both the strong pulse and the weak one. And the control was an active weak pulse at the same spot, which matters because a magnetic pulse makes a loud click and a distinct scalp sensation, so a sham omitting both compares two experiences rather than two doses.

Then the finding complicates the simple story, and this is the part to carry forward. Disruption did not switch the region off and degrade performance in proportion. Activity went up: locally, and in distant regions earlier work had mapped as general-purpose rather than specialised for meaning. And the people whose right frontal activity rose most paid the smallest cost in reaction time. The brain answers back, and the consequence is sharp. A disruption that produces no behavioural effect is not evidence that the region was idle, because in behaviour alone, compensation and irrelevance look the same.

Three questions, then, for any stimulation study. Where was the coil placed, and was the site defined in this participant or taken from an atlas? Did the control match the click and the scalp sensation as well as the timing? And if behaviour did not change, was any brain measure taken that could tell a region that was not needed from one whose loss was quietly covered?

# One. Raising the school leaving age by a single year, and dementia half a century later

Silvia Barcellos and colleagues, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, use a British law of nineteen seventy-two. Children born on or after the first of September nineteen fifty-seven had to stay at school until sixteen; those born a day earlier could leave at fifteen. That gives a sharp boundary in time, and comparing people either side of it is called a regression discontinuity. The data come from UK Biobank, with diagnoses taken from linked health records to the end of twenty twenty-two.

The law worked, modestly, raising the share staying on to sixteen by about fourteen percentage points, adding about two tenths of a year of schooling on average. Dementia was still rare: about half of one per cent of those born just before the cutoff had a diagnosis, and those born under the reform were two tenths of a percentage point less likely.

Here is the detail worth your attention. Scaled to a full extra year of schooling, the reduction becomes nearly nine tenths of a percentage point, and the authors themselves call that implausibly large. They are right: you are dividing a small effect by a small change, so the ratio inherits both uncertainties. Note also what is identified. The design estimates the effect of a law, not of education. The sample is healthier and better educated than Britain, and the paper may be some months old.

# Two. Voters prefer an improving trend to a better one

Markus Prior and Talbot Andrews, in the American Political Science Review, showed survey respondents four years of economic data as bar charts and asked them to choose between a flat line and a rising one. Then they raised the flat line step by step until the respondent switched, locating each person's indifference point.

On income growth, a flat path had to deliver a steady two point three per cent before people preferred it to a rising path averaging only one point three. On jobs, a steady market had to guarantee three hundred and fifty thousand a year to beat an improving one averaging about two hundred and forty thousand. Between a third and nearly half of respondents chose rising sequences that were worse on every arithmetic comparison.

When the display printed the cumulative total and greyed out the blank space around the bars, the preference shrank but did not disappear, which points at how a graph is read rather than at a judgement about the future. Against that: no coefficients, no intervals, no p-values, no country named, and no account of how the modified display was assigned.

# Three. Interfering with the network that handles meaning

This is the paper behind the concept, so I will be brief. Anna-Lisa Schuler and colleagues, in Nature Communications two days ago, combined magnetic stimulation with brain imaging in twenty-five adults, interrupting a region on the left side of the brain that handles word meaning while people decided whether two things matched in meaning. Full-strength stimulation, against a much weaker pulse at the same spot, pushed activity up rather than down: locally, in regions handling the control and retrieval of meaning, and in distant general-purpose regions. Those with the biggest local increase were slowest, and those with the biggest right frontal increase were least slowed. Two cautions. The general-purpose label comes from earlier maps, not from this study, and the key local correlation is one-sided and uncorrected, sitting just under the conventional threshold. Our read of the paper was partial, with the methods reaching us secondhand.

# Four. A perceptual marker for social anxiety that three experiments could not find

Madeleine Moses-Payne and colleagues, in the Journal of Affective Disorders, asked whether socially anxious people have a wider cone of direct gaze. The cone of gaze is the range of eye angles over which another face still feels as though it is looking straight at you, and has been proposed as a marker of social anxiety.

They did two things. First they pooled the existing literature: nineteen studies and over nine hundred participants between them. Both pooled results were modest and, on the authors' own account, highly inconsistent, with the individual studies often small. Then they ran three fresh online experiments, of about one hundred and ninety, one hundred and seventy and one hundred and fifty participants, flashing faces with the eyes angled up to eight degrees aside and asking on every trial whether the face was looking at them.

The first experiment, with neutral faces, found nothing. The second, with angry faces, found the cone widening by one hundredth of a degree per point of anxiety, which is about as close to nothing as a significant result gets. The third found nothing, and the pooled data no relationship at all. The shape is the lesson: nineteen small studies produce a modest pooled effect, and five hundred fresh participants produce none. There is no Bayes factor, no equivalence test and no power analysis.

# Five. A meditation trial that lost to progressive muscle relaxation

Olivia Young, Katherine O'Connell and colleagues, in PLOS One, randomised seventy-five adults with no meditation experience to four weeks of online loving-kindness meditation or to progressive muscle relaxation: six fifteen-minute audio sessions a week, matched for schedule and format. Sixty-nine finished.

The meditation arm beat the comparison arm on nothing. Perceived social support rose slightly after relaxation and fell slightly after meditation, a difference significant in the comparison arm's favour. Loneliness fell after relaxation and not after meditation, just short of significance. A wall-sit endurance task, their measure of pain tolerance, improved by about eight seconds after relaxation and not at all after meditation.

The interesting part is what the trial does and does not establish. It was registered and preregistered, and a power calculation gives ninety per cent power for a small effect on pain tolerance, which is good practice. But for the null there is no Bayes factor, no equivalence test and no stated minimal detectable effect, so what this establishes is a failure to beat an active comparison, not an absence of effect. And there was no do-nothing arm, which the authors acknowledge, so practice effects cannot be pulled apart from the treatments.

# Six. Two societies, one structure of punishment

Leo Fitouchi and Manvir Singh, in Psychological Science, asked seventy-four Mentawai horticulturalists in Indonesia, where justice is handled locally rather than by courts, and six hundred Americans to judge nine scenarios of property damage varying what the victim lost, whether the damage was accidental, and whether the offender had done it before. In both samples the fines people prescribed tracked two separate concerns: replacing what was taken, and an extra penalty that rose with the risk of repetition. Moral outrage predicted the size of the fine in both places. Two things to hold against it. The materials were not identical across the two societies and no measurement equivalence work is reported, and not one effect size or test statistic appears in the coverage.

# The methods watch is that the largest journal in the biggest medical database has stopped being indexed

On the thirtieth of October, Retraction Watch reported that the United States National Library of Medicine has paused the indexing of new articles from the journal Scientific Reports while it reconsiders whether the journal belongs there at all. The newest article from that journal in the database was published in mid-September. For this year, about twenty-eight and a half thousand of its articles are indexed, against about forty-one thousand published on its own website. The chief editor confirmed the pause and gave no reason, the library has named no concerns whatever, and a review of this kind usually takes seven to ten months.

Why is that a methods story rather than a news story? Because indexing is not a quality badge. It is a sampling frame. Almost every systematic review and meta-analysis you read defines its evidence base by a database search, and in medicine and psychology that search is usually of this one. What is not indexed is not retrieved. So a gap in indexing becomes, months later, a gap in somebody's evidence synthesis, and it will not be visible anywhere in that synthesis.

The gap is also not random, which is the part that should bother you. It falls on one journal over one window of months, so it is correlated with subject matter, with where the authors work, and with the kinds of study a high-volume open-access journal publishes. A review searching during the pause drops that slice silently, which is the structural signature of selection bias: a systematic omission, with a sign, that the analysis cannot see. And the scale matters, because this is the largest journal in that database by articles indexed.

Do not over-read it, though. Nothing has been delisted and articles already indexed are untouched. The lesson is that a database is an instrument with inclusion rules, that those rules change, and that the sentence we searched the literature from twenty ten to twenty twenty-six is a methodological decision and not a formality. So ask which databases a review searched, and over which dates. And treat an absence from a listing as a fact about the listing before you treat it as a fact about the world.
