# The concept of the day belongs to Foundations of Psychological Science, and it is how you tell a learned perceptual effect from a universal one.

Start with the result. Japanese participants rated animated figures for attractiveness, from zero to a hundred. When the figure bowed, the rating went up. A quick nod bought six to twelve points, a deeper bow of about a second and a half between fifteen and nearly thirty, and the face itself did not change.

So where does it come from? Two accounts, of a kind you will meet all year. The first is universalist: lowering the head is an ancient display of submission, found across species, so any human should read it and the effect should appear wherever you test it. The second is cultural: in Japan a bow is elaborate learned etiquette, graded by depth and duration, so what the rater registers is courtesy, legible only to those taught to read it.

Here is the difficulty. Within one culture those accounts predict the same data, so a study run only in Japan cannot separate them. What you need is a dissociation: the same stimulus, scale and task, in a group that has not learned the code. The identical sequences shown to American raters moved the ratings by typically less than three points.

Two details do real work. The effect is graded, a long bow beating a short one in every test, which fits a politeness gradient rather than an all-or-nothing signal. And Japanese raters gave the bonus to European, South Asian and African faces as readily as to East Asian ones, with no significant movement-by-face-type interaction. So the knowledge sits in the observer rather than the stimulus, which is close to a definition of a culturally acquired perceptual schema.

Three cautions. These are animated models, not people, and a bow in life carries voice, gaze and timing. The baseline faces were chosen to sit around forty, which leaves room to rise. And the central claim compares two separately recruited samples with no interaction statistic reported, so you see the gap between the averages but not its uncertainty.

Which gives three questions. What would the universal account have predicted here? Is the observer or the stimulus doing the work? And am I being shown a test of a difference, or two descriptions set side by side?

# One. Nearly nine voters in ten expect their own side to win, and that cannot be true.

Among Americans who name a preferred presidential candidate, close to ninety per cent now expect that candidate to win, against about seventy a few decades ago. In a two-party contest about half have to be wrong. Seth Warner at the University of Connecticut sets out the trend in Political Behavior, using American National Election Studies data from the nineteen fifties onward, adjusted for the polls at the time. Note the base: the question goes only to the seven in ten expressing a vote intention, so this is a share of partisans, not of all adults. Warner decomposes the rise between nineteen eighty and twenty twenty, about eighteen percentage points. Partisan animosity, rating the other party below twenty-five on a hundred-point scale, rose nearly fifty points and accounts for about a fifth of the increase. Party loyalty in voting accounts for roughly a quarter, and three smaller factors for under a tenth between them. So the measured ingredients cover about half the rise. A panel following the same people across three elections puts the within-person estimate at eight to nine percentage points per standard deviation of animosity with polling controls alone, three to four with the full set. It is observational throughout, and the decomposition is an accounting rather than a demonstration of cause.

# Two. Some employees will take a title over a pay rise, if the title is visible.

Emily Zitek at Cornell, with two colleagues, reports four studies in Social Psychological and Personality Science. About a thousand full-time American employees in total, every study preregistered with data and code posted and power analyses reported, better practice than most items in this brief. The first study asked people to name one public and one private reward on offer at their own job and rate each for motivation. Private rewards won on average, five point six against four point four. But the higher someone scored on grandiose narcissism, the more motivating they found the public reward, while narcissism made no difference to the private one. The fourth study is the headline. Two hypothetical jobs, both demanding harder work: one offering a prestigious title and no extra money, the other a higher salary nobody outside the firm would know about, authority and reports held equal. Most people took the money. But narcissism predicted both choosing the title and working harder for it. Two pieces of restraint. Every outcome is a stated preference or a self-reported willingness, so nobody's actual effort was measured and the headline's working harder is an intention, not a behaviour. And the coverage gives no effect sizes, p-values or intervals, only averages on seven-point scales.

# Three. A bow makes a face more attractive, but only to raters who know what a bow means.

The concept of the day has given you the design, so here is the paper. Takayuki Osugi and Jun Kawahara, in Japanese Psychological Research. Six sub-experiments with Japanese raters, between forty-five and fifty people in each, judging animated figures of four racial appearances standing still, bowing quickly, or bowing for about a second and a half. Then the same sequences with thirty to forty American raters, where the movement bought under three points. Two further experiments varied the baseline face's attractiveness, and among Japanese raters the bonus held at both ends of that range. One limitation is credited to the authors, that they collected no information on the American raters' own racial background. Two more are not, and should be: about half the Japanese participants served in more than one sub-experiment, and the set ran over roughly three years.

# Four. Across forty-one cultures almost the whole moral hierarchy holds, and one comparison does not.

Isil Ayca Akkus and Bastian Jaeger at Tilburg, with three colleagues, in the European Journal of Social Psychology. Six and a half thousand university students in forty-one cultural groups. The data were collected in twenty eighteen and nineteen, so this is a re-analysis of older material, not preregistered. Participants sorted thirty entities into four rings of moral concern, from strong obligation at the centre to none outside. The authors took thirteen category orderings that had held in earlier Western work and asked how many survived in each group. Most did. Family and friends came top in every group, villains bottom in thirty-eight of forty-one, and the median group reproduced eleven of thirteen. The disagreement is concentrated in one place: human outgroup members, meaning foreign citizens, political opponents and people of other faiths, against the environment. Only twenty-one of the forty-one put the outgroup above old-growth forests and coral reefs. The individual picture is far messier, the typical person reproducing about seven of thirteen. And here is the number to keep. A multilevel model attributed roughly forty per cent of the variation in whether an ordering held to which pair was compared, and only thirteen per cent to which culture was answering. The question matters more than the culture. No intervals or p-values anywhere, and each category rests on three examples.

# Five. Of five campaign videos, only the one about policy moved anything.

A five-arm online experiment in Political Research Quarterly, by Sydney Carr-Glenn, Chaya Crowder and Christine Slaughter. About twelve hundred participants, each randomly assigned one of five short videos shown outside the app, as though a friend had sent it: four Harris campaign clips, among them a policy montage of over a minute and an eleven-second identity appeal, against a nonpolitical control of teenagers dancing. Only the policy clip raised overall favourability. All four political clips raised stated willingness to share, and no clip moved voting intentions, which the researchers themselves call limited evidence of any effect on votes. Two things to register. Outcomes were measured only after exposure, with no baseline described, which random assignment makes defensible but leaves nothing to check the arms against. And the coverage reports not one number: no per-arm counts, no coefficients, no p-values, just the words meaningfully and significantly. The fieldwork was during early voting in November twenty twenty-four, so this is current coverage of older work: not wrong, just not news.

# Six. Who overrates their own science knowledge, and how that was worked out.

Marcus Mann and Cyrus Schleifer, in Public Understanding of Science, using just over seven thousand General Social Survey respondents from two thousand and six to twenty eighteen. Among self-described extreme liberals who got their science information offline, thirty-nine per cent were classed as overestimating their knowledge and fifteen per cent underestimating it. Among extreme liberals who named the internet, those figures roughly flip, to twenty and twenty-nine. Every other combination of ideology and source had about a third or fewer overestimating. Liberals outscored conservatives on the test, but by no more than five percentage points. The design is cross-sectional, so nothing establishes whether going online humbles people or humbler people go online. Nothing is attributed to the authors, nobody is quoted, and no coefficient or interval appears anywhere. This item is in the brief for its measurement, which is the methods watch.

# And the methods watch, from the statistics and methods course: what a calibration score requires.

Overconfidence sounds like one quantity. It is not. It is a difference between two measurements, and that means something only if both terms are about the same thing.

Look at what was differenced. On one side, a single item asking how well you understand the term "scientific study" when you meet it in a news report. On the other, the fourteen-item science literacy scale, mostly true or false questions about facts. Is the centre of the Earth very hot. Do the father's genes decide a baby's sex.

Those are not the same competence. A person can know perfectly well what a scientific study is and still get the genetics item wrong, or hold a stock of facts and no idea what a study design does. So the gap between them is not obviously arrogance or humility. The coverage concedes the point: a study comparing confidence in specific facts against knowledge of those same facts might give a different answer.

Three more. First, the rule. Somewhere a three-level self-rating and a fourteen-point score became three categories, overconfident, accurate and underconfident. That rule is not reported, and it decides who lands where. Shift the cut by a point and the percentages move, from identical data.

Second, the denominator. The headline cell is a subgroup of a subgroup. Extreme liberals are under four per cent of seven thousand respondents, perhaps two hundred and fifty people, then split by where they get their science news. The cell carrying the finding may hold well under a hundred, and its size is never stated.

Third, the claim is an interaction. That the effect of the medium depends on your ideology is a statement about whether two differences differ, and it needs an interaction term. None is reported. What you get is two percentages from two cells, with no coefficient, interval or p-value, next to a remark that a neighbouring comparison fell just short of significance.

Three habits, then. When a result is a gap between two measures, ask what each measured and whether they are commensurable. When the headline is conditional, ask whether that condition was estimated as an interaction or merely illustrated by splitting the sample. And when one percentage carries the argument, ask how many stand behind it.
