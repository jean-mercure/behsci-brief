# The concept of the day, from Foundations of Behavioural Science: costly punishment, and what the punisher is buying.

Start with the ultimatum game, the field's founding experiment. One player is given a sum and proposes how to divide it. The second either accepts, in which case both are paid, or rejects, in which case neither gets anything. The simplest economic account predicts that the second accepts any offer above nothing, because something beats nothing. What people do instead is reject low offers, routinely, and pay for the privilege. That refusal is costly punishment, and it is the standard evidence that people hold social preferences: a dislike of unequal outcomes, or a taste for returning bad behaviour in kind.

What the standard result cannot tell you is what the punisher is buying. Two possibilities overlap. The rejecting player may be buying the outcome, removing an unfair division from the world. Or that player may be buying the effect on the other person, making someone who behaved badly worse off. With a human partner you cannot pull those apart: the same person both collects the money and can be made to feel something about losing it.

Today's first item prises them apart. An algorithm can take a payoff and cannot be made to suffer, and participants said as much: asked how far their partner could experience anything, they rated algorithms under two on a five-point scale against over three for humans. The decision to punish was indifferent to that. Rejection of unfair algorithm offers ran at about seventy per cent, slightly above the rate for humans. The satisfaction of punishing was not indifferent at all. Treating an algorithm harshly in a trust game went with less sense of justice done, and telling participants it had suffered, or had learned something, raised satisfaction above a no-message condition.

Three things follow. The act and the feeling are separate measurements, and a study taking both can find them coming apart. A manipulation that leaves the rate of an action unchanged while altering what the action delivers is telling you something about the motive rather than nothing. And the design worked by separating two properties always welded together in a person, which is a move worth borrowing. Hold it loosely: the overall machine against human difference in the largest experiment explained about half of one per cent of the variance.

So when a paper tells you that people punish, put two questions to it. Is the measure the act or the feeling? And was the target's capacity to be affected varied, or simply assumed?

# One. People will pay to punish an algorithm, and feel less for having done it.

Alexander Eng at the Asia School of Business in Kuala Lumpur and Kai Chi Yam at the National University of Singapore, in the Journal of Experimental Psychology General. Six experiments, more than six thousand participants, using ultimatum, dictator and trust games with a partner described either as a person or as an algorithm. The numbers are in the concept of the day, so this entry is confined to the design. The main experiment was a registered report. Eng's own caveats deserve repeating: the stakes were pennies, the satisfaction measure held together poorly, and the machine against human differences were slight. One point the coverage does not attribute to anyone: participants chose whether to punish, so punishers may differ from those who accepted.

# Two. The psilocybin trials and the press coverage of them, counted side by side.

Kim Madden and Anthony Adili at McMaster University, with six colleagues, in JAMA Network Open. They coded seventy trial publications on clinical uses of psilocybin from the past eleven years and a hundred and seventy-two English-language news articles from the same period. The headline comparison carries the methods watch at the end of the episode, so I will leave those numbers there. One that is not in dispute: ninety-three per cent of the trial publications had fewer than a hundred participants. I carry this item for what it says about how evidence travels, not as a view about the drug.

# Three. A few minutes of speech, turned into an estimate of how old a brain is.

Hernan Hernandez, with Agustin Ibanez and Adolfo Garcia as senior authors, in Science Advances. They recorded nearly three thousand native Spanish speakers across five Latin American countries, average age about sixty-five. About half were cognitively healthy; the rest had Alzheimer's disease or frontotemporal dementia. Seven speaking tasks produced more than seven hundred acoustic and linguistic measurements, from which a machine-learning model guessed each speaker's age. The gap between guess and truth is the marker on offer. Healthy adults had the smallest gaps and the language variant of frontotemporal dementia the largest, and bigger gaps went with worse memory and executive scores and with a blood marker of Alzheimer's pathology. Now the accuracy. The average error is about nine years, the model explains something like forty-four per cent of the variation in age, and the reported correlation of point seven four follows what the coverage calls a standard correction. Whether the model was trained and tested on separate people is not stated. Both senior authors stress that the design is cross-sectional and the tool is not a diagnosis. Garcia co-founded the app that processed the audio. An outside researcher, Mai-Carmen Requena-Komuro in Hamburg, questioned the screening cut-off and the risk of over-reading a single composite number.

# Four. Household chaos and school results, with the family held constant.

Sophie von Stumm at the University of York, with three colleagues, in Learning and Individual Differences. Seven and a half thousand pairs of British twins born in the mid-nineties. The children rated how chaotic home life was at ages nine, twelve and sixteen on a six-item questionnaire, and achievement came from teacher ratings and national examinations at the same ages. Both directions showed up. More chaos earlier predicted lower attainment later, and lower attainment predicted more chaos later, both effects called relatively small, with no figures given. What changes the reading is the decomposition. Once parental education, occupation and income were accounted for, the path from attainment to chaos was entirely explained by genes shared within the family. The path from chaos to attainment was entirely explained by environment shared within the family, and adjusting for income and education made no difference to it. So neither direction survives as a causal story about the home itself. No variance components are reported, no limitation is attributed to the authors, and the obvious cautions are left to the journalist: a six-item scale is coarse, and a child's rating of chaos may report the child's own state.

# Five. A receptor map taken from adult brains and laid over children's.

Yuan Zhang, with Vinod Menon as senior author, at Stanford, in Nature Communications. They measured grey matter volume across two hundred and eighteen brain regions in nearly a thousand typically developing children aged seven to fourteen, in two samples, and related it to tests of calculation, problem-solving, word reading and comprehension. They then compared the regional pattern against an existing atlas of nineteen neurotransmitter receptor and transporter systems. Of those nineteen, one replicated across both samples and both abilities: the glutamate receptor NMDA. Regions where grey matter mattered most for attainment tended to have higher NMDA density, and receptor distribution accounted for between eight and twenty per cent of the variation across regions. Here is the fact that governs how much this can mean, stated plainly in the coverage. No receptor, and no glutamate, was measured in any of these children. The atlas was built from earlier imaging of more than twelve hundred healthy adults. So the claim is that a correlation found in children is strongest where the average adult brain happens to have a given receptor density. That is a statement about regions, not children, and nothing here says a child with more of these receptors does better at arithmetic.

# Six. One region out of four, and nothing at all across the whole brain.

Hanlin Wu at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, with colleagues, in NeuroImage Clinical. Children listened in a scanner, without responding, to spoken Cantonese sentences: some about people in emotional situations, some about objects, and some played backwards as a control for language. Every sentence was eight syllables, matched for word frequency and grammatical complexity, and spoken in one synthetic voice so that tone was constant, which is neat stimulus control. Seventy-eight children were recruited and twenty excluded for moving their heads, leaving thirty-seven autistic and twenty-one non-autistic children. In both groups a region of the left temporal lobe responded more strongly to the emotional than the object sentences. In one of four regions chosen in advance, that preference was weaker in the autistic children. Two things hold the finding down. The exclusions were uneven, thirty-one per cent of the autistic children against thirteen per cent of the others, so the groups that remained were selected differently. And no difference anywhere else in the brain survived correction, with no correction stated for the four chosen regions.

# The methods watch, from the statistics and methods course: two percentages that are not the same quantity.

Back to the psilocybin review, because it contains a trap worth a few minutes.

Seventy per cent of the trial publications concluded in the drug's favour on their main outcome. Seventy-three per cent of the news articles made at least one positive claim about its effectiveness. Three percentage points apart. The invitation is obvious: the press tracked the science. Resist it. Those two numbers are not measurements of the same thing, and the review itself says so.

Start with the denominator. On one side the unit is a trial publication, on the other a news article, and the two do not correspond: one widely covered trial can generate dozens of articles while a trial that found nothing generates none, so the sets are not samples of a common population. Nor is a publication a trial. Seventy publications are not seventy independent experiments, because the same patients can appear in more than one paper, which is why the review's own breakdown, thirty-six randomised studies and thirty not, tells you more than the round seventy.

Now the numerator. Concluding that psilocybin benefited patients on the primary outcome is a claim about one pre-specified endpoint in one study, and ninety-three per cent of these studies had fewer than a hundred participants. Making at least one positive claim about effectiveness is a far lower bar and a quite different object. Two proportions can be defined so differently that their near-equality carries no information.

The interesting divergence appears only when you stop comparing rates and look at scope. Forty-three per cent of the articles mentioned post-traumatic stress disorder, and the review found no eligible trial of psilocybin for it. That is not coverage overstating a result. It is coverage reporting on a condition the literature had not tested. No measure of enthusiasm would have caught it: the claim is not too strong for its evidence, it has no evidence at all.

One last caution. Every figure I have given you comes from people reading articles and assigning codes, and the agreement between those coders is described as moderate, with no statistic.

Three habits. Before comparing two proportions, write down the unit of the denominator on each side and check they are the same kind of thing. Read the definition of the numerator, not the label on the chart. And when a content analysis hands you percentages, look for the agreement statistic, because without it every percentage carries an error nobody has measured.
