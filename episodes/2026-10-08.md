# The concept of the day is relative coding

This belongs to Foundations of Psychological Science, and it is one of those ideas that looks narrow when you first meet it and then turns out to be everywhere.

The nervous system is largely uninterested in absolute values. What it stores, almost wherever you look, is relations. Your visual system does not record how many photons came off a sheet of paper; it records how bright that paper is relative to what surrounds it, which is why a page looks white in a dim room and in full sun, though the light reaching your eye differs by orders of magnitude. Weber's law says the same thing about discrimination: the difference you can just detect is a proportion of the baseline, not a fixed quantity. Adaptation and contrast effects are the same principle showing its workings.

The fourth item today is a clean demonstration in a domain where you might have expected otherwise. Ten patients with electrodes already implanted on the cortex for epilepsy monitoring listened to familiar children's songs, each presented in three different keys, and then imagined the next two bars in silence. The researchers tried to decode what the patients were imagining. Relative pitch came out: the intervals, the shape of the tune, recoverable at twenty-six per cent against a chance level of sixteen. Absolute pitch did not come out at all, not above chance. The transposition into three keys is what makes this work as a design, because it forces the two to come apart. A code that tracked actual frequencies would have been confounded by key; one that tracks intervals would not.

Now take the step across to Foundations of Behavioural Science, because the same principle is the spine of prospect theory. Kahneman and Tversky's central claim is that people do not evaluate wealth, they evaluate changes from a reference point, and that the reference point moves. Losses and gains are relations, not quantities. The third item today, on lottery winners, carries a version of the same question: if winning money changes a man's marriage prospects mainly when he started below the median, the mechanism may be relative standing rather than absolute security, and the authors are honest that they cannot settle which.

So the question to carry: when a study reports a quantity, ask what it is a quantity relative to, and whether the design let the absolute and the relative version of the measure come apart. If it did not, the paper may not have measured what its title says.

# One. A safe tier for teenagers may work where an outright ban does not

A new working paper from Bursztyn, Jimenez-Duran, Leonard, Milojevic and Roth, and I should say at once that it is theory and mechanism design, with no field data behind it. The argument is that a platform kept liable for teenagers on its adult tier, while allowed to offer a stripped-down safe tier, faces incentives that work in the right direction: enforcement and the safe tier reinforce one another, and the network is preserved rather than destroyed. The point worth keeping is about why bans are hard. A teenager's decision to leave a platform depends on whether everyone else leaves, so the policy problem is a coordination problem, and the safe tier breaks that dependence.

# Two. Conservatives think more rigidly but do not follow their party more closely

Triki, Cassario and Brandt, in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology yesterday, across four experiments and more than seven thousand American participants, two of them re-analyses of older studies and two new.

Conservatives did score higher on the standard rigidity measures, which replicates a long literature. But when the researchers tested the behavioural implication, that conservatives should therefore follow party cues more closely, it did not appear. Both sides shifted by about three tenths of a point on a seven-point scale when told where their party stood. The same, in other words.

One exception resisted: right-wing authoritarianism did predict greater cue-following, and the authors do not have an explanation for it, which they say plainly.

The limitations matter here, because the finding is a null and nulls need defending. These are stated attitudes, not behaviour. The cues were accurate ones. There was no left-wing authoritarianism measure to balance the exception. Everything is American, and one sample was a convenience panel the authors themselves down-weight.

# Three. A windfall changes men's marriages and women's divorces

Cesarini, Lindqvist, Ostling and Terskaya, in the Journal of Human Resources yesterday, using Swedish register data on nearly seventy-seven thousand lottery players aged eighteen to forty-four, across three lotteries, comparing winners with people who faced the same odds and did not win. That comparison is what makes this credible: the control group bought the same tickets.

Single men who won were about five percentage points more likely to marry within five years, which on a base of sixteen per cent is a rise of roughly thirty per cent, and the effect was concentrated among men whose income before the win was below the median. Their fertility rose about fourteen per cent at ten years. For married women who won, the chance of divorcing within two years roughly doubled from a base of four per cent, and the effect had faded by ten years.

Be careful how you state that contrast. The effects for women's marriage and fertility were small and not statistically significant, and the authors did not confirm that the difference between men and women is itself statistically reliable, which is a separate test and a commonly skipped one. The male divorce result moves about when you change age bands or definitions. And this is Sweden, with its particular welfare state.

# Four. Imagined melodies can be read off the cortex, but only as intervals

We have used this one for the concept of the day, so briefly. Kwon and colleagues, in eNeuro yesterday, recording directly from the cortical surface in ten epilepsy patients, decoded imagined music in half-second windows into six relative pitch classes. Contour was recoverable, absolute pitch was not. The signal sat in the superior temporal gyri on both sides and in the left precentral gyrus, which is the motor strip, and that last detail is the interesting one: imagining a tune appears to involve the machinery of producing it.

A clinical sample of ten, with familiar stimuli and electrodes placed for medical rather than scientific reasons. Treat it as a demonstration of what is possible, not an estimate of anything.

# Five. Reward circuitry runs in opposite directions in ADHD and autism

Zhu, Bore, Wang, Xu and Feng, in Molecular Psychiatry yesterday. A meta-analysis pooling the locations of findings across twenty-nine neuroimaging studies, covering four hundred and sixty-eight people with ADHD, four hundred and twenty-four with autism and just over a thousand controls.

The amygdala and putamen were over-responsive to reward in ADHD and under-responsive in autism. Prefrontal activity was reduced in ADHD and heightened in the medial prefrontal cortex in autism. Opposite directions in the same structures.

That pattern has a name worth knowing: a double dissociation. When two conditions move two measures in opposite directions, you can argue that the measures are tapping separable systems rather than one dimension of general severity, which a single difference could never establish. It is one of the strongest forms of argument available in this kind of work.

The caution is that monetary and social rewards had to be pooled, because there were too few autism studies to separate them, and the connectivity mapping came from an independent sample of healthy brains rather than from the patients themselves.

# Six. The halo does not extend to misogyny

Meerson, Koban and Matthes, in Computers in Human Behavior yesterday, ran an online experiment with eight hundred and eighty-four Austrian adults. Mock social media profiles carried faces pre-rated as high, medium or low in attractiveness, paired with comments graded by experts at three levels of severity, from incivility through intolerance to outright threat.

Attractiveness did nothing. Not on acceptability, not on how sexist the comment was judged, not on anger or disgust, not on willingness to report it. Severity drove all of those. The attractiveness halo, which is robust enough in other judgements, simply did not reach this one.

One sobering detail from the other direction: willingness to reply to the comment directly stayed low at every level of severity. People were content to report and to support moderation, and reluctant to say anything themselves.

Static images, male perpetrators only, one country. But this is the second well-powered null in today's brief, and two nulls in a day is a reasonable state of affairs rather than a disappointing one.

# Seven. Who actually took part, and what the average effect hides

A working paper from Attanasio, Cardona-Sosa and Liu on a problem that sounds technical and is not. In many trials you know who was offered the programme, but not who in the control group would have taken it up had they been offered it. That missing information is what stops you saying anything about the people the programme actually reaches.

The authors identify the effects using a noisy signal of participation together with information from the treated sample, and apply it to a clustered trial of an early-years intervention in Ghana, where the corrected estimates depart substantially from the naive ones on some outcomes.

This sits directly on the distinction between intention to treat, which is the effect of being offered something, and treatment on the treated, which is the effect on those who took it. Keep those two apart in your head. They answer different questions, and the gap between them is where most arguments about programme effectiveness actually live.

# The methods watch. When an effect loses most of itself to one control variable

Davidovich, Shestakova, Arzumanyan and Panidi, in Frontiers in Psychology yesterday, asked whether liking sweet things predicts impatience, measured as steeper discounting of delayed rewards. There is a plausible story behind it about a common reward sensitivity. Three features of the paper are worth your attention, and the authors disclose all three.

First, the headline association rests on a subgroup. Twelve sweet-likers out of forty-nine participants in the first experiment, and sweet-liking accounted for something like two tenths of one per cent of the variance. A subgroup that small, carrying a share of variance that small, is a finding to hold very loosely.

Second, and this is the general lesson, the association weakened by roughly forty per cent and lost significance once risk aversion was controlled. Think about why. In a discounting task a delayed reward is not only later, it is also less certain, because the future is less certain. So impatience and risk aversion are entangled by the structure of the task itself, not by any failure of the experimenters. When an effect gives up that much of its magnitude to a single control, the honest question is not whether it survived, but whether it was ever the effect being named, or whether it was risk aversion wearing a different label from the start.

Third, the physiological measure and the questionnaire measures of impulsivity and sensation seeking diverged. When two instruments that supposedly measure the same construct disagree, at least one of them is measuring something else, and the paper cannot tell you which.

None of this makes the work bad. It is candid and it reports what it found. But it is a useful specimen of a result that reads as a discovery in the abstract and as something considerably more fragile in the tables.
