# The concept of the day comes from the statistics and methods course, and it is the third variable that moves both of your measures.

Item three reports a correlation of nought point three five between how often a small child blinks and how accurately that child switches sorting rules.

In a developing sample, the first question about any correlation is never whether it is real. It is whether the relationship runs between the two measures, or between each of them and the child's age.

This paper gives you both legs of that confound, which deserves credit. Age correlates with switching accuracy at nought point six eight, very strong indeed, and with blink rate at nought point two nine. So age sits inside both correlated variables. A confound is not a story to argue about in prose. It is a quantity, and when both its legs are reported the reader can do the arithmetic.

The partial correlation is that arithmetic. It asks what survives among children of the same age, and the answer falls to nought point two two. So roughly a third of the association was age doing the work at both ends, and the nought point two two is the paper's actual claim: about five per cent of the differences between children in rule switching.

Now the part students skip. Partialling out age removes age as measured, to the precision it was measured with, in whatever shape the model assumed, almost always a straight line. Age in months stands in for everything that develops with age, so residualising on it does not isolate a blink-specific process. It isolates whatever is not linearly predictable from months since birth, which is vaguer. And because each child was measured once, nothing distinguishes a child who blinks more as a stable trait from one further along the same curve. The authors say so, which is why they claim a group-level tendency rather than a marker.

Three questions, then, for any correlation in a developing sample. Is the obvious confound measured at all, and how precisely? Is the partial reported, and how much smaller is it than the raw correlation? And is the control variable a nuisance you want gone, or the very process producing the effect, in which case removing it removes your finding.

# One. A television drama in northern Nigeria moved beliefs about reporting corruption by fifty points, and moved reporting itself not at all.

The Behavioural Insights Team published this yesterday, and it is the best piece of policy behavioural science in some weeks.

Vote-buying at Nigerian elections is widespread: more than half of citizens said they saw it personally in twenty nineteen. Yet formal reports run at fewer than a hundred and forty a year in a country of two hundred and forty million, so the behaviour wanted is almost invisible.

The reasoning was Bandura's. People take up uncommon behaviours by watching relatable models, so rather than telling viewers to report corruption, the team wrote characters who did. Anti-corruption storylines went into a widely watched Nigerian drama broadcast during the twenty twenty-two voter registration period. The trial ran across sixteen northern states with just over six thousand two hundred participants, who were offered mobile airtime for quizzes on either that drama or a family comedy. So exposure was encouraged rather than assigned.

The beliefs moved enormously. The probability that a viewer thought reporting corruption was always or often helpful rose by about fifty-three percentage points, and the belief that neighbours frequently report it by about sixty-eight. That is not a nudge; it is a different view of the world.

The behaviour did not move at all. No increase was detected in calls to the electoral commission's hotline after the February election. The team offer three explanations, all theirs: extended outages on the hotline, a general fall in trust in administrative systems, and viewers using unmonitored channels instead. What the post nowhere gives you is uncertainty: no intervals, no tests, and no word on whether those figures cover everyone or only those who actually watched.

# Two. A simple reappraisal instruction quietened the autistic brain's response to a scratchy texture, without quietening the complaint.

Forty-seven autistic and forty-five typically developing young people between eight and fifteen, matched on age and intelligence, lay in a scanner while an experimenter rubbed their forearm with artificial grass and with scratchy wool. Each twenty-second block carried one of two prompts. Feel, meaning experience the texture naturally. Or reporter, meaning describe it objectively, as a news reporter would, which is reappraisal by emotional distancing. Everyone did both.

Reappraisal reduced activity in touch and sound-processing regions in the autistic group, but did not change what those young people said about how badly they felt. Hold on to that split: a brain measure moved and a self-report did not. The coverage prints no test statistics and no correction for multiple comparisons. But the reason this item is here is what happened before anything was measured, and that is the methods watch.

# Three. How often a preschooler blinks tracks how well they switch rules, until you allow for age.

A hundred and thirteen Japanese children between three and nearly seven, from two preschools in Osaka and Kyoto. Two trained raters counted spontaneous blinks from video across six minutes, rule switching was measured with a card-sorting task scored on the trials where the rule changed, and prefrontal activity with near-infrared light through the forehead.

The concept of the day has given you the arithmetic, so the paper can be brief. Blink rate correlated with switching accuracy at nought point three five, and with right dorsolateral prefrontal activity at nought point two seven. Only that one region of four survived correction, and no dopamine was measured.

What earns the paper its place is the authors' restraint. They say the data are cross-sectional, that this is a group-level tendency too weak to assess any individual child, and that blink rate should not be used to diagnose anyone. One caution: the coverage gives no publication date and the journal's own listing of this year's articles does not carry the paper, so I cannot date it.

# Four. Self-rated masculinity and femininity did more work than binary sex, though nobody quantified the comparison.

Three surveys of adults, each of about a thousand people, fielded in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-two. Participants rated how masculine and how feminine they felt, on what appear to be two separate scales rather than one dimension running between them. No instrument is named and no item count is given, which is a poor start for a paper whose argument is about measurement. The outcomes were self-reported participation, attitudes to gun rights and the death penalty, and the authoritarianism and social dominance scales.

The weak point is the headline, and it is instructive. The comparative claim, that the self-ratings beat biological sex, rests on one term losing significance when another enters the model. That is not a test of whether one predictor outperforms another, and no coefficient, variance explained or measure of fit is printed anywhere. To make the claim properly you would compare models and say by how much.

# Five. Rehearsal during a quiet quarter of an hour does not explain why rewarded material is remembered.

Read on its abstract alone: the article page served an early accepted version with no body text, so I have no sample sizes, no statistics and none of the authors' own limitations. Participants learned material under high or low reward, rested for a quarter of an hour, and sat a surprise memory test a day later. One group was told to rehearse the rewarded items during the rest. Reward improved memory, as expected, but neither the instructed rehearsal nor the time participants said they spent thinking about those items predicted the size of the benefit.

Two cautions. The title says the benefit cannot be explained by thought after learning; the abstract says it cannot fully be explained, and the weaker claim is the supported one. And the abstract reports no Bayes factor, no equivalence test and no power analysis, so a null that is the paper's whole contribution arrives with no evidence of the sensitivity needed to read it.

# Six. Dark traits and poor reasoning each predicted rule-breaking, and neither one ran through the other.

Five hundred and fourteen people, mostly university students, with the country unstated although the authors work in Belgrade. Cross-sectional, no preregistration, and not one instrument named.

Participants completed questionnaires for psychopathy, narcissism, Machiavellianism and sadism; a logic test built to tempt an intuitive wrong answer; estimates of how many they had got right; self-ratings against the average person on appearance, intelligence and moral quality; a scale of black-and-white thinking; and self-reported rule-breaking.

Sadism, psychopathy and Machiavellianism each predicted more rule-breaking. Narcissism did not. More wrong answers on the logic test went with more rule-breaking. Overconfidence about intellect, appearance and test performance went with more rule-breaking, while overconfidence about one's own moral quality went with less, the most interesting line in the paper.

Then the headline. A path analysis tested whether the cognitive measures carry the effect of the traits, found no mediation, and that null is the whole basis for the claim of separate tracks. No interaction was tested, no correlation between the predictor sets is reported, and no coefficient or interval appears anywhere. The authors note that their short scales might have hidden subtle effects, an admission of limited power that works against reading their null as independence.

# The methods watch is about differential exclusion, and what matched groups can and cannot repair.

Item two is a study of forty-seven autistic and forty-five typically developing young people. It began with eighty and fifty-four. Thirty-one of the eighty autistic participants were excluded for moving their heads in the scanner, against nine of fifty-four typically developing. That is thirty-nine per cent against seventeen, and the gap is the most consequential number in the study.

Excluding people for movement looks like neutral housekeeping, and usually it is. Not here, given what the study is about. Lying still for eight minutes while someone rubs scratchy wool on your forearm is itself an instance of what is being measured. So the exclusion rule is correlated with the construct, and plausibly removes the children at the severe end of sensory over-responsiveness. The effect is estimated in whoever could tolerate the procedure.

The groups are reported as matched on age and intelligence. Matching is good practice and it is not the answer to this, because it happens among the survivors. It cannot undo a selection made earlier, and two groups can be made equivalent on every measured covariate while still differing in how they came to be in the sample.

Nor can you reason out which way the bias runs. If the excluded children were the most reactive, the surviving group might show a larger effect, because these are the ones who could do the task, or a smaller one, because there is less response left to reduce. The senior author's remark, that the task was hard for younger children and those with more cognitive difficulties, points the same way without settling it. The honest answer is that the direction is unknown.

And what would settle it is cheap, and almost nobody does it. The study already holds the information, because parents completed a sensory questionnaire on every child recruited. One table comparing the children dropped with those kept, on that measure, would turn a worry into a measurement.

So three habits. Find the number recruited, not only the number analysed. Ask whether the exclusion rule is correlated with whatever is being studied. And ask whether the paper compares those it dropped with those it kept.
