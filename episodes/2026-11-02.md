# The concept of the day comes from the statistics and methods course, and it is what a heritability estimate actually is.

Thirty-one per cent. That is the number in today's lead item, and the first thing to say is what it is not. It is not a statement about a person. It does not mean that thirty-one per cent of anybody's pay comes from their genes.

Heritability is a statement about variance. It is the share of the variation in an outcome, within one population at one time, that can be attributed statistically to genetic variation in that population. Change the population, or the labour market, and the number changes.

So how is such a share estimated? The classical twin design compares two kinds of pair. Identical twins share all their genes, fraternal twins about half. If genes matter, identical pairs should resemble one another more closely, and the size of that gap identifies the genetic component.

Everything then rests on a single assumption with a name. The equal environments assumption: that identical and fraternal pairs share their surroundings to the same degree. Where that fails, whatever extra the identical pairs share gets counted as genes.

Today's study cannot take the first step, because American census records do not say whether twins are identical. So the authors compare same-sex pairs, roughly three in five of them identical, against opposite-sex pairs, all of them fraternal. Then they subtract the same comparison among ordinary brothers and sisters, to strip out the fact that families treat same-sex and opposite-sex children differently.

And here is the part to admire. They do not defend the equal environments assumption in prose. They find a specific violation of it, measure it, and price it. Same-sex twins turned out to be about three times as likely as opposite-sex twins to have worked for the same employer. Shared workplaces mean shared wages, and that sharing follows the genetic contrast exactly, so it inflates the estimate. Dropping those pairs moved the headline figure down five percentage points. A second assumption, random mating, pushes the other way: if it fails, the estimate rises by about a sixth.

Three questions to carry away. What population, and which variance, does a heritability figure describe? Which assumption is it leaning on, and in which direction would a breach push it? And did the authors argue for the assumption, or measure it? Only the second can be checked by a reader.

# One. Genes account for about a third of the variation in American earnings.

The concept of the day has given you the design, so here is the paper. David Card, Jesse Rothstein and Moises Yi, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, linked household records from the two thousand census to state wage filings from twenty eighteen to early twenty twenty-three. The wage analysis covers forty-four and a half thousand twin pairs and more than a million ordinary sibling pairs.

Their first estimate put thirty-six per cent of earnings variance down to genetic factors. After removing pairs who had ever shared an employer it came to thirty-one, their best figure. The heritability of the employer pay premium fell from twenty-one per cent to seven, and stopped being significant.

No confidence intervals appear anywhere in the coverage, and the sample requires both siblings to have held steady full-time work, so the self-employed and the weakly attached are not in it.

# Two. A single dose of endotoxin dampened pleasure and the wish to talk, for about two hours.

This is the only randomised experiment in today's brief. Chloe Boyle and colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, writing in Psychoneuroendocrinology, gave forty healthy women aged twenty-five to forty-four either a low intravenous dose of bacterial endotoxin or saline. Eighteen received the endotoxin and twenty-two the placebo, double-blind, with blood drawn hourly.

Two hours in, the endotoxin group reported more anhedonia, meaning a reduced capacity for pleasure, and less desire to chat with another person. Both effects survived adjustment for fatigue and for feeling ill, and the effect was specific: their rated desire to do a puzzle, watch film clips or sit quietly alone did not move. Within that group, the women whose markers rose most in the first hour showed the largest drop.

The most interesting result is the timing. By four hours the anhedonia had returned to baseline, even though the inflammatory markers were still elevated. Whatever the mechanism, it is not simply tracking the level of inflammation.

The authors note that the relevance to clinical depression needs further work. Forty women in one age band is a small sample, a sharp endotoxin challenge is not the low-grade persistent inflammation seen in depressed patients, and social motivation was a rating of wanting rather than an observed approach to anybody.

# Three. Feeling humanised by the other side lowered disgust, and disgust was the route to compromise.

Melissa Pavetich and Sofia Stathi, in the Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, ran five experiments on party supporters in Britain, the United States and Canada, with about fifteen hundred participants randomly assigned to read invented posts in which the opposing party either humanised or dehumanised them. The four later studies were preregistered.

The manipulation was strong. Asked how humanised they felt on a seven-point scale, American Republicans averaged above five in the humanising condition and one and a half in the dehumanising one. Among British Labour supporters, humanising posts cut rated disgust from about three point two to two point one. The modelling then picked out disgust, rather than anger or empathy, as the route to willingness to compromise.

Here is the catch, and it is the recurring one. The treatment was randomised. The mediator was not. Disgust, humanisation and willingness to compromise were all reported by the same people, on the same questionnaire, at the same moment, which establishes nothing about which came first. No mediation coefficient, interval or p-value appears anywhere. Pavetich's own assessment is that the effects were small to moderate and will not erase partisan hostility.

# Four. People who take part in online shaming score higher on taking pleasure in others' misfortune.

Dosun Kim and Yeon Soo Kim, criminologists at Dongguk University in Seoul, surveyed a representative sample of a thousand South Korean adults for Computers in Human Behavior, sorting them by what they said they had done: non-participants; passive participants, who had looked at content criticising rule-breakers; and active participants, who had shared somebody's personal details, written accusatory comments or made critical posts.

The active group scored higher than both others on schadenfreude, on what the literature calls moral grandstanding for prestige, and on the belief that online shaming achieves justice and deters bad behaviour.

A representative non-Western sample on a question usually studied in Western ones is the main reason this is in the brief. But nothing was manipulated, the grouping variable is self-reported past behaviour, and the outcome is a judgement about whether shaming works rather than any observed act. One subtlety is worth crediting: the overall interaction across their three groups was not significant, and they call a contrast within it exploratory rather than a finding.

# Five. Mental health from the age of fifteen to the early forties tracked how people voted at forty.

Sam Fluit and colleagues at the University of Oslo, in Political Psychology, followed two thousand two hundred Norwegians across five waves over twenty-eight years, from about age fifteen in the early nineteen nineties to twenty twenty. Symptoms were self-reported at every wave, and people were sorted into trajectories: three for internalising problems, meaning low mood and anxiety, two for externalising problems, meaning rule-breaking and aggression.

Elevated symptoms of either kind went with lower turnout and lower trust in government and in other people. The internalising trajectories went with a more left-leaning profile. Persistently elevated externalising problems went with a more right-leaning one, and twice the reported likelihood of voting for populist-right parties.

Now the reasons for caution, and there are several. The coverage gives no odds ratios, no intervals, no p-values and no group sizes. Only directions, and two ratios with no base rates underneath them. The externalising questions had to change as the participants grew up, so the thing measured is not identical across waves. Nothing is said about attrition over twenty-eight years, nor about adjusting for childhood circumstances or parental politics, the obvious common causes of both halves of the association. And politics was measured once, at the end, so despite the word trajectory, no trajectory in political attitudes is observed at all.

# Six. The case for reading for pleasure.

The reason this item is in the brief at all is what kind of document it is, which is the methods watch, so I will be brief. Barbara Sahakian at Cambridge, with two colleagues, argue in Psychological Medicine that reading for pleasure belongs in preventive psychiatry, and that reading and book clubs could be cheap ways to support wellbeing and protect the ageing brain.

The evidence ranges widely: survey data suggesting that reading among British children may be at its lowest recorded level; the authors' own earlier analysis of ten thousand adolescents, in which early readers showed better memory and attention; and several cohorts of older adults showing lower dementia risk.

Angelina Sutin at Florida State University, who was not involved, supplies the line that matters: most of this is observational, healthier people may simply be the ones who read, and the answer is a randomised trial against an active control.

# And so to the methods watch, also from the statistics and methods course: what a narrative review is, and what it is not.

Nothing in that reading paper is wrong. That is why it is worth half a lecture.

Start with what it is. It is an editorial, and its own coverage says so plainly. It is not a systematic review. The difference is not prestige, it is rules. A systematic review commits in advance to a search strategy, to criteria for including and excluding studies, and often to a registered protocol. The set of studies it ends up with therefore follows from rules rather than judgement, and a reader can ask what was left out. A narrative review makes no such commitment. Its studies were selected. And selection is where an argument can be made to come out whichever way the author would prefer, without a single false sentence being written.

Then the exposure. Reading for pleasure is measured in at least five incompatible ways across the studies assembled. Daily frequency. Hours per week. Years of early reading. A rating of enjoyment. And familiarity with authors' names, as a proxy for print exposure. Worse, two of the dementia cohorts do not measure reading at all. They measure a composite scale of stimulating activities that bundles reading with chess and other pursuits. So a thirty-three per cent lower risk attached to a one-point rise on that scale is a statement about the bundle, not about books.

Then the outcome. Brain health is never defined, and it stands in turn for cognitive test scores, imaging measures, dementia incidence and self-reported wellbeing, which carry very different evidential weight. The strongest designs here are the multi-wave cohorts of older adults. The weakest are self-selected charity surveys. And one imaging study of about four hundred and sixty people found no structural difference at all, which the piece does report.

Finally the declared interest, which the paper discloses, and the disclosure is the right way round. One co-author is chief executive of the literary charity with which the follow-up work is planned, and the piece recommends reading and book clubs. A disclosure is not an accusation, and it invalidates nothing. What it does is give a reader reason to look harder at the selection step, which in a narrative review is the step carrying the whole conclusion.
