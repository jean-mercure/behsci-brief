# The concept of the day comes from the statistics and methods course, and it is the predictor that cannot be separated from what it stands for.

Today's lead item reports that reading fluency accounted for forty-nine per cent of the variance in a false-belief task. That is a striking number, and it rewards being taken apart.

Start with what variance explained is. It describes how much of the spread in an outcome a model accounts for, in this sample, across this range of predictor values. It is not an effect size in any portable sense. Narrow the range of the predictor and the figure falls. Widen it and the figure rises, with nothing about the underlying relationship having changed at all. In this study the range was widened deliberately. One group had a few months of schooling. The other had more than seventeen years of education. A predictor that separates those two groups will explain a great deal of variance almost by construction.

Now the substance. Reading fluency in this design is very nearly the same variable as group membership, and very nearly the same variable as years of education. When two predictors carry almost identical information, a regression cannot divide the credit between them. It will tend to hand the credit to whichever is measured with less noise, and the coefficient on the other will collapse towards zero. That is the most likely reading of one of the paper's secondary results, which is that intelligence and inhibitory control added no predictive power at all once reading fluency was in the model. The honest interpretation is not that those faculties are irrelevant to perspective-taking. It is that in this sample they had almost no independent variation left to explain. The authors say so themselves, and treat fluency as a stand-in for literacy and for the schooling that comes with it.

Which leaves the question of what design would pull them apart. You want somewhere that schooling is roughly constant and literacy varies. Adult literacy programmes are one answer, because attainment differs among people of similar age and background. Comparing literate and illiterate siblings in the same household is another. The logic is exactly the logic of the within-family designs this brief has used several times, most recently yesterday. Find a source of variation in the exposure that is not also a source of variation in everything else.

So two questions to put to any study reporting a large share of variance explained. What else moves with the predictor. And does anything in the design separate the two.

# One. Reading fluency predicted second-order false-belief reasoning, and the design cannot separate it from schooling.

Tan Arda Gedik and colleagues, in PLOS One, reported yesterday. Seventy-six Turkish-speaking women, about forty of them with only a few months of schooling, about thirty-five with more than seventeen years of education. The task asked what one person thinks another person believes. Under the stricter scoring the literate group answered about eight in ten belief questions correctly, against about two in ten for the illiterate group. The illiterate group passed a basic comprehension check almost nine times in ten, so this is not a failure to follow the story. The authors' own limitations are the ones that matter: the design is correlational, the sample is small, every participant was a Turkish-speaking woman, and the task tests one narrow form of perspective-taking rather than social understanding at large.

# Two. A happy video of twenty-eight seconds moved attitudes to diversity, and an awe video did not.

Jens Agerström, Magnus Carlsson and Towe Nilsson, in the International Journal of Psychology, reported four days ago. Rather more than three thousand adults, randomly assigned to a brief awe clip, a brief happiness clip, or a neutral control, and then measured on diversity attitudes, on what the authors call diversity fatigue, and on preference for group hierarchy. Happiness moved all three in the expected direction. Awe moved none of them. The effects were very small, and I have made them the methods watch at the end of the episode, so I will not labour them here. Note only the authors' own caution, which is that twenty-eight seconds of video may not produce the awe anybody means by the word.

# Three. Two styles of meditation beat a wait-list, and only one of them touched dysfunctional attitudes.

Céline Stinus and Sophie Berjot, in the journal Mindfulness, reported yesterday. One hundred and forty-seven French adults, randomised into three arms of roughly fifty: focused-attention meditation, a secular self-inquiry practice with the Buddhist teaching stripped out, and a wait-list. Four weeks, five hour-long online group sessions, and twenty minutes of practice on five days a week. Both meditation arms reduced depressive symptoms and what the authors call identity threat, at moderate effect sizes, and they did not reliably differ from one another. Both raised cognitive decentering, which is the capacity to observe a thought as a thought rather than as a fact. Only focused attention shifted dysfunctional attitudes.

The exploratory part is the interesting part and the weakest part. Decentering appeared to carry the focused-attention group's symptom reduction. In the self-inquiry group an increase in connectedness to humanity tracked the fall in identity threat, and nothing the study measured accounted for its reduction in symptoms at all. The limitations are substantial and the authors list them. Wait-list participants knew they were untreated, and every outcome was self-reported. Nobody in the trial had a psychiatric diagnosis and the depression scores were in the non-clinical range, so this is not a trial of treatment for depression. One instructor led both programmes, and that instructor was the first author. Attrition was lopsided: twenty people left the wait-list, against four or five from each meditation arm. And the trial was neither preregistered nor followed up.

# Four. Unhealthy adolescent habits cluster together, and the clusters predict anxiety and depression a year later.

Wanyu Che, Ziyan Ruan and Xiaoyan Wu, at Anhui Medical University, with colleagues, in BMC Psychiatry, reported yesterday. Something over six and a half thousand Chinese adolescents, average age fourteen, across eight regions and both urban and rural areas, surveyed by smartphone in class in late twenty twenty-one and again a year later. The researchers recorded fifteen unhealthy behaviours, covering smoking, alcohol, inactivity, poor sleep, late bedtimes, screen time and diet, and then fitted a latent class model, which sorts people into groups by the pattern of their answers rather than by any score decided in advance.

Four groups came out. About a quarter were low risk. About four in ten were characterised chiefly by poor diet. About a fifth were sedentary and screen-based. And about one in seven showed multiple unhealthy behaviours at once. At one year, roughly one in seven of the whole sample met criteria for anxiety and depression together. The sedentary group and the multiple-behaviour group carried substantially higher risk than the low-risk group, and risk also rose with the simple count of behaviours: seven or more carried close to three times the odds of three or fewer. The model adjusted for family income, number of friends, academic pressure and family history of depression. Everything here is self-reported, the design is observational, and only students still enrolled were sampled, which leaves out those who had already left school and who are plausibly the higher-risk group.

# Five. Sex differences in deep-brain tissue were absent at nine and present after puberty.

Christopher Filippi, Jay Giedd and colleagues, in Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, reported three days ago. Sixteen hundred-odd participants aged nine to thirty-five, drawn from two large open imaging datasets, scanned with a diffusion technique that separates the movement of water inside cells from its movement in the space between them. Between the ages of nine and twelve the differences between males and females were minimal. They emerged across adolescence. From puberty onwards the restricted component, which indexes how tightly cells are packed, was consistently higher in males, chiefly in the hippocampus, the amygdala and the thalamus, while females showed more of the component that indexes extracellular space.

The limitations deserve attention because they bear directly on the headline. The data are cross-sectional, so these are different people at each age rather than the same people followed through puberty. Pubertal stage was rated from physical development, which tracked age so closely that the two could not be separated, and no hormones were measured, which makes any hormonal reading of the result speculative. And two datasets acquired on different scanners were combined, with the statistical correction falling right at the transition into adulthood.

# Six. Flagged rather than described: young children's preference for strivers.

Nina Ye and Kathleen Corriveau, in Communications Psychology, published yesterday, under the title "Young children prefer to learn from and befriend strivers rather than endowed individuals". The title, the authors and the date are confirmed. The article could not be read this morning, so nothing is reported here about the method, the sample or the findings. For context only, an earlier paper from the same research line, in Child Development, ran three preregistered studies with a couple of hundred children aged four to nine in the United States and in China, and found a preference for strivers over naturally able peers appearing around the age of seven, strongest in children who expected strivers to succeed and who treated effort as more controllable than talent. Worth returning to.

# The methods watch, from the statistics and methods course: a large sample, a real difference, and a difference too small to matter.

Item two is the mirror image of a caution this brief raised five days ago. There the question was whether a study had the power to find anything at all. Here the sample is more than three thousand people, and the question runs the other way. With that many participants, a shift of eight hundredths of a point on a six-point scale is comfortably detectable. Detectability tells you nothing about whether it matters.

Three points. The reported shifts amount to somewhere between one and three per cent of the range of the scales on which they were measured. Standardised, they sit at or below the floor of what is conventionally called a small effect. A single twenty-eight-second video moving a general attitude by that much is unsurprising, and read honestly it is close to nothing.

Second, the comparison here is between conditions, so the null result for awe is a result about this manipulation and not about awe. The authors concede the clip may have been too brief. More to the point, the main study carried no manipulation check at all. There was a pretest, on a hundred and twenty-five different people, which found the two clips comparable in emotional intensity. But without a check inside the main study you cannot distinguish awe that failed to shift attitudes from awe that was never induced in the first place. Those are different problems with different remedies, and one of them is a measurement failure rather than an absence of effect.

Third, the hierarchy measure sits against a floor. Baseline preference for hierarchy was low, which compresses the room available for any movement downwards.

So two habits. For any significant result, ask for the effect in the units of the scale, and divide it by the range of that scale. And look for the manipulation check before you read the null.
