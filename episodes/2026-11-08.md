# The concept of the day, from the statistics and methods course. What a contextual exposure identifies.

Item two joins two levels, and the join is the thing to understand. The outcome is a property of a person: this respondent, in this survey, says he intends to vote this way. The exposure is a property of a place: this county lost jobs held by men without a degree and gained jobs held by women without one. That is a respectable design, and a great deal of political behaviour and public health research is built exactly this way. But be clear what it estimates. Not the effect of losing your job. The association between living somewhere this was happening to and voting a particular way. A narrower claim, and a real one.

Nothing here was assigned, and place-level exposures carry a complication of their own. People can leave. A county losing industrial work is also a county people move away from, and the ones who go are not a random sample of the ones who were there. So the exposed population is partly composed by the exposure itself, and controlling for the characteristics you happened to measure does not repair that.

The exposure is also a composite. It is not job loss. It is job loss among men without a degree happening at the same time as rising employment among women without one. Two things that move together in your data cannot be prised apart by that data. The estimate belongs to the pair.

Then the mechanism. The authors read this as status threat, and their best evidence is that men with degrees shifted too, although the layoffs never touched them. Stop and ask what else predicts that. An unmeasured county-level confounder predicts it exactly: whatever else was changing in those places reached graduates and non-graduates alike. So the observation fits the authors' account and fits the obvious alternative, which means it does not choose between them. What would choose is measuring felt threat in the same people whose vote is the outcome. That was not done.

Three questions for any study of this shape. At what level was the exposure measured, and at what level the outcome. Could people enter or leave the exposed condition, and would leaving be related to the outcome. And is the mechanism measured in the same people as the outcome, or inferred because somebody else moved in the same direction.

# The methods watch, also from the statistics and methods course. When the sample is chosen by how much the outcome moved.

Item five, the tau and mood study, analyses three hundred and ninety people. The directional claim, that tau leads and mood follows, rests on a hundred and thirty-one. Ninety-five of the two hundred and twenty-six cognitively normal participants were dropped from that model because their depression scores never changed from visit to visit. In the already impaired group, only twenty-six of a hundred and sixty-four went the same way. Forty-two per cent against sixteen.

As arithmetic the exclusion is defensible. A model of change needs something to change, and a flat series contributes nothing. The difficulty is what the filter is made of. It is made of the outcome variable. So the surviving sample is defined as the people whose mood fluctuates, and the finding is a statement about them, not about older adults. Worse for the comparison the paper actually makes, the filter fell at very different rates on the two groups, so the conclusion that the relationship is there in one and absent in the other is partly a conclusion about two differently constituted samples. The coverage says as much: the findings apply only to people whose mood fluctuates. That is not a caveat but a restatement of what was estimated.

A subtler problem sits in the instrument. The depression measure is the fifteen-item Geriatric Depression Scale, and one of its items asks about memory problems. In a study whose predictor is a marker of Alzheimer's pathology, that item is not a clean measure of mood. It is partly a measure of the thing doing the predicting.

What the authors did next is the part to admire. They removed that item and reported that the association then lost significance and the model would not run stably at all. Publishing a check that dissolves your own headline is the behaviour to copy. They add, unprompted, that self-report gets less reliable as thinking declines, and that dropping the item may have narrowed the range too far to find a small effect, which is a competing explanation for their own null.

Three habits. Ask on what variable a sample was filtered, and whether that variable was the outcome. Ask whether the filter fell equally on the groups compared, because a differential exclusion can manufacture a group difference by itself. And read the items of a scale against the predictor, because a questionnaire can share content with the very thing meant to explain it.

# One. Artificial intelligence raised everyone's drafts, and only the seniors' skill.

A working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research, about a month old and not yet peer reviewed. Eleven American patent law firms, a hundred and thirty-three lawyers, randomised inside each firm: ninety given an unreleased Google writing tool, forty-three left with their ordinary software. Three months. Attorneys at an independent firm scored the drafts without knowing who had the tool.

Those with the tool wrote better drafts, at ten days and again at ninety, by about a third of a standard deviation. The gain came from fewer bad drafts rather than more brilliant ones, and they finished roughly ten minutes quicker. Then the interesting test. At ninety days everybody marked up a machine-written draft with no tool to help. The AI group did better, and the whole of that advantage came from the senior lawyers. The juniors were no better than junior controls.

Three cautions. Seniority was measured, not assigned, so the headline is an interaction between something randomised and something merely observed, and the junior-senior gap in draft quality was itself not significant. An audit flagged fifteen of ninety-one mark-ups for possible tool use, and dropping those makes the overall group difference disappear. And the study was funded by Google, with six of its seven authors working there, which the coverage says plainly.

# Two. Industrial decline, gender, and the political right.

The American Journal of Political Science, undated on the page. Richard Clark, Roza Khoban and Noah Zucker take more than a quarter of a million individual survey answers about congressional vote intention, from two thousand and six to twenty twenty, and attach to each respondent the labour-market record of the county he lives in. Where men without a degree were losing work while women without one were gaining it, the vote moved right. Men shifted whatever their education. Women without a degree shifted too; graduate women did not. A second strand follows a cohort born in the late nineteen fifties and finds that men whose income fell sharply endorsed the breadwinner model more strongly.

Not one coefficient, interval or p-value appears in the coverage, and no identification strategy is named, although the headline uses a causal verb. This is the paper behind the concept of the day.

# Three. Is there a core to conspiracy belief?

The European Journal of Social Psychology, current by its article number. The standing assumption in this literature is that a general conspiracy mentality, a broad suspicion of the powerful, is the hub that generates the particular beliefs and explains why they travel together. Arturo Bertero and colleagues tested that across three studies and nearly thirty thousand respondents in seventeen national samples. The method: put a summary mentality score into a network alongside the specific belief items and see whether it becomes the most connected node. Their rule called the idea supported if the score beat every single item, falsified if it lost to every single item, and inconclusive otherwise.

In eight-country data from twenty twenty-four it was falsified everywhere but France. In a reanalysis of two thousand Americans it leaned the other way but came out inconclusive. In a third survey of nearly eight thousand people it was falsified in six countries of eight, and the mirror idea, that the specific beliefs are the core, was falsified in all eight. The authors do not say mentality measures should be dropped. They note the data are cross-sectional, that the theory was never stated precisely enough to test cleanly, and that none of this was preregistered because the design followed the data.

# Four. Revealing a slide one line at a time.

The Journal of Computer Assisted Learning. Hikaru Ito and Hiroko Ichikawa recorded a twenty-minute biology lesson over seven slides and randomly split forty Japanese undergraduates. Half saw each point, picture and graph appear as the lecturer began explaining it. Half saw the whole slide from the moment it arrived. Same narration, same content.

The gaze difference is enormous. In the cumulative condition students looked at the right part of the slide after about a second. In the whole-slide condition it took nearly six. Dwell time on the relevant region was about a third higher, while total time on the screen did not differ at all.

The learning difference is much smaller. Marks out of twenty were seventeen point seven against sixteen point seven, an effect size of about a half in a study designed to detect eight tenths, and the coverage calls that significant but narrowly, with no p-value given. Four of the forty were dropped from the eye-tracking analysis for poor tracking. And the test was immediate and repeated the pre-test, so it measures short-term retention and nothing about transfer.

# Five. Tau before mood, in the people whose mood moved.

The Journal of Neuroscience, undated on the page. Teodora Markova and colleagues used shared data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative: three hundred and ninety older adults, none clinically depressed at the start, each with at least two tau brain scans over about three years alongside a repeated depression questionnaire.

Among those who were cognitively normal, faster tau build-up went with steeper rises in low mood, and a continuous-time model put tau ahead of mood rather than the other way round. Neither direction reached significance in those already impaired. A separate estimate put first tau positivity at about sixty-eight in the impaired group and seventy-two in the normal one. No effect sizes, intervals or p-values in the coverage, and no correction for multiple testing named. This is the paper behind the methods watch.

# Six. A rhetorical tic, counted.

Federico Boggia, working alone, in a preprint from July that has not been peer reviewed. He measured how often large language models reach for epanorthosis, the classical self-correction of the form not X, but Y. A pattern-matching detector counted instances per ten thousand words in machine output and in human writing across seven genres, from encyclopedia articles to Conan Doyle to historical speeches.

In oratory the models ran at about thirty-four against a human fourteen, though only the two larger models overshot. In informal question and answer writing they used the figure far less than people did. Five of the seven paired genres showed no significant difference, and the author notes that two of the significant ones would not survive a correction for testing seven genres at once.

The detector's own properties are the interesting part, and he reports them honestly. It caught about half the true cases, and when it flagged something it was right forty-five per cent of the time overall, but eighty-two per cent on machine-written passages against seventeen per cent on human ones. An instrument whose error rate depends on which group you point it at can produce a difference all by itself.
