# The concept of the day, the reference group

This belongs to Foundations of Behavioural Science, and the Psychological Science paper at item four is the occasion for it.

Consider the question: all things considered, how satisfied are you with your life these days? It looks like a reading taken off an instrument. It is not. To answer it at all, the respondent has to supply a comparison, and nothing in the question tells them which one. The course meets this under the heading of wellbeing measurement; the normative half of it is the Norms element of the MINDSPACE framework.

Yesterday's paper is a clean illustration, because it holds the objective state fixed and varies only how common that state is locally. Being partnered is worth more reported life satisfaction where most people are partnered than where fewer are.

Two readings are available, and the study cannot separate them. The first is a real effect on welfare: where couples are the majority, the social infrastructure of coupled life is denser, and where they are not, a single person has more available others. The second is an effect on measurement: the local norm shifts the implicit reference point against which someone grades their own life, so two identical lives report differently. The honest position is that comparing average satisfaction across regions is partly comparing norms rather than lives.

Two details discipline the interpretation, and they are the part worth remembering. First, the asymmetry runs the opposite way to the obvious story. If the mechanism were social disapproval of being single, the gap should widen because single people report less. Instead it widens because partnered people report more, with single satisfaction nearly flat across districts. Second, the authors looked for the obvious mediator, how much each group says a relationship matters, and did not find it. Single people in couple-dense districts rated it slightly less important, which is what preference adaptation looks like, and the reverse of an unmet-aspiration account.

The transferable lesson concerns using wellbeing as a policy outcome at all. A subjective scale is answered against a reference point that the policy environment can itself move. An intervention that raises reported satisfaction may have improved lives, or may have lowered the standard people hold them to, and a single-item measure cannot tell you which.

# One. Language models are poor stand-ins for human economic preferences

In Nature Human Behaviour yesterday, Fadong Chen and colleagues, with Sudeep Bhatia at the University of Pennsylvania, ran what amounts to a virtual survey. They took the Global Preference Survey, which measures risk-taking, patience, reciprocity, altruism and trust, and gave every one of about sixty-seven thousand real respondents in seventy-four countries a persona twin specified by age, gender, cognitive ability and country. Twenty model configurations then answered the same questionnaire, mostly prompted in the local language with local currency amounts. That is something like one and a half million personas and twenty-eight million generated answers.

The alignment is positive and very weak. Model answers explain a little over one per cent of the variation between individuals and about four per cent of the variation between countries. The models recover the direction of demographic gradients about four times in five. But on cultural differences they are right only about half the time, which is a coin.

Three further findings are worth more than the headline. The models resemble each other more closely than any of them resembles people, which suggests a shared regularity departing from human data in a common direction. Their errors are larger in poorer countries. And they overstate women's altruism and trust, more so where gender stereotypes are stronger, which is a stereotype being reproduced rather than a preference being modelled.

The caveats are the authors' own. The survey was fielded in two thousand and twelve, so it may sit in the training data, and they call their figures an upper bound, though paraphrased items found no sign of memorisation. The sampling temperature was fixed at zero, which homogenises the output.

# Two. Polygenic indices predict turnout, and sibling comparisons cut the estimate down

In Political Behavior, reported yesterday, Rafael Ahlskog and Sven Oskarsson at Uppsala, with Christopher Dawes and Aaron Weinschenk, tested ten polygenic indices against political participation in more than fifty thousand people drawn from four cohort studies, restricted to European ancestry.

Comparing across families, a standard deviation more of the index for cognitive performance or for self-rated health went with about four percentage points higher turnout in lower-profile elections. Indices for neuroticism and for depression predicted lower turnout. Indices for extraversion and risk tolerance predicted the non-voting forms of participation, contacting a politician or attending a rally.

Two things make this more than a curiosity. Only about a third of the association ran through the trait the index is named after, and that share was almost everything for cognitive performance and almost nothing for physical activity. So an index is not a measurement of its own name. And when the analysis was restricted to full siblings, who share a family environment, associations survived for high-profile elections and shrank for the rest. The cross-family estimates were therefore partly picking up the effect of parents' genes on the child's environment, rather than the child's own genotype.

# Three. Musicians hold a weaker prior that sound and sight share a source

In the Journal of Experimental Psychology, reported yesterday, Matthew O'Donohue at Queensland University of Technology and colleagues ran a preregistered comparison of thirty-five musicians and thirty-five non-musicians. Participants located a flash, a burst of noise, or both, on a horizontal line. On combined trials the two came from the same place only one time in five.

Precision on single-sense trials was identical between the groups. On combined trials, the musicians' judgements of where the sound came from were pulled about one and a half degrees less towards the flash. A Bayesian model of causal inference put the difference not in the senses but in the prior: the musicians were less ready to assume that a sound and a light belong to the same event. Telling people the true one-in-five figure shrank the illusion in both groups.

Be careful with it. The groups were self-selected rather than randomly assigned to training, so musical practice is not shown to cause anything. The effect was small, significant on one of two analyses and just short on the other. And it is unresolved whether the instruction changed what people perceived or only how they chose to respond.

# Four. The satisfaction advantage of being partnered is larger where partnership is common

In Psychological Science, reported yesterday, Tita Gonzalez Avilés at Bamberg and colleagues used a British personality survey of about three hundred and eighty thousand adults across three hundred and seventy-nine districts. This paper carries the concept of the day, so briefly: nationally the partnered scored about half a standard deviation higher on life satisfaction, and the gap was nearly a third wider where partnership was most common than where it was least. The data are self-selected online volunteers from around two thousand and ten, two thirds of them women, and the design is correlational, so people moving between districts cannot be ruled out.

# Five. Replay during rest buys the gist at the cost of the detail

In the Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, reported yesterday, Nicholas Hindy and colleagues scanned twenty-four adults listening to word lists built around a theme word that was never played, each word spoken in either a male or a female synthesised voice, so that meaning and source could be tracked separately. Ninety seconds of arithmetic followed, during which they measured how often the hippocampus reinstated the pattern of each list.

More frequent reinstatement predicted more confident false recognition of the theme word that had never been played, and worse memory for which voice had spoken a given word. Memory for the words actually heard was unaffected. Different subregions carried the two errors, and along the length of the hippocampus the front and middle tracked the theme while the back tracked the source detail.

That is a clean demonstration that consolidation is not simply strengthening. The same process that extracts the theme discards the surface. The design is correlational, the sample is twenty-four, and the rest window catches only the first seconds of the process.

# Six. Ask the power question before the data, not after the null

A working paper from Kelly Jones and Mayra Pineda-Torres makes a point of method that travels well beyond their own example. Public policies very often target a small subgroup, and the datasets used to evaluate them rarely contain enough observations on that subgroup to detect an effect of the size the policy was designed to produce. They run the power calculations explicitly and propose a routine for establishing, before any data are opened, whether a study can detect a meaningful effect at all. There is no new finding here; the contribution is the order of the questions. This carries today's methods watch.

# Seven. Growth in patience with daily annoyances tracked falling anxiety

In Psychology of Religion and Spirituality, reported yesterday, Laura Upenieks at Baylor and colleagues surveyed two hundred and seventy-four students at a Christian university in the American south in their first term and again in their final year.

Religious and spiritual struggles went with anxiety at the outset, and increases in struggles with increases in anxiety. The hypothesis under test, that patience would buffer that relationship, failed outright: the interaction was not significant. What did emerge was oddly specific. Patience with other people and patience with hardship went with lower anxiety at the start, but their growth predicted nothing. Growth in patience with everyday annoyances went with a notable fall in anxiety by the final year. The mundane facet did the work and the grand ones did not, which is more interesting than the headline.

It is one institution, the struggles were measured with a single item, and the models are demanding for two hundred and seventy-four people.

# The methods watch, what was this study capable of detecting

This belongs to the statistics and methods course. The working paper at item six inverts the usual order of questions, and the inversion is the lesson. The standard sequence is to run the analysis, find a coefficient that is not significant, and then reach for an explanation. The better sequence is to ask first whether the design could have detected an effect of the size that would matter.

Three implications. The first is that precision is governed by the number of treated observations in the group the policy actually reaches, not by the headline sample size. A survey of a million households may hold only a few thousand members of that group, and the standard error scales with the smaller number. A very large dataset is not a well-powered study of a small population, and the two are routinely confused.

The second is that an underpowered null and a true null are indistinguishable in a results table. A confidence interval spanning both zero and a policy-relevant effect is uninformative in both directions: it neither supports the policy nor refutes it. This is why the interval, not the significance test, is the thing to report and to read. Set that beside yesterday's methods watch, where the Welsh cohort could at least sign the direction of its confounding and thereby say something. The difficulty here comes earlier: whether the estimate was ever available to the design.

The third is that running the calculation first changes what you do. It tells you whether to collect more data, pool jurisdictions, accept a cruder outcome, or decline the question, all cheaper than publishing an uninterpretable null that others will cite as evidence of no effect.

So the habit to acquire, on meeting any null result: what effect size would this study have found eighty per cent of the time?
