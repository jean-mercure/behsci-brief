# First, the concept of the day, and today it is status quo bias.

This is for Foundations of Behavioural Science, from today's lead item.

Status quo bias is the preference for an existing arrangement over an alternative, over and above any reasoned preference for what the arrangement contains. Samuelson and Zeckhauser named it in nineteen eighty-eight, and it underlies a great deal of the nudge literature. Defaults work because the option already in place gets treated as different in kind from the options that are not.

What is interesting is not that the paper finds the bias, but the trouble taken to measure it.

Here is the difficulty. What people think about a drug and what the law says are tangled together. Attitudes shape the law, the law shapes attitudes, and both respond to whatever the substance actually does. So noticing that Americans approve of legal alcohol and disapprove of banned substances tells you nothing about which way the influence runs. The paper reports a correlation of about minus a half between expert-rated harm and support for legality, not significant. That tells you the law tracks harm poorly, not that there is a bias, because experts and the public may simply disagree about harm.

So the authors build a counterfactual. They collect separate judgements about unbranded drugs whose harms, source, medical usefulness and neurological effect were randomly assigned, and fit a model of how much support a substance attracts when nobody knows what it is. That model then predicts support for an unnamed twin of each real substance. Same harms, no name, no legal status. The gap between the real drug and its twin is the estimate of the bias.

The randomisation is the whole point. Those attributes were assigned rather than observed, so the model building the counterfactual cannot be contaminated by the associations under investigation.

And the results take a shape no plain correlation could produce. Legal substances gained about forty points relative to their twins. Illegal substances lost about thirteen. An asymmetry in both directions, which tells you the bias is not caution about change in general but attachment to whatever is already in place.

Three questions for any study of this kind. Where did the counterfactual come from, and what assumptions does it carry? Is it a bias or an error, and what would tell them apart? And which half of the design does each claim rest on?

# One. The legal status of a drug shapes how harmful people think it is.

The concept of the day has given you the design, so here is the paper.

Bence Hamrak at the European University Institute and Gabor Simonovits at the Central European University, in Nature Human Behaviour. Two thousand American adults on an online panel, and then a second experiment with three thousand more.

The date caveat. The coverage gives no publication date for the journal article, and the paper's article number sits well below things Nature Human Behaviour published in the middle of October. The lead author's own website lists it simply as twenty twenty-six. So this is probably current coverage of a paper some months old. New to this brief, but not news from the past two days.

And the figure that sticks. Support for legal alcohol ran about seventy points above support for a banned substance of comparable expert-rated harm.

# Two. Neuroticism predicts the sleep people report, not the sleep they get.

Derick DeCamp and Elizabeth Pantesco at Villanova University, in Personality and Individual Differences.

A hundred and thirty-four undergraduates, average age nineteen, most of them women, none with a diagnosed sleep disorder. Sleep was measured three ways. A questionnaire asking how many hours they usually slept over the past month. A second figure calculated from their usual bedtime, waking time and time taken to fall asleep. And a wrist monitor worn for five nights.

Each one-point rise in neuroticism went with roughly fifteen to twenty fewer minutes of reported sleep. That held after adjusting for the monitor's own estimate, demographics, body mass, depression and anxiety symptoms, night-to-night regularity and the other personality traits. Nothing at all predicted the monitor's reading.

The honest limit is that the two kinds of measure cover different windows. A month of recall against five nights of recording. The authors do allow that people higher in neuroticism may be lying awake in bed, which a wrist monitor can happily score as sleep. What the paper does not seem to do is model the gap between what people say and what the monitor records, which is the quantity the headline is really about.

# Three. Intelligence bought status, but only through something the partner could see.

Jonathan Egeland and colleagues at the University of Agder and the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, in Evolution and Human Behavior. Preregistered, which is worth saying.

A hundred and seventy-five Norwegian students in eighty-five pairs. Each took a short cognitive test, spent five minutes alone on a resource-allocation puzzle, swapped written answers with their partner, and had one minute to explain their strategy out loud. Then each rated the other on respect, leadership and usefulness to a group.

Better test scores produced better solutions, and better solutions earned more respect. The authors read this as full mediation. Intelligence earns status by supplying something useful to the group.

The arithmetic of that claim is the methods watch, so I will leave the numbers until the end. What matters for now is that nothing in this study was manipulated at all.

# Four. Trusting science over doctrine went with more belief in ghosts, among churchgoers.

Paul Froese at Baylor University with two colleagues, in Social Forces. Survey data from last year, about eighteen hundred American adults, described as nationally representative. The authors built a scale of what they call secular rationalism. Relying on science as the only route to understanding, taking moral decisions from human experience rather than religious teaching, and not much caring what doctrine says. Then three belief scales. Traditional miracles. Magic, covering ghosts, curses, foretelling the future and speaking to the dead. And everyday superstition.

Higher secular rationalism went with weaker belief in miracles and stronger belief in both magic and superstition. But the finding is conditional, and this is where the headline flattens it. The link was strongest among people who believed in God, claimed an affiliation or attended church. Among strict non-believers and those who never attend, it largely vanished. The authors read this as religious institutions policing the boundaries of the supernatural: keep the faith while adopting secular reasoning, and you step outside the doctrine rather than out of enchantment.

Now the problem. There is not one coefficient or confidence interval anywhere in the coverage, and no statistics for the interaction the whole claim rests on. The pattern is interesting. Its strength is unknown.

# Five. Twenty years of a low-fat diet, and no difference in dementia deaths.

Rowan Chlebowski and colleagues, in Menopause, reporting a secondary analysis of the Women's Health Initiative dietary modification trial.

Nearly forty-nine thousand postmenopausal American women, aged fifty to seventy-nine, enrolled in the nineteen nineties, all eating at least a third of their calories as fat to begin with. They were randomised between a nutritionist-guided pattern cutting fat to a fifth of calories with more fruit, vegetables and whole grains, and standard health leaflets with no counselling. The intervention ran about eight and a half years. Mortality follow-up ran about twenty. Just under fourteen hundred women died of dementia-related causes.

The answer is a clean null. Dementia mortality was almost identical in the two arms. There was a hint of lower mortality among women in their fifties or sixties at the start, and nothing among those over seventy.

Three things about what this design can and cannot say. It measures mortality, not incidence, so a diet that delayed dementia without changing the death certificate would not show up here. Death certificates attribute badly when conditions overlap. And that hint in the younger women sits among many subgroups examined, which is the usual reason to distrust one.

# Six. One metaphor, six books, and nothing to compare them with.

Short treatment for this one, because what it teaches is better than what it found. Xuhai Chen and colleagues at the National University of Defense Technology in Nanjing, in Frontiers in Psychology, hand-coded six books by future American officials and counted about two thousand metaphors. Some ninety per cent were about threat and about ten per cent about repair or revival, with war and conflict the commonest category in every book. The reason to be careful is that there is no comparison corpus of any kind, so nothing tells you whether that pattern is unusual for these writers or simply what commercial political non-fiction sounds like. Agreement between the two coders on whether a phrase was even metaphorical was eighty per cent, which once you take out agreement by chance leaves a reliability figure of about point five five. Moderate at best. One book supplied about a third of all the metaphors counted, and the figures were not adjusted for length.

# And now the methods watch, on mediation.

This is for the statistics and methods course, from item three.

A mediation analysis splits an association between two things into a part running through a third and a part that does not. The numbers from the Norwegian study. The total effect of the cognitive test on status was zero point one six. Of that, zero point one three ran through the puzzle score and zero point zero three did not. The indirect path was significant. The total effect, in the coverage's own words, fell just short.

So the authors are reporting full mediation of an effect the study did not establish.

This is not a simple blunder. There is a respectable position, associated with Zhao, Lynch and Chen, that a significant indirect path can stand without a significant total effect, because a total effect is a sum and noisy or opposing components can dilute it. But notice how much narrower the supported claim becomes. It says the data are consistent with a pathway. It does not say intelligence predicts status. And with no confidence intervals anywhere, you cannot see how close that zero point one six came.

Second, and this one is structural. The mediator and the outcome were measured by the same person at the same moment. The partner rated you on three things, one of which was usefulness as a group member, and usefulness to a group is very nearly the definition of the coalitional value the mediator is meant to capture. When a mediator and an outcome share a rater, a method and an occasion, the path between them soaks up whatever common-method variance is going.

Third, a causal reading of a mediation model wants a randomised mediator, and there wasn't one. Neither cognitive ability nor puzzle performance was assigned; both were measured. So the design gives you temporal order, test then puzzle then rating, but not that the puzzle score produced the rating. Anything correlating with doing well on the puzzle and visible in a one-minute spoken explanation, fluency and confidence being the obvious candidates, produces the same set of paths.

Four habits, then. Read the total effect before the indirect one, and ask whether an effect was established at all before accepting an account of how it works. Check whether the mediator and the outcome share a rater, a method or a moment. Ask what was randomised. And look for the interval around every path, because a mediation paper giving you point estimates alone describes the shape of a model, not the strength of the evidence.
