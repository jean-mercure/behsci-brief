# Behavioural Science Daily Brief, voice sample

Start with the method, because one of today's papers is a textbook case of it and the design is the whole of its credibility.

A team of economists wanted to know whether having a daughter changes how a man runs his business. The obvious approach would be to compare firms run by men who have daughters with firms run by men who do not. That comparison is worthless. The kind of man who has a daughter, and the kind of firm he runs, may differ from the outset in a hundred ways you cannot observe, and any difference you find could be those hundred things rather than the daughter.

What rescues the question is a fact about biology. The sex of a first child is very close to randomly assigned by nature. So among men who have a first child, those who get a daughter and those who get a son are, on average, balanced on everything else. The researcher does not have to model the selection, because nature has already broken it.

Now the numbers. A team led by Gail Rosenbaum, with Christopher Chabris among the authors, ran three preregistered randomised field trials covering about thirty-six thousand, forty-two thousand and thirty-nine thousand patients. Vaccination rose by between one and three and a half percentage points, a relative improvement of between three and fifteen per cent. The result worth remembering, though, is a null: arms that named the algorithm did no better and no worse than arms that concealed it.

That bears directly on the standing objection to nudging, that these interventions work only because people do not notice them, and that disclosure would therefore destroy them. Here it did not.
